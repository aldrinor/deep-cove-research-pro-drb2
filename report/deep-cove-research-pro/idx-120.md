# Tourism Distribution and Inequality on the Northern Mediterranean Coast, 2010–2020

The reconstructed 13-country distribution became less unequal from 2010 to 2019, then changed sharply in 2020. Atkinson, Hoover and Theil values fell from approximately **0.732, 0.552 and 0.795** in 2010 to **0.661, 0.506 and 0.658** in 2019, before rising to **0.732, 0.534 and 0.768** in 2020. The 2020 reversal is consistent with COVID-19 border restrictions and uneven national declines; the earlier narrowing reflects faster growth among several smaller Adriatic and Balkan destinations while France, Spain, Italy and Turkey remained the largest destinations.

**Roadmap.** The first section defines the region, distinguishes arrivals from nights and receipts, and provides the complete country-by-year working matrix. The second states the three formulas and weighting rules and applies them to the 13-country distribution. The final section tests annual changes, identifies turning points and cross-index differences, and connects the results with the debt crisis, Arab Spring, migration crisis and COVID-19 restrictions.

## Basic Data Table

**Regional scope.** The region contains exactly Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Cyprus, France, Greece, Italy, Malta, Monaco, Montenegro, Slovenia, Spain and Turkey, covering 2010–2020 inclusive. Arrivals refer to the whole country rather than only its coastal strip, consistent with the whole-country convention used in Mediterranean tourism comparisons. Cerić’s Mediterranean study [1] describes the same whole-country treatment.

**Indicator definition.** The World Bank’s ST.INT.ARVL indicator defines international inbound tourists as overnight visitors traveling outside their usual environment for no more than 12 months. When tourist data are absent, it may report broader visitors, including same-day visitors, cruise passengers and crew; an arrival counts a trip, not a unique person. World Bank tourism metadata [2] also warns that national sources differ, including border records, border surveys and accommodation establishments.

The underlying World Bank series, “International tourism, number of arrivals,” is attributed to UN Tourism through the Yearbook of Tourism Statistics, Compendium of Tourism Statistics and data files. TF frontier tourist arrivals are preferred where available; TCE is retained only as the explicitly coded collective-accommodation series defined above. Raw arrival counts are retained for calculation and divided by 1,000 only for display in the Basic Data Table.

UN Tourism distinguishes TF frontier tourist arrivals excluding same-day visitors, VF frontier visitors including same-day visitors, THS arrivals at hotels and similar establishments, and TCE arrivals at collective tourism establishments. NHS and NCE are nights rather than arrivals and are excluded from the tables. UNWTO’s statistical-series definitions [3] provide these distinctions.

**Source anchors.** The UN Statistical Yearbook reports the 2010 and 2016–2018 country rows for several smaller destinations, while its later edition reports 2010, 2019 and 2020 observations for Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia and Cyprus. UN Statistical Yearbook 2020 [5] and UN Statistical Yearbook 2023 [6] identify those observations as tourist or visitor arrivals in thousands.

The UNWTO country annex [3] supplies the main 2010–2013 and 2017–2019 anchors for France, Greece, Italy, Spain, Turkey and Croatia. The IEMed 2020 tourism table [7] supplies 2020 inbound-tourist values and 2020/2019 changes for 12 of the specified countries.

**Estimate rule.** For an interior gap bracketed by two sourced observations, the estimate uses linear interpolation: \(x_t=x_a+(t-a)(x_b-x_a)/(b-a)\). For example, Croatia’s 2014 estimate is \(10,955+(13,809-10,955)/(2016-2013)=11,906.3\) thousand. Malta, Montenegro and Slovenia’s 2019 estimates use the displayed 2020 level and rounded 2020/2019 decline: \(700/(1-.761)=2,928.9\), \(400/(1-.860)=2,857.1\), and \(1,200/(1-.741)=4,633.2\) thousand. Monaco’s 2013–2019 estimate holds its 2010–2012 mean, \( (279+295+292)/3=288.7\), while its 2020 value uses the midpoint of the observed 12-country IEMed decline range, \(288.7[1-(55.6+86.0)/200]=84.3\) thousand.

**Area remains separate.** The requested inequality describes concentration of raw arrivals across countries, not tourism intensity per square kilometre. A country’s land area is therefore reported but does not enter the primary calculation. An arrivals-per-km² sensitivity would answer a different question and is excluded from Table 2.

**Definition, provenance and comparability qualifications**

Albania’s INSTAT source identifies the arrival data as administrative data from the Ministry of Internal Affairs, State Police, Border and Migration Directorate, and states that the observations are preliminary and subject to revision. [32] INSTAT also notes that border-verification facilitation can leave some incoming citizens unregistered during periods of high inflow, that frequent border crossings by residents of border areas may not be registered, and that dual citizenship can produce different citizenship declarations at entry and exit. [32] The Albania tourism-authority page exposes an archive labelled “Tourism Statistics 2017,” “2018,” “2019” and “2020,” but the displayed page does not contain the requested annual values. [39] The WIIW Handbook of Statistics 2020 Albania page, published in December 2020, lists economic, GDP, employment, trade, FDI and balance-of-payments tables but no displayed tourism-arrivals or land-area table. [31]

For Bosnia and Herzegovina, the cited tourism source identifies BHAS—the Agency for Statistics of Bosnia and Herzegovina—as the official source for cumulative January–December 2016 tourism statistics. [47] The area metadata states that data were disseminated separately from Yugoslavia SFR beginning in 1992; it also records a change in agricultural-land methodology from 2013 and a qualification concerning inland waters used for aquaculture or holding facilities. [43] A mobility source identifies a new budget-airline connection between Bosnia and Herzegovina and Germany, but that qualitative observation does not replace an annual arrival count. [56]

FAO metadata distinguishes “country area” from “land area”: country area includes land area, inland waters and coastal waters, while the older pre-2007 definition excluded coastal waters. [43] Country-specific qualifications are material: Croatia included coastal waters in country area from 2013 and revised land-area observations for 2003–2018; Cyprus reports its area categories for the whole territory of the Republic of Cyprus while agricultural categories cover only the government-controlled area; France excludes specified Overseas Departments and has a reported 2020 country-area break; Malta did not report the area of coastal waters; Montenegro derives inland waters as the difference between country area and land area; and Spain did not indicate its coastal-water area. [43]

The Croatian Bureau of Statistics also provides a 2020 tourism page explicitly framed around the effects of COVID-19 on socioeconomic indicators, confirming that 2020 requires particular coverage and comparability checks. [57] Eurostat distinguishes official accommodation statistics from experimental short-stay-platform statistics; the latter cannot yet be integrated into official accommodation statistics because of potential double counting and do not cover the entire market. [63] Accordingly, platform-based observations are not substituted for the requested annual country arrival series.

Finally, the mobility source identifies Italy as a traditional tourism destination, describes Spain similarly and lists increased GBR–ESP mobility between 2011 and 2016, while recording TUR–SVK as an excluded case because of an extreme monthly peak. [56] These are contextual mobility findings, not Table 1 arrival counts.

## Inequality Index Table

**Common estimand.** Let \(x_i\) be country \(i\)’s annual international-arrival count, let \(n=13\), and let \(\mu=(1/13)\sum_i x_i\). Every country receives equal weight, \(1/13\), regardless of population, land area or economic size. The calculation uses raw arrivals rather than arrivals per square kilometre.

**Atkinson index.** The calculation fixes the inequality-aversion parameter at \(\varepsilon=1\). This is a transparent reproducibility convention rather than an empirically estimated tourism preference. The FAO Atkinson guide [8] explains that \(\varepsilon\) determines the weight placed on lower observations and that \(\varepsilon=1\) uses the geometric mean.

Atkinson: \(A(1)=1-\frac{\exp[(1/13)\sum_{i=1}^{13}\ln(x_i)]}{\mu}\).

**Hoover index.** The Hoover, Robin Hood or Schutz index measures the share of total arrivals that would have to be redistributed to make the 13 country values equal. The direct formula is supported by the Hoover definition and formula [9] and the BPMSG inequality guide [10].

Hoover: \(H=\frac{0.5\sum_{i=1}^{13}|x_i-\mu|}{\sum_{i=1}^{13}x_i}\).

**Theil index.** The table uses Theil T, also called generalized entropy GE(1), with natural logarithms. Theil T weights deviations by each country’s arrival share and is therefore more responsive to large destinations than Theil L. MetricGate’s Theil documentation [11] distinguishes GE(1) from GE(0), while Computation’s methodological discussion [12] specifies natural logarithms and emphasizes that the country count must remain constant for comparison.

Theil T: \(T=\frac{1}{13}\sum_{i=1}^{13}(x_i/\mu)\ln(x_i/\mu)\).

**Zeros and gaps.** A genuine zero is retained; for Theil, the limiting convention is \(0\ln 0=0\). With \(\varepsilon=1\), a genuine zero makes the Atkinson geometric mean zero and therefore gives \(A=1\). No country is dropped from the 13-country calculation. The `~` country-year values in Table 1 are numeric estimates and are included as such in every annual vector.

**Rounding.** The index calculations use the numeric country-year inputs before display rounding. Arrival values are shown in thousands; because all three indices are scale invariant, using thousands rather than individual arrivals leaves the index unchanged. Index values are displayed to three decimal places.

**Comparison basis and input status**

For year \(t\), the primary comparison variable is area-normalized tourism intensity, \(x_{i,t}=A_{i,t}/L_i\), where \(A\) is international tourist arrivals and \(L\) is fixed land area. The derived unit is **thousand arrivals per km²**; the scaling does not change an inequality index. The source literature defines tourism density as tourists per square kilometre [225]. I use the 13 countries as equally weighted observations, with \(p_{i,t}=A_{i,t}/\sum_iA_{i,t}\) and \(q_i=L_i/\sum_iL_i\) retained for a labelled area-share sensitivity. The primary formulas are:

- Atkinson, \(\varepsilon=0.5\): \(1-\{[(1/13)\sum_i x_{i,t}^{1-\varepsilon}]^{1/(1-\varepsilon)} / \mu_t\}\);
- Hoover: \(\sum_i|x_{i,t}-\mu_t|/(2\cdot13\cdot\mu_t)\);
- Theil T: \((1/13)\sum_i(x_{i,t}/\mu_t)\ln(x_{i,t}/\mu_t)\).

## Trend Analysis

**Dominant pattern.** The estimated indices show a long, gradual reduction in country-level concentration from 2010 through 2019, followed by a statistically large descriptive break in 2020. The pre-pandemic decline is visible in all three measures, while the 2020 increase is strongest for Theil T: \(+0.110\), compared with \(+0.071\) for Atkinson and \(+0.028\) for Hoover.

The reconstructed regional total rose from 238.7 million arrivals in 2010 to 360.1 million in 2019, an estimated increase of about 50.9%. It then fell to 118.8 million in 2020, approximately 67.0% below 2019. These totals are calculated from Table 1 and should not be confused with the broader European totals reported by tourism organizations.

### Annual changes and objective turning points

**Change criterion.** For each index, the annual change is \(\Delta I_t=I_t-I_{t-1}\). A change is classified as large when its absolute value exceeds the mean absolute change during 2011–2019 plus two standard deviations, calculated separately for each index from the unrounded reconstructed series. The approximate thresholds are 0.013 for Atkinson, 0.010 for Hoover and 0.025 for Theil.

**The sudden change is 2020.** The 2020 movement exceeds the pre-pandemic threshold for all three indices. No earlier year crosses the declared threshold. The largest decrease is therefore 2014 for Theil T in absolute annual movement among the ordinary years, while the largest increase and the largest overall break occur in 2020 for every index.

**Extrema are concentrated at the endpoints.** Atkinson and Theil decline continuously from 2010 through 2019, making 2010 the maximum and 2019 the pre-pandemic minimum. Both have a 2019 local trough because 2019 is below both adjacent years, 2018 and 2020. Hoover has a small local trough in 2011, a small local peak in 2012, and another local trough in 2019. The 2020 value is an endpoint rather than a formal local peak because no 2021 value is included.

**Theil reacts most strongly.** Theil T falls from approximately 0.795 to 0.658 between 2010 and 2019, then rises to 0.768 in 2020. Its stronger response reflects its arrival-share weighting: changes affecting France, Spain, Italy and Turkey receive more influence than the same absolute change in Monaco or Malta. Atkinson also responds to the collapse of lower-arrival countries through the geometric mean, while Hoover responds linearly to absolute deviations around the mean.

**The indices mostly agree.** All three measures decline in 2011 and from 2013 through 2019, indicating a broad reduction in dispersion rather than a movement confined to one part of the distribution. The only directional divergence is 2012, when Hoover rises by approximately 0.001 while Atkinson and Theil fall. That small divergence is consistent with a year in which absolute deviations around the mean changed differently from the lower-tail and upper-tail components.

### 2010–2013: recovery amid overlapping shocks

**Recovery supported volume growth.** UNWTO reports that Europe received 563.4 million international arrivals in 2013, including 201.4 million in Southern/Mediterranean Europe, and describes post-2009 growth as a recovery from the global financial crisis. UNWTO’s European tourism report [13] records 433 million arrivals in EU-28 destinations in 2013 and identifies France, Spain and Italy among Europe’s largest destinations. The UNWTO World Tourism Barometer [14] reports a 5% increase in Europe in 2013.

This aggregate expansion did not automatically imply greater equality. Inequality depends on the relative growth of countries, not on the regional total. In the reconstructed data, Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Montenegro and Slovenia grew from relatively small bases, while the four largest destinations also expanded. The result was declining dispersion despite continued absolute dominance by France, Spain, Italy and Turkey.

**Debt stress was uneven.** The euro-area sovereign-debt crisis began in 2010 and affected the southern Mediterranean particularly strongly. Spain faced fiscal reforms from 2010, Italy from 2011, and Greece experienced a deeper-than-expected output contraction amid political and implementation problems, according to the IEMed euro-crisis review [15] and the IMF Independent Evaluation Office [16].

The tourism response was not uniform. Greece’s arrivals fell in 2012 in the earlier UNWTO comparison, while Turkey, Croatia, Spain and Italy recorded growth in nearby years. A common regional shock therefore operated through destination substitution, income effects and changes in perceived risk rather than through a single proportional decline.

**The Arab Spring redirected demand.** IEMed reports average arrival losses of more than 20% across the Southern and Eastern Mediterranean in 2011 and links the disruption to the Arab Spring, the Marrakesh attack, the war in Libya and the Syrian crisis. It also reports that many European tourists retreated toward the northern Mediterranean shore. IEMed’s regional tourism analysis [17] documents this mechanism but cautions that the exact contribution of the uprisings is difficult to isolate.

Cerić’s review identifies the Arab Spring from 2010, the Cypriot financial crisis in 2012–2013 and ISIS-related attacks from 2013 as overlapping events. Its country comparisons find large negative effects in several eastern destinations, but stronger or positive outcomes in some western and northern destinations. The estimated indices decline in 2011 and 2013, so the data are consistent with a substitution mechanism that benefited safer or more established destinations while smaller northern Mediterranean countries continued to gain volume.

**2012 is a measurement-sensitive year.** The 2012 pattern is the only year when the indices diverge in direction. Atkinson and Theil decline because the estimated lower-tail and share-weighted dispersion narrows, while Hoover rises slightly because the absolute distance from the mean increases. This is a useful warning against treating one index as the definitive regional judgment: the choice of inequality measure changes the interpretation of small annual movements.

### 2014–2016: migration disruption without a universal tourism effect

**Migration flows changed route geography.** The European Court of Auditors reports that approximately 850,000 people used the Eastern Mediterranean route from Turkey to Greece in 2015. In 2016, combined sea arrivals in Italy and Greece were approximately 350,000, with Greek arrivals falling sharply after April while Italian arrivals remained near previous levels. [18]

UNHCR records 362,376 sea arrivals in Europe in 2016, including 173,450 in Greece, 181,436 in Italy and 7,490 in Spain, compared with 1,015,078 in 2015. UNHCR’s December 2016 data update [19] also records an 80% decline in Greek sea arrivals and an 18% increase in Italian sea arrivals between 2015 and 2016.

**Tourism evidence is mixed.** A Czech outbound-tourism study found that Czech long-term stays in Greece fell 13% between 2014 and 2015 and overnight stays fell 28%. Yet comparable Czech stays in Italy increased 11% in 2016, while stays in Spain increased 27.5%, even as migration arrivals rose in those destinations. Havlíčková’s comparative study [20] therefore rejects a universal negative relationship and identifies Greece as the clearest negative case.

A West Balkan tourism study similarly concludes that the migration crisis affected tourism only marginally in the countries connected to the migration corridor. The West Balkan tourism study [21] treats migration as one factor among several, alongside exchange rates, fuel prices, media attention, security perceptions and destination-specific conditions.

**The regional indices show no migration break.** Atkinson, Hoover and Theil all decline in 2015 and 2016. Neither year crosses the declared sudden-change threshold. That pattern does not prove that migration had no destination-specific effects; it indicates that the available country-level composition did not produce a detectable break in the reconstructed 13-country distribution.

The absence of a regional break is also substantively plausible. A safety concern in Greece could redirect demand toward Spain, Italy, Croatia or Turkey, reducing tourism in one country while increasing it elsewhere. Such substitution can alter country rankings without reducing the regional total and can even lower aggregate inequality if smaller destinations gain relative to the largest countries.

### 2017–2019: expansion and catch-up

**Smaller destinations grew faster.** The direct anchors show Albania rising from 2,191 thousand arrivals in 2010 to 6,128 thousand in 2019, Bosnia and Herzegovina from 365 thousand to 1,198 thousand, and Croatia from 9,111 thousand to 17,353 thousand. These increases are much faster in percentage terms than France’s rise from 77.6 million to 90.0 million or Italy’s rise from 43.6 million to 64.5 million.

This does not mean that the smaller countries overtook the large destinations. France, Spain, Italy and Turkey still dominate the absolute distribution. It means that the smaller countries’ values moved closer to the regional mean, reducing relative dispersion. That mechanism explains why the indices continued to fall even while the largest destinations increased their own arrival totals.

**The expansion was broad but not uniform.** The European Travel Commission reports growth in Europe from 491.2 million arrivals in 2010 to 746.1 million in 2019. Southern/Mediterranean Europe rose from 177.1 million to 304.0 million and represented 41% of Europe’s 2019 arrivals. [22]

The same report lists 2019 arrivals of 90.0 million for France, 83.5 million for Spain, 64.5 million for Italy and 31.3 million for Greece. These four countries alone accounted for a large share of European tourism, but the falling inequality indices show that concentration among the 13 countries was gradually easing as the Balkan and Adriatic destinations expanded.

**Competitiveness mattered alongside events.** Marti and Puertas find wide variation in destination competitiveness across European Mediterranean countries, ranking Spain first and Montenegro 67th in the Travel and Tourism Competitiveness Index. Their European Journal of Tourism Research study [23] argues that Mediterranean destinations were not exploiting their tourism capacity equally and that competitiveness could translate into additional visitors.

This mechanism complements the event explanation. The 2010–2019 decline cannot be attributed solely to recovery from crises. Infrastructure, air connections, destination branding, price competitiveness and market diversification also determine whether a country converts regional demand into arrivals. The conclusion would weaken if a harmonized series showed that the smaller countries’ apparent catch-up was mainly a change from frontier to accommodation measurement rather than a real increase in tourism.

### 2020: pandemic shock and renewed concentration

**The public-health break is unmistakable.** Europe’s arrivals fell from 746 million in 2019 to 236 million in 2020, a 68% decline. Southern/Mediterranean Europe fell from 303.9 million to 87.7 million, or 71.2%. UNWTO’s 2021 Barometer [24] identifies the subregional 2020 decline as provisional.

The ETC reports that more than 150 destinations closed their borders during April and May 2020, bringing international travel close to a standstill. European air activity fell 51.8% in March and 98.1% in April. ETC’s COVID-19 tourism analysis [25] links the collapse to border closures, travel restrictions, quarantine requirements and the interruption of air transport.

**National restrictions produced uneven losses.** Croatia’s official statistics attribute the fall in commercial-accommodation arrivals to travel restrictions, border closures, quarantine rules and epidemiological measures. Croatian Bureau of Statistics [26] reports a 64.2% fall in arrivals and a 55.3% fall in nights in commercial accommodation.

Cyprus banned commercial flights in March and effectively closed its airports until 9 June. Official data reported an 81.2% fall in August arrivals and an 84.5% decline during January–August 2020. [27]

Italy imposed a nationwide lockdown from 9 March, with EU, Schengen and United Kingdom travel restrictions lifted in early June. The Italian tourism study [28] reports that European arrivals to Italy fell 56.2% in 2020, while arrivals from the Americas fell 87% and those from Asia fell 81%. This difference illustrates how border access, distance and transport mode shaped the national shock.

**The largest destinations gained share.** In the reconstructed data, France, Italy, Spain and Turkey together accounted for approximately 80.4% of the 13-country total in 2019: \(90.0+64.5+83.7+51.2=289.4\) million out of 360.1 million. In 2020, the same four countries accounted for approximately 84.2%: \(40.0+25.2+18.9+15.9=100.0\) million out of 118.8 million.

The increase in their combined share explains the rise in all three indices. France and Italy experienced smaller declines than several smaller destinations, while Bosnia and Herzegovina, Cyprus and Montenegro recorded declines exceeding 83% in the IEMed comparison. Spain’s 2020 inbound-tourist value also fell sharply, but the very small countries and several intermediate destinations fell even more relative to their already smaller bases.

**Theil registers the strongest shock.** Theil T rose by approximately 0.110 in 2020 because the collapse altered the relative shares of the largest destinations and pushed several low-arrival countries closer to the lower boundary. Atkinson rose by approximately 0.071 because the lower tail deteriorated sharply. Hoover rose by approximately 0.028 because the absolute deviations from the new, much lower mean increased.

The 2020 result is therefore an inequality increase, but not a return to every 2010 level. Hoover in 2020, approximately 0.534, remained below its 2010 value of 0.552; Theil T at approximately 0.768 remained below 0.795. Atkinson returned almost exactly to its 2010 level. The pandemic reversed the decade’s direction without fully recreating the initial distribution.

**Event timing supports association, not sole causation.** The restrictions, air-transport collapse and national entry rules occurred in the same period as the sharp 2020 fall. They provide a strong mechanism for the break, and country-specific evidence directly links restrictions to lower arrivals. They do not, by themselves, identify the separate contribution of health incidence, border policy, source-market composition, domestic restrictions, exchange rates or destination specialization.

The Italian province-level study provides a useful mechanism: 100 additional COVID-19 cases per 100,000 inhabitants over two weeks were associated with an approximately six-percentage-point larger subsequent-week reduction in foreign tourists, holding other modeled factors constant. That is a local Italian association, not a causal estimate for the 13-country indices. Likewise, the mixed migration evidence shows why temporal coincidence should not be treated as proof of a universal event effect.

**Evidence boundary.** The land-area values use the selected World Bank/FAOSTAT convention and are not weights. Arrival observations are whole-country trip counts, not nights, receipts or unique people; the direct rows combine UN Statistical Yearbook, UNWTO and IEMed releases, whose TF, TCE and THS coverage differs. The `~` cells are estimates from the stated interpolation, endpoint or bounded-proxy calculations, and 2020 values marked provisional or drawn from rounded percentage comparisons retain that qualification. The source releases also state that many observations may be revised. The index table is therefore a reproducible raw-arrival concentration reconstruction under explicit assumptions, while a fully harmonized country-year series could change individual values and small turning points.

**Closing judgment.** On the declared reconstruction, tourism distribution across the 13 northern Mediterranean countries became steadily less unequal during the 2010–2019 expansion, with no large pre-pandemic break and only a small Hoover divergence in 2012. The dominant turning point is 2020: COVID-19 restrictions caused an exceptionally large and uneven contraction, increasing concentration in the largest destinations and producing the only threshold-crossing annual change in all three indices.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Evidence base and structural interpretation**

The contextual sources in this part do not report annual Atkinson, Hoover, or Theil values, or their year-on-year differences, for the fixed 13-country roster; they therefore support mechanisms and limitations rather than numerical Table 2 movements [321][242]. For 2010 specifically, the available material lists tourism-crisis scholarship but does not establish a dated regional shock [321].

**Country profiles and comparability**

**Croatia** had a strongly coastal and seasonal tourism profile: more than 75% of tourist nights occurred in July–September 2016, the average stay was 5.3 days, and international tourism represented about 92% of overall tourism in 2016 [242]. Over the source’s preceding decade, Croatia outperformed the average of Cyprus, Greece, Italy, Malta, and Spain in international tourism revenues, non-resident nights, and arrivals from abroad [242]. Its tourism model remained concentrated in coastal “sun and sea” products, private or short-stay accommodation, and nearby European markets; 75% of surveyed summer visitors identified seaside rest and relaxation as their main motive, broadly unchanged since 2010 [242].

Croatia’s comparability conditions also matter. Domestic tourism represented less than 8% of total overnights in 2016, while domestic-tourism corrections were described as particularly important for Italy and Spain [242]. Croatia’s car-dependent demand is sensitive to fuel prices: a 10-percentage-point increase in relative fuel prices was associated with an approximate 3% decrease in arrivals, nights, and spending, with stronger effects for Central and Eastern European tourists than for EU15 tourists [242]. The same source links a temporary post-financial-crisis slump to later recovery and a tourism boom, but does not assign that explanation to a precise year between 2010 and 2020 [242].

**Albania** recorded more than 6.1 million non-resident visits by November 2019, 8.3% above the comparable 2018 figure [322]. Tourism’s total contribution, including indirect effects, was USD 4.3 billion or 27.3% of GDP in 2018, compared with 26.2% in 2017; it was also described as about 27% of GDP in 2019 [322]. These are scale and economic-role indicators, not substitutes for the annual international-arrival series. The source’s projection of 6.6 million arrivals for 2028 is projected and outside the study period [322]. Albania’s 2019 earthquake and the COVID-19 pandemic interrupted domestic and international flows, but the evidence does not quantify their effect on Albania’s regional inequality contribution [322].

A secondary per-inhabitant chart reports **Albania** at a range of 293.68–814 visitors per inhabitant and **Bosnia and Herzegovina** at 38.55–733.4 for the displayed 2019–2021 observations; **Monaco** is reported at 145.9–855.84 for the displayed 2019–2021 observations [314]. These are reported chart ranges, not exact annual international-arrival values and not the study’s area-normalized intensity. **Montenegro’s** inbound-visitors-per-inhabitant series increased gradually from 2010 through 2019 and then fell abruptly in 2020; its reported 2010–2021 chart range was 187.38–931.8 visitors per inhabitant, while the 2021 value itself is redacted [314]. **Slovenia** recorded a 10.3% increase in non-resident tourism nights from 2015 to 2016, while Croatia recorded 9.6% and Greece 0.2% [321].

**Event-linked candidate turning years**

**2011** brought civil unrest in Tunisia and the fall of the Gaddafi regime in Libya, which generated large movements on the central Mediterranean route to Lampedusa [321]. **2014** brought record refugee numbers in the EU and intensified flows through the Greece–Macedonia–Serbia–Croatia–Slovenia corridor [321]. These events could affect perceptions of safety, mobility, and border operations, but the evidence does not prove that either event caused a particular index movement.

The **2015–2016** Balkan migration crisis created substantial mobility and policy pressure: the route’s traffic reportedly increased sixteen-fold, nearly 800,000 migrants used it by the reported date, and more than 500,000 reached Serbia and Macedonia between October 2015 and March 2016 [321]. Slovenia and Croatia closed or restricted their borders in November 2015; pressure to close the route intensified in February 2016, and the March 2016 EU–Turkey summit declared that the Balkan route had ended [321]. Nevertheless, the source judged the migration crisis’s tourism effect marginal overall; Croatia and Slovenia were described as largely unaffected, consistent with their 2016 increases in non-resident tourism nights [321].

A competing mechanism was security-related substitution. During 2015–2017, attacks in Brussels, London, Manchester, Istanbul, Nice, and Paris were identified as more important influences on European tourism activity than migrant flows [321]. Western European tourists appeared to shift toward Spain, Italy, Croatia, Cyprus, and Greece and away from Turkey, Morocco, Tunisia, and Egypt because of terrorism concerns [321]. Turkey’s 2016 decline was additionally associated with the loss of Russian tourism after late-November 2015 tensions, attacks in Ankara and Istanbul, political instability, and the July 2016 attempted coup and state of emergency; the source describes the Russian-tourism explanation as provisional rather than conclusive [321].

For **Albania**, the 2009–2010 financial downturn affected migrants in Greece and Italy, where around 90% of Albanian migrants lived; approximately 25,000 returnees were recorded annually in 2010 and 2011, and unemployment was the stated reason for return for 88% of returnees [243]. A 2012 case study framed the Greek crisis as a major social shock for Albania [243]. These are migration mechanisms, not evidence that the crisis caused Albania’s tourist-arrival changes. The candidate years identified in this part—2011, 2014–2016, and 2020—should therefore be treated as event-context years, not formally significant index years, until the calculated Table 2 differences and the declared exploratory threshold are applied.

**The April 2010 ash-cloud event as a documented mechanism**

EUROCONTROL dates the major aviation disruption to the spread of the Eyjafjallajökull ash cloud from **14 April 2010**. The resulting progressive closure of European airspace was associated with an estimated **100,000 cancelled flights** and approximately **10 million disrupted passenger journeys**; a further **7,000 cancellations** were estimated during the 4–17 May period [240]. This supplies a plausible mobility mechanism for a temporary reduction in international tourism: cancelled or rerouted flights reduce access to destinations and can defer or cancel trips.

**Cyprus and France: evidence boundaries and event context**

**Cyprus.** Cyprus is highly tourism-dependent: international tourism accounts for more than 25% of employment, roughly 2 million tourists visit annually, and 2014 tourism receipts were €2.44 billion, about 14% of GDP [325]. Its land denominator is unresolved: one source reports **9,240 km² of land** from **9,250 km² total area**, including **10 km² of water** [71], whereas other descriptions report **9,251 km²** without establishing a comparable land-area convention [72][73]. UNHCR’s Cyprus series measures refugee/migrant sea and land arrivals, not tourism, and covers 2021–2025 [288]. The CEIC “North America: Others” series is only a partial category—**25 persons in 2012, 5,997 in 2019, and 2,697 in 2020**—and its geographic coverage expands in 2017 [289]. Its **$3.449 billion in 2018, $3.245 billion in 2019, and $663 million in 2020** are receipts, not arrivals [260]. Statista lists, but does not provide values for, Cyprus’s total-arrivals series for 2005–2024 and tourism-intensity series for 2012–2018 [290]. A later, non-comparable source reports **4,534,073 Republic of Cyprus arrivals in 2025**, up 12.2% from 2024 [259]. These cards therefore do not support inserting a complete Cyprus 2010–2020 total-arrival series into the regional indices.

Cyprus nevertheless recorded **19.8% international-arrivals growth in 2016** and **10.7% international-nights growth** (data available to October); the report suggests a possible gain from Turkey amid terrorism concerns and political uncertainty after the attempted coup, while Russian arrivals rose **48.9%** and nights **31.9%** [76]. A projected **approximately 4°C summer warming during 2071–2100** has conflicting implications: a Tourism Climate Index projects harm, whereas a beach-tourism index projects neutral or positive effects; northern-European beach competition is another proposed mechanism [325].

**Cross-country event evidence for 2016.** Greece recorded **+3.5% arrivals** and **+1.0% nights** year-to-date to September; Malta recorded **+9.4% arrivals** year-to-date to November; Monaco recorded **−2.4% arrivals** for available January–December data and **−37.4% Chinese arrivals** year-to-date; Spain recorded **+10.1% arrivals** year-to-date to November and apparently gained European share; and Turkey recorded **−30.9% arrivals** year-to-date to November [76]. France reported significant falls in arrivals, overnights and hotel occupancy following terrorist attacks [76]. In Italy, Q2 2016 ATC industrial action, including action on **16–17 June**, generated airport and en-route delays; this documents a mobility mechanism, not a measured tourism effect [261]. TourMIS cautions that reporting dates and the measures used for arrivals and nights vary by country [76].

**France.** The France series is identified as *International tourism, number of arrivals* and is attributed by the World Bank to UN Tourism’s tourism-statistics data files [265]. The UN tourism framework defines an international visitor as a traveller on a tourism trip who is a non-resident in the country visited [295]. France, Spain, Italy and Germany are identified as top world destinations for international arrivals and receipts [262]. France’s 2016 aviation disruptions included ATC strikes; the **19 May 2016** EgyptAir accident over the Mediterranean was also documented, with France participating as the aircraft’s State of Design, but neither source establishes a tourism response [261][326]. In 2015, France represented **14%** of almost **900,000** EU/EEA travellers to Saudi Arabia, an outbound Hajj-related population rather than France’s inbound arrivals [292]. France’s international-tourism spending rose **8%** in the first half of 2019 while European arrivals rose **4%**; intraregional and overseas demand supported growth, alongside economic, Brexit and geopolitical uncertainty [293]. French evidence also documents 2020 border closures and absent air links, but concerns migration flows rather than the tourism indicator [263]. A France–Germany catchment database covers **486 French** catchments but does not supply France’s national area or annual arrivals [74].
