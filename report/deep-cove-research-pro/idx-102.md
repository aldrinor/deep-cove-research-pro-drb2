# Health Impacts of Video Games, Esports, and Online Gaming: A Literature Review Through Early 2021

**Gaming’s effects depend on context.** By early 2021, active video games showed benefits for energy expenditure, some fitness measures, and modest improvements in body composition compared with sedentary activities. Conventional gaming and esports presented a different combination: possible perceptual and cognitive advantages alongside prolonged sitting, competitive stress, and overuse complaints. Ordinary or cooperative gaming could support enjoyment and social connection, whereas Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) was associated with depression, anxiety, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) symptoms, sleep problems, and impaired social or academic functioning. These findings support neither a uniformly beneficial nor a uniformly harmful judgment about gaming. [1][2][3]

**The report follows three questions.** First, which bodily outcomes improve or deteriorate, and under what gaming conditions? Second, do cognitive and brain changes represent specific learning or broader enhancement? Third, how can gaming support well-being and relationships while also becoming associated with clinically significant impairment? The indicator-level tables answer these questions first; the accompanying analysis explains mechanisms, conflicting findings, and practical meaning.

**The scope is international and date-bounded.** This detailed literature review covers human research through early 2021 without imposing a geographic restriction. “Active video games,” also called *exergames*, require substantial bodily movement; “action games” describe a genre involving rapid perceptual and motor demands and are often played while seated. Esports means organized competitive gaming, while online gaming describes internet-connected play. These categories overlap, but none is itself a diagnosis.

## Physiological Health

### Body composition, obesity, and cardiorespiratory function

**Active games show the clearest benefits.** Compared with sedentary play or minimal intervention, movement-based games can increase energy expenditure and produce small improvements in weight-related outcomes. Conventional gaming shows more variable associations with obesity, while a raised heart rate during esports competition reflects acute physiological load rather than proof of improved aerobic fitness. [4][5][6]

**Weight-related improvements were generally modest.** Oliveira and colleagues pooled 12 randomized controlled trials in children and adolescents. Active games reduced body mass index (BMI), or age-standardized BMI expressed as a z-score, relative to no or minimal intervention. The short-term standardized mean difference (SMD) was −0.34, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.62 to −0.05; the intermediate-term estimate was −0.36. Body weight also favored active games at intermediate follow-up, SMD −0.25, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.46 to −0.04. These are standardized effects, not reductions of 0.34 BMI units or 0.25 kilograms. [4]

**Direct body-fat measurements add detail.** The 2013 systematic review *Exergaming as a Strategic Tool in the Fight against Childhood Obesity* summarized Maddison et al.’s randomized trial of 322 overweight or obese children aged 10–14. At 24 weeks, the reported treatment contrasts favored active gaming by 0.24 BMI units, 0.83 percentage points of body fat, and 0.80 kg of fat mass. A smaller 12-week trial by Ni Mhurchu et al., involving 20 children aged 10–14, reported a 1.4 cm advantage in waist-circumference reduction for active games over conventional games. These outcomes show why body composition should include fat and waist measures rather than BMI alone. [9]

**Statistical improvement need not be clinically large.** Kracht and colleagues described a 16-week trial of 75 overweight children receiving family weight management with or without exergaming. Adding exergaming produced an approximately 0.12-unit advantage in BMI z-score reduction and one additional hour of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per week. The review compared that BMI z-score change with a cited clinically meaningful reduction of at least 0.25 units. Thus, exergaming can be useful within weight management without being a sufficient obesity treatment by itself. [7]

**Conventional gaming does not show uniform obesity effects.** In Kracht et al.’s 26-study child review, 14 studies found no association and 12 found a positive association between gaming and obesity; none found an inverse association. Calculated from those counts, the distribution was approximately 54% null and 46% positive—not a 46% probability that gaming causes obesity. Pelletier et al.’s separate review of healthy players identified a preliminary higher-BMI signal in adults, while adolescent results were less consistent. Together, the reviews support a possible adverse association, not a universal effect across ages. [7][5]

**The replacement activity explains much.** Active games can improve energy balance when they replace seated gaming, but their advantage may shrink when they replace existing exercise. Gao, Chen, Pasco, and Pope’s 2015 meta-analysis of 35 articles found a large aggregate health-outcome effect against sedentary behavior, marginal physiological differences against laboratory exercise, and null-to-moderate effects against field-based physical activity. The defensible judgment is therefore comparator-specific: active games are a promising alternative to inactivity, not a consistently superior alternative to conventional exercise. [10]

**Acute cardiovascular responses are directly observable.** Mellecker and McManus’s 2008 experiment, summarized in the 2013 obesity review, tested 18 children aged 6–12 during short seated and active gaming bouts. The more movement-intensive running game produced substantially greater energy expenditure and heart rate than seated gaming. [9]

**Movement intensity matters within active gaming.** Bowling-style movements and full-body running are not equivalent exposures. In this experiment, the running game’s energy expenditure was approximately four times the seated condition’s value—5.23 ÷ 1.31 ≈ 4.0. Other studies summarized in the same review found energy expenditure comparable with moderate-intensity walking, while some less demanding games generated lower-intensity activity. A game’s “active” label therefore does not determine whether it supplies an adequate exercise dose. [9]

**Oxygen uptake requires careful interpretation.** Reviews reported favorable oxygen-consumption and maximal-oxygen-uptake outcomes for active games, alongside higher heart rate and perceived exertion than sedentary behavior. Nevertheless, oxygen consumed during a short gaming bout is an immediate metabolic response, whereas maximal oxygen uptake, commonly written VO₂max, concerns cardiorespiratory capacity. The practical conclusion is strongest for increased activity during play; a sustained fitness benefit depends on repeated sessions of sufficient intensity and duration. [1][9]

**Esports stress is a different stimulus.** Martin-Niedecken and Schättin reported tournament heart rates reaching 160–180 beats/min, and the esports–exercise review also described elevated heart rate and cortisol during competition. These observations indicate substantial competitive arousal even when the player remains seated. They do not make seated esports physiologically equivalent to running: similar heart rates can occur alongside very different muscular work, energy expenditure, and training adaptations. [6][11]

**Autonomic findings concern disorder-level samples.** Sugaya et al. summarized an adolescent study associating IGD with lower heart-rate variability, greater sympathetic activity, and lower parasympathetic activity. Heart-rate variability describes variation between successive heartbeats and provides information about autonomic regulation rather than simply average pulse rate. This finding belongs alongside the clinical IGD evidence; it should not be assigned to all online players or esports athletes. [8]

### Physical activity, motor fitness, and muscular strength

**Fitness outcomes should remain separate.** Activity during gameplay, total weekly activity, balance, motor skills, and muscular strength measure different things. Active games have comparatively favorable balance and agility findings, but their effects on overall activity and fundamental motor skills were mixed. Muscular strength warrants its own uncertain classification rather than inheriting a positive label from improvements in coordination. [12][4]

**Activity during play is not total activity.** A 2020 review of six adolescent randomized trials concluded that all but one improved activity or slowed the decline in moderate-to-vigorous activity. Its interventions lasted from 10 weeks to one year and used accelerometers, pedometers, Fitbits, or validated self-reports. Oliveira et al., however, found no significant advantage over minimal intervention for total physical activity despite favorable BMI and weight results. These findings can coexist because more movement during a session need not increase activity across the entire day. [13][4]

**Compensation is a plausible explanation.** A participant may replace another active pastime with exergaming, become less active later, or gradually stop using the game. Conversely, a structured intervention may add activity that otherwise would not occur. Differences in adherence, supervision, comparison activities, and measurement periods therefore help explain why a review can find positive session-level physiology but a null total-activity result. This is an interpretation of the contrasting findings, not a measured explanation for every trial.

**Esports athletes can exercise and sit extensively.** The systematic review *E-athletes’ lifestyle behaviors, physical activity habits, and overall health and wellbeing* included six articles and concluded that a majority of the studied athletes met World Health Organization activity guidelines, despite considerable sedentary time and reported injuries. Pelletier et al. also cited a preliminary 73% guideline-adherence estimate. In another descriptive esports sample, 40% reported no physical activity. These estimates describe different samples and should not be combined into one population percentage. [14][5]

**Daily exercise does not describe sitting patterns.** Martin-Niedecken and Schättin summarized survey reports of approximately 1.08 hours of exercise per day among professional and high-level players, alongside gaming practice averaging 5.28 hours daily in one study. Other reports described 3–10 hours of gaming practice per day and sessions involving three or more hours seated without standing. The important distinction is between participating in exercise and avoiding prolonged uninterrupted sitting: one behavior does not directly measure the other. [6]

**Motor-skill learning depends on movement quality.** Liu et al.’s review distinguished fundamental skills, such as running, jumping, throwing, and catching, from fitness components such as balance and agility. Only two of five motor-skill studies showed significant intervention–control improvements. A convincing mechanism for this inconsistency is that commercial games may reward successful input without requiring technically proficient movement: a player can sometimes score well through small or poorly executed gestures. [12]

**Purpose-built feedback offers a useful counterexample.** In one eight-week study summarized by Liu et al., children using a purpose-built exergame improved across several locomotor skills, whereas children using commercial games improved in only one measured skill. The purpose-built intervention incorporated teacher adjustment of the gaming environment. Another eight-week comparison found both Kinect-based training and conventional motor-skill instruction improved object-control skills, with no significant difference between those two interventions. These findings favor appropriate task design and instruction rather than the assumption that any sports-themed game teaches real-world technique. [12]

**Balance findings extend beyond children.** The 2016 meta-analysis *How Effective Are Active Videogames Among the Young and the Old?* pooled six older-adult studies involving 142 participants and reported a random-effects Hedges’ g of 0.68 for functional balance, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.13–1.24. That is a favorable balance result, not a strength result. The synthesis explicitly separated a muscle-strength study because its outcome differed from the balance measures being pooled. [15]

**Muscle use is not strength training.** Esports requires repeated hand actions and sustained postural muscle activity, while exergames can demand larger movements. Nevertheless, muscular strength means force-producing capacity, not merely muscle activation, dexterity, or tolerance of repetitive movements. The appropriate early-2021 judgment is Mixed/Uncertain: neither balance gains nor high input frequency establishes that gaming increases strength. A positive strength judgment would require a game and training dose that demonstrably improve a direct force measure. [6][15]

### Injuries, pain, sleep, diet, and general health

**Injury patterns differ by physical demand.** Active games can involve movement-related injuries, whereas seated esports more often raises concerns about repetitive inputs, sustained posture, and visual fatigue. Pain, injury, and poor self-rated health should also remain distinct: a symptom report does not automatically identify a diagnosed injury or its cause.

**Active-game injuries are documented events.** The 2015 injury review included 13 papers, all involving Nintendo Wii, and identified the shoulder and knee as prominent injury sites. Wii tennis and bowling generated particularly frequent injury reports within that literature. The finding supports recognizing movement-related hazards, but the reported case distribution should not be read as the probability of injury each time someone plays one of those games. [16]

**Esports loading is repetitive rather than impact-based.** Martin-Niedecken and Schättin described prolonged sitting, continuous neck and back demands, hand-muscle activity, and reports of up to 400 clicks or keystrokes per minute. Sustained posture and repeated small movements provide plausible pathways to tissue irritation and overuse complaints. The practical implication is that esports health management concerns workload and recovery as well as general fitness; a player can be physically active outside gaming and still experience local symptoms during practice. [6]

**Pain evidence is less uniform than injury descriptions.** Pelletier et al. identified three pain studies: one described complaints among esports athletes, one found a weak association with gaming time, and another found no association with back, upper-limb, or diffuse pain. These results support a Mixed/Uncertain classification for a general gaming-time–pain relationship. They also suggest why total hours alone may be an incomplete exposure measure: posture, uninterrupted session length, equipment, and the movements performed could matter more than a single weekly total. [5]

**Clinical reports cover additional bodily symptoms.** Paulus et al. discussed wrist, neck, and elbow pain; tendosynovitis, sometimes called “nintendinitis”; skin blisters and calluses; sore tendons; hand–arm vibration syndrome; and peripheral neuropathy in the problematic-gaming literature. These reports establish the types of medical complaints considered, rather than a single syndrome shared by all gamers. They should be described by their specific clinical outcome, not combined into an undifferentiated claim that gaming damages the body. [17]

**Dietary mechanisms remain plausible but variable.** Of three dietary studies in Pelletier et al., the randomized crossover experiment found increased food intake during gaming regardless of appetite sensations. Another found no energy-intake difference between frequent and infrequent players, while a third found no association with fruit-and-vegetable consumption. Gaming-related eating could therefore contribute to energy balance in some settings, but it does not supply a uniform explanation for the mixed obesity findings. [5]

**General health is a broad outcome.** Four studies examined general health, with findings including poorer health among players, weak negative associations with playing time, and a marginal association between ritualized gaming motives and health complaints. This supports a preliminary negative signal for self-rated health. It is less specific than measured fat mass, a balance test, or an injury diagnosis, so its practical meaning is that some players report worse overall functioning—not that a particular physiological system has necessarily deteriorated. [5]

**Physiological takeaway:** Active games can improve selected outcomes when they replace inactivity, while conventional gaming and esports require outcome-specific attention to sitting, repetitive load, symptoms, and daily health behaviors.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Scope and overall finding**

**Mixed/Uncertain (evidence through 31 March 2021):** Huard Pelletier, Lessard, Piché, Tétreau and Descarreaux (2020-10-02), *Video games and their associations with physical health: a scoping review*, synthesized 12 peer-reviewed studies of healthy players [41]. More gaming time was preliminarily associated with higher BMI and lower self-reported general health; evidence was insufficient for activity/sedentary behaviour, sleep/fatigue, musculoskeletal pain or diet, so no strong causal conclusion follows [41].

- **BMI/body composition/obesity — Mixed/Uncertain:** BMI used reported weight and height through a homemade questionnaire. The detailed synthesis discusses five studies (overview: four, 33%): two moderate associations, one weak association and one teenage null result; increased BMI was limited to adults. Frequent play was also linked to central adiposity and high-sugar, low-fibre intake in young males [41].
- **Physical activity/sedentary time — Mixed/Uncertain:** Three studies reported a moderate negative association with vigorous activity, no association, and a high-risk descriptive finding that 40% of players did no physical activity. Gaming-time evidence for sedentary behaviour was insufficient; separate data linked sitting for **≥8 hours/day** with higher BMI and lower activity [41].
- **Active games, esports and energy — Mixed/Uncertain:** Active/exergames were excluded from the main review. Preliminary cited data reported **73%** of eSports players meeting activity guidelines; exergames briefly produced light-to-moderate activity, but long-term effects were unclear. One **8%** randomized crossover study found increased food intake; no quantified energy-expenditure or Kinect result was reported [41].
- **Pain/musculoskeletal symptoms — Mixed/Uncertain:** Three low-risk studies reported eSport complaints descriptively, a weak gaming-time association, and no association with back, upper-limb or diffuse pain. Self-report, non-validated tools and contradictory findings made evidence insufficient [41].
- **Sleep/fatigue and diet — Mixed/Uncertain:** Five sleep/fatigue studies yielded one weak association and two null findings; three low-risk diet studies found no frequent-player energy-intake difference, experimentally increased intake regardless of appetite, and no fruit/vegetable association. A cited screen-time review reported adverse sleep findings in **90%** of studies and proposed >2 hours of pre-bed light exposure/melatonin disruption [41].
- **Cardiometabolic indicators — Mixed/Uncertain (unsupported, not null):** No direct gaming-specific blood-pressure, glucose or lipid result was reported; these remain a research gap. Physical activity, BMI and sitting were discussed only as possible pathways to longer-term blood-pressure or diabetes risk [41].

**Esports.** A 2023 Swiss cross-sectional study (data May 2021–September 2022; post-cutoff) compared 51 competitive esports players with 51 age/sex-matched nonesports players. BMI (23.9 vs 23.1 kg/m²), body fat (18% vs 19%) and WHtR (.46 vs .44) were nonsignificant; overweight was 16/51 (31%) vs 12/51 (24%), obesity 2/51 (4%) vs 0, WHtR>.5 8/51 (16%) vs 4/51 (8%), and body-fat obesity 5/51 (10%) in each group [45]. **Active games.** No significant MVPA improvement at 3 or 6 months was reported in a 2025 six-month Boston RCT of 135 children aged 7–14 with overweight/obesity; 79 completed visits (mean 10.9±1.7 years; 63% boys; 62% Black, 25% Latino, 11% mixed, 1% Asian). Measures included fat/lean mass, BMI%, MVPA, insulin, glucose, lipids and CRP [64]. Post-hoc months 0–3 showed +6.8 min/day MVPA for Narrative+AVG versus waitlist; AVG-only had 815 g less lean-mass gain and 7.2 mg/dL less HDL gain, while moderate PA exceeded waitlist by 2.8 and 5.6 min/day and brisk walking by 198.8 steps/day versus AVG-only [64]. Missing gameplay-intensity/engagement data prevent active–sedentary attribution; sedentary-console harm is only proposed. No direct sleep/nutrition result was reported, and diet was not balanced as a confounder [64].

## Cognitive and Brain

### Attention, spatial ability, memory, and overall cognition

**Specific skills show stronger benefits than general intelligence.** The most defensible positive findings concern visual-spatial processing, selected attention tasks, and some perceptual or visuomotor skills. Memory and executive-function results vary more, and comprehensive meta-analysis did not establish broad cognitive enhancement. Gaming expertise and clinically problematic gaming also need separate treatment. [18][2]

**Attention advantages are substantial but task-specific.** Nuyens et al. reviewed 32 experimental or quasi-experimental papers: eight on task switching, 22 on attentional control, and two on sub-second time perception. The review described more effective top-down attention and processing speed among non-problematic players. Top-down attention means directing attention toward a goal, such as tracking a relevant opponent while ignoring distracting movement; it differs from automatic capture by a sudden stimulus. [19]

**Training evidence is more restrained than expertise comparisons.** Toth et al.’s dual systematic review found positive visual-attention effects in seven of 11 action-game intervention studies, compared with favorable player–nonplayer differences in 25 of 28 group-comparison studies. These proportions describe study results, not the percentage of people who benefit. Their contrast matters: habitual players can differ from nonplayers through practice, self-selection, or other characteristics, whereas interventions more directly test whether starting to play changes performance. [11]

**Spatial cognition has a comparatively favorable signal.** Bediou et al.’s 82-study action-game meta-analysis, summarized by Toth et al., identified spatial cognition, top-down attention, and perception as its strongest domains. Relevant tasks included mental rotation, locating targets, and tracking objects. Fast-moving games repeatedly demand these operations, making task-related learning plausible. The resulting judgment is Positive Impact for selected spatial skills, rather than a claim that every genre improves all forms of spatial reasoning. [11]

**Memory provides an important counterexample.** In Toth et al., only one of four intervention studies improved memory, while five of seven player–nonplayer comparisons favored action-game players. The intervention studies used different working-memory, short-term-memory, and spatial-memory tasks. Blacker et al.’s positive training study involved 30 hours of action gaming across 30 days, but the overall pattern does not establish a general memory-training effect. Better memory may facilitate gaming success, develop with prolonged practice, or both. [11]

**Broad cognitive enhancement is a stronger claim.** Sala, Tatlidil, and Gobet examined three models: correlations between gaming skill and cognitive ability, differences between players and nonplayers, and effects of game training. The models included 310, 315, and 359 effect-size comparisons, respectively—not those numbers of independent trials. Their overall effects were small or null, and the authors found no evidence that gaming causally enhances general cognitive ability. Powers et al. likewise found that controlled experiments supported certain perceptual and motor gains without supporting the stronger claim that games make people generally smarter. [20][18]

**Transfer distance explains the apparent disagreement.** Improvement on a task resembling gameplay is *near transfer*; improvement in a substantially different activity is *far transfer*. Detecting brief visual targets shares demands with action gaming, whereas improved academic reasoning or general intelligence is much farther removed. Positive action-game reviews and cautious broad-cognition reviews therefore need not be mutually exclusive: they can be answering different questions about the breadth of learning.

**Genre matters without creating a universal winner.** Some reviews favored fast action games for selective attention, while Nuyens et al. described strategy-game expertise advantages in some flexibility comparisons. Powers et al., however, found that action or violent-game training was not more effective overall than nonaction or puzzle training in true experiments. A large result for movement-mimicking games was also concentrated in one study, which supplied 18 of 21 comparisons in that category. The appropriate implication is to match the game’s demands to the outcome, not to rank genres as generally “best for the brain.” [22][19][18]

**Executive control is not one ability.** Task switching measures how efficiently someone changes rules or goals; inhibition concerns restraining or cancelling a response. Nuyens et al. found many favorable switching results in non-problematic gamers, but problematic-gaming inhibition findings differed between Go/No-Go, Stroop, and response-cancellation tasks. The same literature reported impaired risky decision-making and difficulty delaying rewards while preserving decision-making under ambiguity. Combining these outcomes into a single “executive function” score can conceal meaningful differences. [19]

**Professional esports expertise is demonstrable but selective.** A 2020 study compared 14 Houston Outlaws professional Overwatch players with 16 amateur first-person-shooter players. Professionals showed their largest advantage on visual-spatial memory, more modest advantages in selective and sustained attention and auditory working memory, and higher three-dimensional multiple-object-tracking thresholds. Their improvement rate across tracking training did not differ from the amateurs’. This supports a specialized expertise profile more clearly than a general superiority in learning capacity. [21]

**The student implication concerns realistic expectations.** Gaming can be a setting for practicing particular cognitive operations, but the evidence does not justify treating ordinary play as a general intelligence or memory intervention. The broad-enhancement judgment would change if benefits reliably transferred to substantially different tasks and persisted beyond the training setting; superior scores among already-skilled players alone do not satisfy that condition.

### Brain structure, brain function, and imaging methods

**Brain changes are not automatically health improvements.** Healthy-player training studies demonstrated experience-related changes, while IGD studies described structural and functional differences associated with impaired control. The interpretation depends on the sample, game, task, and accompanying behavior—not simply whether a brain region becomes larger or more active. [23][2]

**Different methods answer different questions.** Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures anatomy; voxel-based morphometry (VBM) compares local gray-matter measurements across the brain. Functional MRI (fMRI) measures blood-oxygenation-related responses during tasks or at rest. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) characterizes water diffusion in white matter, while electroencephalography (EEG) records electrical activity; event-related potentials (ERPs) are EEG responses linked to particular events.

**Healthy-player interventions establish neuroplasticity.** The 2019 MRI systematic review included nine randomized intervention studies, with samples of 20–75 participants, training periods of 4–24 weeks, and reported training totals of 16–90 hours. Outcomes included gray-matter changes, task-related activity, and functional connectivity. This shows that gaming experience can accompany measurable brain adaptation; 16 hours was the shortest reported training total, not a validated minimum prescription for brain health. [23]

**Game demands corresponded to different systems.** The review included adventure, shooting, puzzle, rhythm, and strategy games. Its findings were more coherent when interpreted through the tasks being practiced than through a single “gaming effect.” [25]

**Navigation strategy can reverse structural direction.** In the reviewed shooter intervention, hippocampal gray matter increased among spatial learners but decreased among response learners; response learners also showed amygdala increases. Spatial learners navigate using relationships between environmental features, whereas response learners rely more on learned response sequences. This is a particularly important counterexample to claims that a genre uniformly enlarges or shrinks the hippocampus: players can engage different learning systems within superficially similar gameplay. [25]

**Functional activation has no simple good direction.** Kühn and Gallinat’s critical review described 15 functional intervention reports: seven reported increases only, while eight included decreases or decreases alone. Reduced activity can be interpreted as greater efficiency when performance is maintained or improved; increased activity can reflect greater recruitment, effort, or a different strategy. The imaging signal therefore needs a behavioral anchor before it supports a health judgment. [2]

**Healthy-player connectivity findings are promising but selective.** Palaus et al. described differences in frontoparietal systems involved in attention and working memory, including patterns interpreted as more efficient top-down allocation. Other reviewed findings concerned visual–motor white-matter pathways and hippocampal structure. Importantly, the review also noted that functional differences did not consistently track visual-spatial performance. A changed scan and an improved cognitive outcome are related possibilities, not interchangeable observations. [22]

**IGD structural findings concern control-related regions.** Reviews described smaller gray-matter volumes in regions including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and supplementary motor area. These regions participate in overlapping functions involving planning, valuation, monitoring, and action control. Other findings included increased striatal volume, so the evidence does not describe a uniform reduction throughout the brain. Sugaya et al. also reported null differences in several regions in another study. [8]

**VBM associations are not proof of damage.** The 2018 neurobiological review described a VBM comparison involving 61 young adult males in which IGD was associated with smaller gray-matter volume in executive-control regions. Anterior cingulate and supplementary motor volumes were inversely associated with impulsivity. These findings are compatible with differences in behavioral regulation, but regional volume alone does not establish tissue injury, explain when the difference arose, or diagnose an individual player. [24]

**Craving and reward responses distinguish some samples.** Paulus et al. summarized cue-related fMRI changes in IGD patients that were not observed in recreational game users in the cited comparison. They also described lower anterior-insula and dorsolateral-prefrontal activation during decision-making. The neurobiological review reported altered reward processing, including reduced ventral-striatal activation during anticipation of monetary rewards in a frequent-gamer comparison. These findings suggest potentially different responses to gaming cues and other rewards, rather than a single global increase or decrease in brain activity. [17][24]

**White-matter findings resist a simple benefit label.** A 2017 DTI study compared 58 males with IGD with 26 healthy male controls and found higher fractional anisotropy (FA) and lower radial diffusivity (RD) in several pathways. FA describes the directionality of water diffusion, while RD concerns diffusion perpendicular to the principal direction. The authors proposed increased myelination related to extensive gameplay, but these measures are indirect and do not by themselves establish better functioning. [26]

**Exposure-matched controls sharpen the comparison.** Dong et al. compared 42 people with IGD with 44 recreational internet-game users who played for similar amounts of time. Higher FA in several tract groups remained associated with IGD. Matching gaming time helps distinguish disorder status from simple exposure quantity, although it does not determine whether the brain differences preceded or followed the disorder. [27]

**Electrical and chemical measures add specificity.** A study summarized by Sala et al. found larger P3 ERP responses after 10 hours of *Medal of Honor* training among high-performing trainees, consistent with an attention-related change. Sugaya et al. also discussed magnetic resonance spectroscopy and quantitative EEG findings in adolescents with IGD and ADHD. Some neurochemical differences were shared with ADHD alone, illustrating why a neural difference in a comorbid sample need not be specific to gaming disorder. [20][8]

**Cognitive and brain takeaway:** Gaming can train particular operations and alter their neural correlates, but neither a specialized skill advantage nor a changed brain scan establishes general cognitive enhancement or universal harm.

## Psychological and Social Health

### Positive psychological and social impacts

**Enjoyment and connection deserve equal attention.** Reviews of ordinary online gaming and structured exergaming reported enjoyment, achievement, friendship, and community. Self-efficacy and broader well-being findings were more variable. The relevant contrast is not simply online versus offline: cooperation, competition, perceived competence, existing relationships, and problematic involvement all influence the experience. [28][29]

**Self-efficacy is not the same as enjoyment.** Self-efficacy means believing that one can successfully perform a particular activity. A person may enjoy a game without becoming more confident about exercise, social interaction, or health management outside it. Joronen et al.’s ten-study review found some favorable psychological outcomes but two null self-efficacy results; Santos et al.’s overview reported positive self-efficacy signals in other active-game contexts. The combined judgment is therefore Mixed/Uncertain rather than a universal confidence benefit. [29][1]

**Cooperation offers a plausible confidence pathway.** Rüth and Kaspar’s conceptual synthesis described cooperative parent–child exergaming as a setting that can foster motivation, self-efficacy, and prosocial behavior. Shared goals, achievable challenges, feedback, and encouragement can make success more accessible. This supports a context-dependent interpretation: the benefit may arise from how activity is structured and supported, not from the presence of a game alone. [31]

**Expectations can also reduce confidence.** Haring’s March 2021 experiment assigned 125 participants to a game prompt and 107 to an otherwise equivalent task prompt about managing type II diabetes. The game framing produced a small negative effect on expected self-efficacy, mediated by expected difficulty, with no differences in affect, flourishing, or self-esteem. Crucially, participants were told about the assignment but did not perform it. This is evidence about expectations before gameplay, not evidence that playing a health game lowers self-efficacy. [32]

**Enjoyment has a clearer positive signal.** Sublette and Mullan’s 16-study review found many online players valued enjoyment, achievement, friendship, and a sense of community. Joronen et al. also found favorable enjoyment results in exergaming, with the sole follow-up study indicating persistence for several months. Enjoyment is itself a legitimate well-being outcome and may help explain participation, although it should not be treated as proof that physical health or long-term functioning has improved. [28][29]

**Online interaction can be meaningful.** Multiplayer games provide opportunities to communicate, coordinate, exchange support, and maintain friendships around shared interests. Sublette and Mullan’s findings challenge the assumption that online play is inherently socially isolating. Rüth and Kaspar similarly described locally and remotely connected social exergaming. The important distinction is whether these contacts supply valued relationships and support, rather than counting online contact as automatically inferior to face-to-face interaction. [28][31]

**Offline relationships can benefit from shared play.** The family review discussed benefits associated with normal gaming engagement, while the social-exergaming synthesis described family satisfaction, closeness, and shared activity. A school example involving sixth-grade dance-game groups reported high enjoyment and considerable group cohesion over four weeks. These findings concern cooperative or structured participation; they do not establish that solitary play, adversarial competition, and family co-play have equivalent effects. [30][31]

**Competition can help or hinder engagement.** The social-exergaming synthesis described studies in which competition against peers reduced perceived exertion and improved affect compared with solitary play. It also discussed possible aggression or exclusion in competitive settings and lower perceived competence in one exergaming–exercise comparison. Consequently, the practical question is whether the mode offers an achievable challenge and a supportive social setting. If it instead creates repeated humiliation or exclusion, the expected social benefit changes. [31]

**Positive-impact takeaway:** Ordinary and cooperative gaming can provide genuine enjoyment, achievement, and connection, while broader confidence and well-being benefits depend on the activity and social setting.

### Internet addiction, Gaming Disorder, and Internet Gaming Disorder

**Impairment separates enthusiasm from disorder.** The World Health Organization defines Gaming Disorder through impaired control, gaming taking priority over other activities, and continuation or escalation despite negative consequences. Diagnosis requires significant functional impairment, normally evident for at least 12 months. WHO also emphasizes that only a small proportion of people who game are affected. [33]

| Term | Scope and defining features | Interpretation through early 2021 |
|---|---|---|
| Internet addiction/problematic internet use | Broad terms covering problematic use of internet activities, not gaming alone | Older findings require attention to the activity actually measured. |
| DSM-5 Internet Gaming Disorder | Research condition centered on gaming; at least five of nine criteria over 12 months, with clinically significant impairment or distress | Symptom thresholds and functional problems both matter. |
| ICD-11 Gaming Disorder | Online or offline gaming with impaired control, increasing priority, continuation despite harm, and significant impairment | More explicitly centered on persistent functional impairment. |

**The terminology is overlapping, not interchangeable.** Earlier literature used terms such as computer addiction, internet addiction, pathological gaming, and problematic gaming. A study of general problematic internet use may include activities other than gaming, so its results cannot automatically be assigned to IGD. Likewise, online connectivity does not establish disorder, and the ICD-11 definition explicitly includes offline gaming. [17][34]

**DSM-5 proposed nine symptom criteria.** These concern preoccupation; withdrawal-like symptoms; tolerance; unsuccessful control; loss of other interests; continued use despite psychosocial problems; deception; gaming to escape negative mood; and jeopardized relationships, work, or educational opportunities. At least five within 12 months form the proposed threshold, alongside significant impairment or distress. As of the requested period, IGD occupied DSM-5’s “Conditions for further study” section rather than being equivalent to every established DSM diagnosis. [17]

**Prevalence changes with the measurement basis.** Paulus et al.’s full review reported a median prevalence of approximately 2.0% among its population-based child and adolescent studies and 5.5% across the broader set, including clinical samples. These are review summaries of differently defined samples, not a universal rate among gamers. The distinction between representative and clinically enriched samples explains why apparently conflicting estimates can both be accurate descriptions of their own evidence sets. [17]

**University data illustrate diagnostic sensitivity.** A web survey of 7,022 first-year students at five Mexican universities found 12-month symptom-based prevalence of 5.2% under DSM-5 IGD and 2.7% under ICD-11 GD definitions. Requiring severe role impairment reduced those estimates to 0.74% and 0.46%, respectively. This demonstrates how strongly the apparent burden depends on distinguishing symptom endorsement from severe disruption of everyday functioning. [35]

**The systems identify overlapping cases.** Among 367 DSM-5-positive students in that survey, 178—48.5%—also met the ICD-11 definition, while 189 did not. Agreement was substantial but incomplete, with κ = 0.625. However, adjusted comparisons generally did not distinguish the overlapping and DSM-only groups on severe impairment or several psychopathology measures. A narrower definition therefore should not automatically be interpreted as selecting a uniformly more severe group. [35]

**Some criteria resemble ordinary engagement.** A three-round Delphi study involving 29 experts judged several ICD-11 features, especially impairment and continuation despite consequences, favorably. It also identified concerns about DSM-5 criteria such as tolerance and escapism, which may fail to distinguish problematic from non-problematic engagement. Playing longer as skills develop or playing to relax is not, by itself, equivalent to losing control over gaming despite serious harm. [36]

**Diagnostic takeaway:** Describe the actual screening or diagnostic framework, and assess loss of control and functional impairment rather than treating gaming hours, online play, or esports participation as diagnoses.

**The percentages describe reports, not patients.** González-Bueso et al.’s review included 24 articles with 53,889 participants: 21 cross-sectional articles and three prospective articles. Its figures—92% for anxiety, 89% for depression, 85% for ADHD symptoms, and 75% for social phobia/anxiety—describe the proportions of relevant association reports reaching statistical significance. They do not mean that 89% of people with IGD have depression or that gaming increases anxiety risk by 92%. [3]

**Depression fits a reciprocal account.** Gaming can offer achievement, distraction, and social acceptance to someone experiencing low mood. When involvement becomes disordered, disrupted sleep, neglected responsibilities, and relationship strain may in turn worsen mood. Richard, Temcheff, and Derevensky’s review of 57 longitudinal papers found reciprocal associations between depressive symptoms and Gaming Disorder, supporting a feedback-loop interpretation more strongly than a single universal direction. [37]

**Prospective evidence also identifies vulnerability.** In the South Korean iCURE cohort of 2,319 school students followed for two years, depressive symptoms were associated with subsequent high-risk IGD screening status, adjusted rate ratio 2.04. This is evidence that depressive symptoms can precede new high-risk cases, not merely follow them. The outcome was defined using a self-reported symptom screen, so it should be described as high risk rather than a clinician-confirmed diagnosis. [38]

**Anxiety includes several different experiences.** The reviewed associations may involve generalized distress, trait anxiety, withdrawal-like anxiety when gaming is stopped, or anxiety linked to social situations. Richard et al. reported increased anxiety among the longitudinal consequences of Gaming Disorder, while some individual studies found null anxiety differences. One clinical imaging study that excluded psychiatric comorbidity found higher depression scores but no significant anxiety difference between its retained IGD sample and controls. This illustrates how sample composition changes the question being answered. [37][26]

**ADHD concerns vulnerability as well as outcomes.** ADHD involves persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity–impulsivity; it is not synonymous with performing poorly on one attention test. In the iCURE cohort, ADHD symptoms were associated with incident high-risk IGD, adjusted rate ratio 2.53, and persistence, adjusted rate ratio 2.14. Richard et al. also found reciprocal relationships involving inattentive symptoms. These results make screening for co-occurring difficulties relevant without supporting the claim that gaming generally causes ADHD. [38][37]

**Attention benefits do not contradict ADHD associations.** Performing well on rapid visual-selection tasks and sustaining attention to everyday responsibilities are different outcomes. A player may excel when feedback is immediate and goals are salient yet struggle with inhibition, organization, or attention in less stimulating settings. The non-problematic cognitive literature and the IGD–ADHD literature therefore concern partly different populations and partly different abilities. [19][17]

**Social anxiety can encourage compensatory play.** Online environments may offer a less threatening way to interact, particularly when a player can choose an avatar and control self-presentation. The 2018 neurobiological review discussed avatar identification as a possible compensation for social anxiety and described associated fMRI responses. That interpretation can explain both an initial social benefit and a potential pathway toward overreliance on gaming, but it is a proposed mechanism rather than proof that avatar use produces disorder. [24]

**Sleep findings depend strongly on exposure.** Lam’s review included seven studies: three about addictive internet gaming and four about broader problematic internet use. It suggested poorer sleep quality in addictive gaming, particularly massively multiplayer online role-playing games, and insomnia or poor sleep in problematic internet use. Paulus et al. and Sugaya et al. also described disrupted sleep patterns and greater sleep disturbance in problematic or IGD samples. [34][17][8]

**General-gaming sleep results were less consistent.** Pelletier et al.’s physical-health review found one weak adverse association and two null findings among directly relevant low-risk comparisons, alongside other less directly informative studies. A descriptive estimate of the adverse-study share in that three-study subset is 1 ÷ 3 × 100 ≈ 33%; this measures consistency of findings, not a player’s probability of sleep harm. The resulting classification remains Mixed/Uncertain, with a more concerning pattern for disordered than ordinary involvement. [5]

**Timing and arousal offer plausible sleep pathways.** Gaming may delay bedtime, sustain emotional or physiological arousal, and expose players to evening screen light. These pathways are distinguishable from total weekly gaming hours. Lam proposed sleep problems as a possible mediator between gaming addiction and depression; the proposal is useful for understanding how problems could interact, but it is not a demonstrated mediation effect. [5][34]

**Other psychological problems require specific attribution.** Reviews discussed loneliness, low self-esteem, emotional dysregulation, conduct problems, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and dysfunctional coping. Esports literature separately described burnout and competitive distress. Meanwhile, the often-cited finding on sadness and suicidal thoughts involved heavy gamers with more than five hours of daily screen time, rather than an exclusively diagnosed IGD sample. It is therefore a serious associated signal, not a gaming-specific causal risk estimate. [3][6][17]

**Psychological-health takeaway:** IGD is associated with substantial mental-health difficulties, but the most useful interpretation considers pre-existing vulnerability, co-occurring symptoms, and reciprocal deterioration rather than attributing every problem to gaming alone.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Scope and measurement**

Through 31 March 2021, social findings concern distinct constructs—IGD or problematic gaming, gaming time, online play, and ordinary gaming. In one adolescent study, DSM-5 IGD required at least 5 of 9 symptoms; an adult online-gamer study instead used the 13-item, 5-point AICA-S, with at least 13.5 points or five criteria indicating “addictive use” [142][267].

**Peer relationships**

**Peer relationships — Mixed/Uncertain.** Among 2,666 Chinese middle-school students (mean age 12.77 ± 0.75 years; Shanghai and Xi’an; cross-sectional self-report, published 16 January 2020), 346 students (13.0%) met the DSM-5 IGD screen. Peer bullying (adjusted OR 1.85) and online bullying (OR 1.76) were associated with higher IGD odds, whereas peer support (OR 0.78) and positive peer relationships (OR 0.84) were protective. Bullying was measured from 0–4, support from 1–7, and relationship quality from 1–10 [268]. These are correlates of IGD, not evidence that IGD caused poor peer relationships.

Among 9,733 Dutch adolescents surveyed during 2009–2012, a 5-point Network of Relationships Inventory produced sex- and subgroup-specific findings: males with high online and real-life friendship quality were more likely to be classified as At-Risk Gamers, males with high online but low real-life quality were more likely to be Problematic Gamers, and female Social Engaged Gamers reported higher-quality friendships in both settings (p < .003) [181]. A review likewise reports more peer problems in adolescent problematic-use or IGD groups and higher interpersonal-conflict and social-isolation scores among online than offline gamers, while also identifying few real-life friends, reduced relationships, and social exclusion as possible risk factors for IGD; directionality is therefore bidirectional or uncertain [142][266]. Relevant reviews include Paulus et al. (2018), *Internet gaming disorder in children and adolescents: a systematic review*, and Ferguson, Coulson, and Barnett (2011), *A meta-analysis of pathological gaming prevalence and comorbidity with mental health, academic and social problems* [266].

**Family relationships**

**Family relationships — Mixed/Uncertain, mostly adverse correlates.** Sugaya et al. (2019), *Bio-psychosocial factors of children and adolescents with internet gaming disorder: a systematic review*, identifies family factors as important among minors: problematic gamers showed more family conflict and poorer relationships, poor parent–child quality was associated with greater problem-gaming severity, and paternal bonds might be protective; however, one study found no strong child–parent association [142]. In the Chinese adolescent sample, parental psychological control (OR 1.82) and verbal or physical abuse (OR 1.59) were associated with higher IGD odds, while parental support (OR 0.78) and positive parent relationships (OR 0.84) were protective [268]. Schneider, King, and Delfabbro (2017), *Family factors in adolescent problematic Internet gaming: A systematic review*, is a core review source [267]. These cross-sectional, adolescent self-reports do not establish that IGD caused family conflict.

**Academic and professional development**

**Academic development — Negative Impact, but associational and incompletely quantified.** Review evidence links pathological gaming with poor grades, academic failure, low achievement, truancy, and poor school performance; however, the supplied citations to Skoric et al. (2009) and Ferguson et al. (2011) do not provide instruments or effect estimates [266]. A separate 2-year longitudinal study examined teacher autonomy support, basic-needs satisfaction, and school engagement as predictors of problematic online gaming, rather than reporting a direct grade or attainment effect [268].

### Peer relationships, family relationships, academic development, and work

**Functional consequences are central to the question.** Problematic gaming is associated with social isolation, poorer relationship quality, disrupted education, and occupational difficulties. Ordinary gaming can simultaneously support friendship and family interaction. The decisive contrast is whether gaming adds valued participation or increasingly displaces responsibilities and relationships.

**Peer effects can run in opposite directions.** Ordinary multiplayer gaming can extend existing friendships or create new communities, while disordered gaming is associated with loneliness, lower social competence, and isolation. Sublette and Mullan found significant negative consequences concentrated among players classified as addicted or problematic within their 16-study evidence set. That does not make ordinary gaming universally protective, but it argues against assigning clinical-group findings to every online player. [28]

**Social isolation can be both antecedent and consequence.** Richard et al.’s longitudinal review identified reciprocal associations between isolation and Gaming Disorder. A socially disconnected person may increasingly depend on gaming for belonging, while loss of control can reduce opportunities for other relationships. The practical distinction is whether online ties complement a broader social life or become the only setting in which the person can function comfortably. [37]

**Family relationship quality matters more than a simple ban.** Schneider, King, and Delfabbro reviewed 14 studies and found that poorer parent–child relationships were commonly associated with greater problem-gaming severity. Paternal relationships appeared protective in some studies. Yet parental-supervision findings were divided: two studies found an inverse association with problem gaming and two found no significant association. Restricting play can also be a response to an existing problem, making restriction itself an ambiguous marker of risk. [30]

**Developmental pathways connect family, school, and peers.** The family review summarized Zhu et al.’s one-year study of 833 Chinese students aged 11–14. Poorer parent–adolescent relationships predicted gaming-addiction problems indirectly through lower school connectedness and greater affiliation with deviant peers. This points to an interacting developmental context rather than a problem located solely in the game or solely in parenting. It also explains why relationship support and school engagement may matter alongside gaming behavior. [30]

**Supportive relationships show protective associations.** In the iCURE cohort, stronger parental attachment and greater social support were associated with lower changing IGD-severity scores, with adjusted incidence rate ratios of 0.86 and 0.87, respectively. These values are specific to that study’s models and scales, rather than universal percentages by which family support prevents IGD. Their contribution is to show that gaming exposure and social resources can have different associations within the same population. [38]

**Academic findings provide concrete functional measures.** Sugaya et al. reported that secondary-school students with IGD missed a mean of 19.34 classes over six months, compared with 4.70 among non-IGD students. The calculated difference is 14.64 classes, and the mean was approximately 4.1 times as high—19.34 ÷ 4.70. Reported gaming-related truancy was 65.63% versus 3.92%, and grade-point averages were worse in the IGD group. These are findings from the reviewed school sample, not a forecast for all students who game. [8]

**University evidence supports the same adverse association.** Samaha and Hawi’s 2020 study involved 345 university students, the IGD-20 screening test, and an 18-item student-engagement assessment. Greater engagement negatively predicted gaming-disorder scores, while gaming disorder negatively predicted cumulative grade-point average. The term “predicted” here refers to a statistical relationship, not proof that gaming caused a subsequent grade decline. The result is most relevant to problematic involvement, not recreational gaming considered without impairment. [39]

**Displacement is a plausible academic mechanism.** Gaming that repeatedly overrides sleep, attendance, deadlines, or study time can interfere with education. Conversely, weak school engagement or poor performance may make gaming-based achievement especially attractive. This reciprocal account is consistent with the longitudinal review’s academic findings and avoids treating every hour of leisure as academically harmful. Better performance on a visual task also does not cancel missed instruction or reduced preparation. [37][17]

**Occupational findings are primarily functional descriptions.** Sublette and Mullan reported negative occupational consequences among problematic online players, while Paulus et al. discussed work, educational, and financial problems. A clinic-based study also used distress and impaired behaviors—including joblessness or school refusal—within its operational assessment. Together, these sources support a qualitative negative association with work functioning; they should not be converted into an invented estimate of lost earnings, unemployment probability, or lifetime career progression. [28][17][26]

**Professional esports creates a separate occupational context.** For an esports professional, extensive practice may be part of employment rather than evidence of neglected employment. Relevant concerns instead include overuse symptoms, burnout, recovery, and whether competitive demands compromise health or functioning. Martin-Niedecken and Schättin discussed health problems that can constrain participation or careers, but those observations should remain separate from claims that recreational gaming generally damages professional development. [6]

**Social and developmental takeaway:** Problematic involvement is associated with meaningful relationship, educational, and occupational difficulties, while ordinary shared play can be socially valuable; the pattern of functioning determines which interpretation applies.

## Overall judgment and practical meaning

**There is no single gaming-health balance.** The evidence is most useful when organized around the activity actually performed, the outcome measured, and what gaming replaces. Movement-based play, cognitive expertise, online friendship, and clinically impairing behavior are different exposures, even when the same person experiences more than one of them.

| Gaming context | Strongest supported interpretation | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| Active video games | Increased movement and selected fitness benefits versus sedentary alternatives | Judge actual intensity, adherence, and movement quality. |
| Conventional recreational or online gaming | Enjoyment, connection, and selected skill benefits; variable physical-health associations | Consider how play fits alongside sleep, activity, relationships, and responsibilities. |
| Organized esports | Specialized cognitive expertise alongside competitive and repetitive physical demands | Treat workload, recovery, symptoms, and broader health behaviors as relevant. |
| IGD or Gaming Disorder | Clinically important impairment associated with psychological and functional difficulties | Evaluate loss of control, continuing harm, and co-occurring problems—not hours alone. |

**For a university review, wording matters.** Use “improved” for a measured intervention advantage, “associated with” for observational relationships, and “altered” for a neural finding whose health meaning is unsettled. Reserve “No Significant Impact” for a tested null result, such as the pooled physical-activity comparison, rather than using it for muscular strength or another outcome represented mainly by indirect evidence. This preserves the distinction between mixed findings, measured absence of a statistically significant difference, and a genuinely demonstrated benefit or harm.

**Practical interpretation should remain proportional.** Active games are a reasonable option when they replace sedentary time and provide an appropriate challenge, but they do not automatically replace all exercise. Enjoyable social gaming need not be pathologized, while persistent loss of control, deteriorating sleep, missed classes, neglected work, or damaged relationships deserve attention. WHO’s impairment-centered approach offers a more useful guide than assuming that online play or competitive ambition is inherently unhealthy. [33]

**The evidence has a defined measurement basis.** This synthesis uses research through early 2021; many findings involve small samples, self-report, heterogeneous games or diagnostic definitions, and observational designs, while some reviews share underlying studies. Standardized effects are not kilograms or BMI units, association-report percentages are not patient prevalence, and brain measures are not direct clinical outcomes. The calculated study shares, activity ratio, and attendance contrasts are descriptive estimates from stated inputs; strength, injury, and occupational conclusions remain qualitative rather than pooled force, incidence, or career-trajectory estimates. [5][23][3]

**The central conclusion is conditional.** Gaming can support activity, specialized learning, enjoyment, and relationships; it becomes a health concern when its physical demands or loss of behavioral control undermine the rest of a person’s life.
