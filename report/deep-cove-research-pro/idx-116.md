# Feeling the Qur’an: Arousalism, Situationist Constructs, and the Theater of Cruelty

**The three concepts offer useful but different perspectives.** Arousalism asks how artistic form arouses emotion; Situationist constructed situations emphasize participation in a changing environment; Antonin Artaud’s Theater of Cruelty examines how sensory intensity unsettles an audience. Qur’anic scholarship provides substantial analyses of sound, emotional response, participatory reading, and disturbing imagery. The strongest approach is to connect those documented analyses with the three theories while identifying the connections as comparisons, rather than treating them as interchangeable, established schools of Qur’anic interpretation.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arousalism | Audience-based theory of expressiveness; modern philosophy of art | A receptive listener feels an emotion through artistic form | Q. 101:1–11; Q. 39:23; recitation studies | Strong lens for sound and feeling; strict objectless-emotion theory fits only partly |
| Situationist Constructs | Constructed situations; Guy Debord and the Situationists, 1957–58 | Participants help realize an evolving experience | Q. 12:4, 12:15, 12:18; Q. 101:1–3 | Productive analogy for participation, temporal shifts, and interpretive gaps |
| Theater of Cruelty | Artaud’s early-1930s program; collected in 1938 | Voice, image, rhythm, and space disturb bodily complacency | Q. 81:1–14; Q. 101:9–11; Q. 56:41–56; Q. 78:21–30 | Illuminates visceral address when kept connected to judgment and ethics |

**The report follows three questions.** What does each theory actually claim, which Qur’anic studies provide the closest applications, and how do particular passages produce their effects? After establishing that feeling and meaning belong together, the three concept sections distinguish definitions and origins from passage-level analysis. The conclusion compares their explanatory gains and their tensions with Qur’anic authority, ethical instruction, and Islamic interpretive traditions.

## Introduction: Feeling and meaning belong together

**Affective inquiry expands the interpretive question.** Instead of asking only what a passage asserts, it asks how its recitation, pacing, images, and changes of address organize an encounter with that assertion. “Affect” here includes felt intensity and bodily orientation; “emotion” identifies more recognizable experiences such as fear, grief, hope, or reassurance. “Aesthetic” does not mean merely beautiful or pleasurable: disturbance, vulnerability, and solemnity also belong to aesthetic experience.

**Qur’anic scholarship already examines these dimensions.** Lauren E. Osborne’s 2016 essay, “The Experience of the Recited Qur’an,” explicitly asks what religious sound feels like. Her term “nondiscursive meaning” identifies significance experienced through sound and performance rather than exhausted by a verbal paraphrase. She describes recitation as an emergent event involving collaboration between performer and audience, shaped by listening cultures and occasions. [1]

**Feeling also belongs to understanding.** Karen Bauer and Feras Hamza’s 2024 study of Qur’anic plot asks how scripture seeks to produce emotions that encourage conduct associated with salvation. Their argument joins interior feeling, outward emotional expression, intellect, and behavior. An affective interpretation therefore need not replace a theological reading: it can explain how theological and ethical understanding becomes personally compelling. [2]

**Sound and semantics interact closely.** Michael Sells’s 1993 analysis of Sūrat al-Qāriʿa distinguishes semantic, acoustic, emotive, and grammatical-gender dimensions, but insists that they operate together. His purpose is not to sacrifice meaning to sound. It is to explain a multidimensional meaning in which sound patterns acquire significance through their position, recurrence, and relationship to what the passage evokes. [3]

**The comparison must preserve historical difference.** A theory developed to explain instrumental music, a revolutionary program for everyday life, and an experimental theatrical manifesto address different objects. Bringing them into Qur’anic analysis is most useful when each clarifies a particular relationship: form and feeling, narrative and participation, or sensory disturbance and moral attention. Their differences prevent “affect” from becoming a catch-all explanation.

**The scope is deliberately comparative.** The discussion draws on English-language scholarship, English-presented scholarly arguments, and translations of primary theoretical texts published through 13 September 2026. Qur’anic references use the form “Q. chapter:verse”; quoted translations are attributed, while descriptive paraphrases identify the relevant passage directly. The emphasis remains on what readers and listeners experience, without assuming that experience is independent of language, belief, or interpretive training.

## Arousalism: How form becomes feeling

### Definition, origins, and principal theorists

**Arousalism defines expression through response.** In its philosophical sense, an artwork expresses an emotion because it has the power or tendency to arouse that emotion in an appropriate audience. A musical passage is sad, on this account, because suitably receptive listeners tend to feel sadness when hearing it. Stephen Davies’s *Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry specifies that the relevant response is generally “objectless”: the listener feels sad without necessarily feeling sad *about* something. [4]

**Objectless does not mean causeless.** The artwork causes the response and is what the audience perceives, but it need not be the thing toward which the emotion is directed. Davies contrasts this with sadness for a dying character, disappointment with a badly written ending, or delight in a melodic turn. Those responses have identifiable objects; arousalism, narrowly understood, explains the mirroring response through which a work’s sadness supposedly becomes the listener’s sadness.

**Three approaches ask different questions.** Expression theories locate artistic emotion primarily in an artist’s expression; recognition-based approaches allow audiences to identify expressive qualities without sharing the emotion; arousalism gives the audience’s tendency to feel a central explanatory role. The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* presents arousalism within this debate over musical expressiveness. [5]

| Approach | Where explanation begins | Audience’s essential activity | Illustrative claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creator-expression theory | The maker’s expression | Encountering an expressed feeling | The work expresses the creator’s sadness |
| Recognition-based approach | Expressive qualities in the work | Recognizing emotional character | The listener hears sadness without becoming sad |
| Arousalism | The work’s response-producing disposition | Feeling the relevant emotion | The passage is sad because it tends to arouse sadness |

**Its history has distinct branches.** Philosophical arousalism should be distinguished from empirical “arousal theory” in psychology. The former asks what makes art emotionally expressive; the latter investigates how stimulation, novelty, complexity, and activation relate to attention or preference. These branches overlap in their interest in audiences, but one is not simply an earlier version of the other.

**Fechner supplies an empirical antecedent.** Gustav Fechner’s *Vorschule der Aesthetik* of 1876 is identified by the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* as the first thorough written account of empirical aesthetics. Its importance is the systematic study of aesthetic response. That historical role does not make Fechner the sole founder of the later philosophical claim that expressiveness consists in an emotion-arousing disposition. [6]

**Berlyne made activation theoretically central.** Daniel Berlyne’s *Aesthetics and Psychobiology* of 1971 and the edited *Studies in the New Experimental Aesthetics* of 1974 linked aesthetic preference to arousal. His model predicted greatest pleasure at intermediate activation, with variables such as novelty and complexity affecting arousal potential. The encyclopedia’s historical assessment reports mixed findings and substantial abandonment of that model. [6]

**The principal names have different roles.** Derek Matravers is an important philosophical defender, Stephen Davies and Peter Kivy are major critics, and Jenefer Robinson contributes a related account of lower-level affective processes. Colin Martindale belongs principally to the empirical debate surrounding Berlyne. They should not be listed as though they all advocated one theory.

| Daniel Berlyne | Major works, 1971 and 1974 | Psychobiological theorist | Intermediate arousal and aesthetic pleasure |

**Martindale’s contribution is specifically critical.** The 1996 article “Daniel E. Berlyne: Two Decades Later” discusses Martindale’s negative conclusions concerning stimulus variables, the proposed preference curve, and trade-offs among sources of arousal. It also challenges aspects of Martindale’s position and stresses context and the choice of outcome measure. This is a debate about aesthetic preference and psychological mechanisms, rather than a direct genealogy of philosophical arousalism. [7]

**Matravers and Robinson represent different possibilities.** The *Stanford Encyclopedia* identifies Matravers’s 1998 treatment as a sophisticated defense and notes his later second thoughts in 2011. It credits Robinson’s 1994 and 2005 work with helping establish the importance of less cognitive responses in the causal explanation of musical expressiveness. Accepting that bodily or relatively automatic responses contribute to emotion is a weaker commitment than defining expressiveness entirely through the emotion aroused. [5]

**The objections matter for scripture.** Competent listeners may recognize an emotion without sharing it; different emotions may arise from the same expressive passage; and explanations can become circular if a passage’s emotional character is inferred from the very response it supposedly explains. Davies emphasizes that one may reject arousalism while accepting that sad works sometimes evoke sadness. [4]

**A Qur’anic application therefore needs precision.** An arousalist comparison asks how patterned language and delivery contribute to feeling. It need not claim that the Qur’an’s expressiveness consists wholly in producing the same emotion in every qualified listener. Nor should physiological activation, aesthetic pleasure, spiritual attention, and a named emotion be treated as four measurements of one thing.

### Sound: Recitation gives form an emotional duration

**The strongest textual example is Q. 101.** Sells’s “Sound and Meaning in Sūrat al-Qāriʿa” offers a detailed account of how phonology, syntax, imagery, and semantic uncertainty interact. Tareq Moqbel’s 2021 “Aural Aesthetics” similarly argues that acoustic qualities and recitation rules contribute to Qur’anic guidance and interpretation. Their stated approaches are sound–meaning analysis and aural aesthetics; they provide the foundation for the arousalist comparison developed here. [3][8]

**Repetition establishes an unresolved encounter.** Q. 101:1–3 introduces *al-qāriʿa*, repeats it in a question, and then asks what can make the addressee know what it is. The root evokes striking, shocking, or breaking, but Sells preserves the term’s semantic openness. The listener receives an emphatic vocal presence before receiving a settled conceptual explanation. [3]

**Deferred explanation redirects attention.** If the opening immediately supplied an exhaustive definition, attention could move quickly from question to answer. Instead, repetition and syntactical incompleteness prolong expectation. The mechanism is not sound operating in a semantic vacuum: the language creates a demand for understanding, and the delay makes its acoustic organization more prominent.

**The next images lengthen attention.** Q. 101:4–5 compares people to scattered moths and mountains to colored, carded wool. Sells identifies syntactical, phonetic, and semantic balance between the verses, including recurring vowel and nasal patterns and runs of long syllables. These features make the reciter and listener linger over the images rather than pass over them as informational items. [3]

**Duration contributes to imaginative force.** The two balanced clauses give human disorientation and mountainous dissolution comparable audible space. What differs radically in ordinary experience—small, unstable moths and massive mountains—is brought into a shared pattern. Through this relationship, sound supports the passage’s collapse of familiar distinctions between vulnerability and security.

**Articulation can reinforce semantic contrasts.** In Q. 101:6–9, Sells examines the contrasting verbs for scales becoming heavy and light. Their rhythmic placement and surrounding sound patterns make the contrast perceptible in the act of speaking. His claim is contextual: the words acquire these expressive qualities through their deployment within the sūrah, not because individual phonemes carry fixed emotional meanings. [3]

**Performance adds another explanatory layer.** Michael Frishkopf’s 2009 study of mediated recitation in Egypt identifies variations in timbre, pitch range, mode, dynamics, pauses, accent, melisma, pacing, and syllable duration. *Melisma* means extending one syllable across several pitches. These choices can change the expressive character of a recitation without changing its linguistic text. [9]

**The same verse permits contrasting experiences.** Frishkopf compares recordings of Q. 12:4 by Shaykh Mustafa Ismaʿil in *mujawwad*, an elaborate recitational style, and *murattal*, a more measured, comparatively restrained style. His analysis reports average syllable durations of 0.74 seconds and 0.45 seconds respectively, alongside differences in pitch range, ornamentation, and repetition. In the live mujawwad recording, the verse unfolds across three breath phrases, with audience responses during pauses. [9]

**That contrast changes the explanatory emphasis.** A written verse has phonological possibilities, but a particular performance realizes those possibilities through timing, breath, and vocal texture. The arousalist question thus concerns a relationship among text, performance, listener, and occasion. It is less adequately answered by calling the written text an automatic “emotional trigger.”

**Breathing connects technique and experience.** Osborne’s interviewee Fatima explains her preference for shorter verses partly through the opportunity to pause and breathe. Another reciter, Abd Allah, associates Sūrat al-Raḥmān with calming listeners after work. These examples show bodily technique, thematic content, and social expectation working together. [1]

### Language and imagery: Emotion is not separate from understanding

**Q. 39:23 describes bodily reception.** The verse presents revelation as discourse before which the skins of those who fear their Lord shiver, followed by the softening of skins and hearts toward God’s remembrance. As a descriptive paraphrase, its emotional movement is from trembling to receptivity. It is particularly relevant because the scripture itself connects hearing, bodily sensation, religious orientation, and a changing emotional state.

**The sequence resists simple excitation.** Shivering and softening are not two interchangeable signs of greater stimulation. They suggest different bodily orientations and a transition between them. A useful arousalist reading asks how sound and content may sustain that movement; a stronger claim that the verse specifies one universal physiological mechanism would confuse textual representation with audience measurement.

**Recognition can itself produce feeling.** Q. 5:83 describes eyes filling with tears because listeners recognize truth in what has been revealed. Karen Bauer’s 2017 overview uses this verse to argue against an exclusively aesthetic explanation of Qur’anic emotion: the content recognized by the listener matters. This is an important counterexample to the idea that the Qur’an moves people only before or apart from comprehension. [10]

**Strict arousalism fits only partway.** Fear of judgment, grief for Joseph, or gratitude for mercy ordinarily has an object. The listener fears, grieves, or gives thanks *for something*. These responses belong more comfortably to a broad account of aesthetic emotion than to the narrow philosophical thesis about objectless feeling. The valuable transfer is attention to form’s emotional contribution, not the assumption that all scriptural emotion has the structure proposed for instrumental music.

**Imagery makes an assertion perceptible.** The moths and wool of Q. 101 do not simply decorate an independently complete statement about the end of the world. They invite the audience to imagine scattered movement, lost solidity, and altered scale. Sells connects these images with an “ontological reversal”: apparently secure worldly structures disappear, while the moral weight of human action becomes decisive. [3]

**Sensory portrayal has Islamic interpretive resources.** Bishr Pandikkad’s 2025 study summarizes Sayyid Qutb’s *al-taṣwīr al-fannī*, or artistic depiction, through *taṣwīr ḥissī*, sensory portrayal, and *tajsīm*, embodiment. On that account, abstract truths become perceptible scenes. This offers a particularly useful comparison because it joins aesthetic force to revelatory purpose rather than treating form as independent of truth. [11]

**The mechanisms can be distinguished.** Sound directs the duration and texture of attention; syntax creates expectation or postpones completion; imagery supplies perceptible relations; semantic recognition gives the feeling an object and significance. Their combination explains more than a list of emotional adjectives.

| Dimension | Formal feature | Passage | Affective operation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sound | Repetition and sustained syllables | Q. 101:1–5 | Prolongs expectation and attention |
| Language | Questions and delayed completion | Q. 101:1–3 | Makes understanding an unfolding encounter |
| Imagery | Moths and carded wool | Q. 101:4–5 | Recasts human and cosmic stability |
| Recognized content | Truth acknowledged through tears | Q. 5:83 | Connects understanding with emotional response |

**Phonological arousal is another distinct usage.** The study titled “Phonological Arousal in Surah Ṭaha,” framed through Hinton’s theory, points bibliographically toward *sound symbolism*: associations between sounds and meaning. Its references include Hinton, Nichols, and Ohala’s 1994 *Sound Symbolism*. For the present comparison, its stated orientation belongs beside phonological interpretation, rather than supplying a result about Matravers-style emotional expressiveness or a measured listener response. [12]

### A concrete corpus: The fifteen sajdah locations

**A recent study makes the comparison specific.** Zahra Aulia Hanifa, Dita Puspita Dewi, and Asep Sopian’s 2026 “Phonological Rhetoric of Submission” examines fifteen *sajdah* locations—passages associated with prostration. Using phonetic transcription and stylistic “foregrounding,” meaning patterned prominence that attracts attention, the authors connect recurring sounds with solemnity, command, and submission. [13]

**The distribution concerns textual classification.** The authors assign each location a dominant phonological feature. The shares below are calculated from their counts: each count divided by fifteen and multiplied by 100, rounded to one decimal place where necessary. They describe the study’s corpus, not percentages of listeners experiencing an emotion.

**Their interpretation joins softness and force.** The authors associate recurring rounded or long vowels, including /u/ and /ā/, with resonance and solemn continuity. They relate concentrated consonantal articulation, involving sounds such as /r/, /q/, and /ṭ/, to imperative force. Repetition, terminal sounds, and patterned duration then support a movement from divine command toward receptive submission. [13]

**The bodily endpoint is especially relevant.** Q. 17:109 and Q. 19:58 connect hearing with weeping and prostration; Q. 96:19 commands prostration and drawing near. These passages help move the analysis beyond a private feeling that remains inside the listener. The response takes the form of an orientation or practice, in which bodily action and understanding belong together.

**Context remains central to that interpretation.** Sells’s model explains why the study’s sound associations should be read relationally: a phoneme becomes expressive within a particular semantic and acoustic arrangement. The sajdah study supplies a defined corpus and recurring patterns; its more persuasive contribution is the sound–meaning relationship, rather than a universal dictionary equating individual Arabic sounds with particular feelings.

### Reception evidence and the resulting judgment

**Different studies measure different outcomes.** Literary interpretation explains a passage’s expressive organization; interviews describe experience; electroencephalography, or EEG, records electrical activity associated with particular tasks. Comparing them is useful when their distinct outcomes remain visible.

| Study or account | Material and comparison | Population or design | Reported finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kamal and colleagues, 2013 | Reciting Q. 39:20–31 versus reading a novel | Ten healthy men; EEG | Higher alpha-band power during recitation |
| Samhani and colleagues, 2022 | Listening to Q. 1:1–7 versus Arabic news | EEG; 128 channels, nineteen electrodes analyzed | Decreased left-occipital beta power |
| Cairo phenomenological study | Everyday Qur’anic listening | Six listeners aged 28–40; diaries and interviews | Accounts of immersion, weeping, release, and captivated listening |
| ICU trial summarized in 2022 | Thirty minutes of recitation versus rest | Fifty-five intensive-care patients | No significant differences in reported physiological outcomes |

**The EEG findings concern task differences.** A review reports that Kamal and colleagues found higher alpha-band power during recitation than novel reading; participants also heard their own voices. It reports that Samhani and colleagues associated decreased left-occipital beta power during listening with visual-mental imagery. These are physiological correlates interpreted in relation to calm or imagery, rather than direct readings of religious awe, sadness, or truth recognition. [14]

**The listening accounts describe emotional texture.** The Cairo study selected six heavy listeners after initial interviews with nearly fifty people. Their diaries were recorded in March 2020, with interviews conducted during May 2020. Its phenomenological discussion relates listening to catharsis—immersion, weeping, and release of tension—and *ṭarab*, an emotionally captivated state associated with performance. [15]

**A larger review adds mixed findings.** A 2022 systematic review synthesized twenty studies involving 2,566 participants, covering literature through April 2021: seven randomized or experimental studies, five quasi-experimental studies, and eight observational studies. It reported promising associations with several psychological outcomes among Muslims, alongside the ICU trial’s non-significant results for physiological measures and recovery. [16]

**The judgment favors a relational account.** Sound and performance demonstrably offer more explanatory detail than a proposition-only reading, while object-directed religious emotions extend beyond strict arousalism. The conclusion becomes stronger where a specific feature, performance, and reported experience can be connected; it becomes weaker where “arousal” merely renames whatever response occurs.

**Arousalism’s practical interpretive gain:** it makes the contribution of form to feeling explicit, while Qur’anic examples show why that contribution must remain connected to understanding, religious orientation, and embodied practice.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Definition and terminological boundary**

**Arousalism** is best treated here as a contested, listener-centred family of theories of **musical expressiveness**, not as a verified single school. Its central formula is that a musical passage expresses emotion *E* if and only if it arouses *E* in listeners [145]. Thus arousal theory prioritizes emotions evoked in the audience, whereas expression theory prioritizes the artist’s emotions in creating the work [155]. The theory does not equate every feeling with relevant arousal: associative responses arise from personal memories, broad responses relate to musical features without tracking formal change, and music-specific arousal changes as the music changes [145]. The proposed mechanism is that formally non-emotional features produce an emotional state, after which the listener hears the work as expressive; the cited musical examples are slow tempo and an insistently chromatic piano part [145].

The label remains qualified. Arousalism has been heavily challenged as a theory of expressiveness, although rejecting it does not mean denying that music can arouse emotion [145]. The 2023 source primarily uses **arousal theory**, not “Arousalism,” and does not establish the latter as a standard named school [155]. A traceable chapter titled *Expression Theories and Arousalism*, in *Imagination, Music, and the Emotions*, was published on **7 August 2017** (DOI: 10.2307/jj.18252905.5), but its title does not establish the concept’s first formulation [146].

**Terminology and mechanism**

On the supplied evidence, “Arousalism” is best used as shorthand for a family of “arousal theories of expression,” not a securely bounded school under that exact name. The core definition is that an artwork expresses *x* when it can arouse a feeling or sensation of *x* in a viewer or listener; sad music is the standard example [154]. The approach focuses on effects, unlike expression theory’s focus on the artist and embodiment theory’s focus on properties located in the work, which need not be felt by the audience [154]. Matravers’s Dispositional/Arousal theory makes the listener–work perceptual relation central: musical expressiveness is a disposition to arouse the emotion heard as expressed [158].

Arousal is not an automatic stimulus–response. Basic properties may cause responses, but the relation is contingent, empirically discoverable, and unsystematic; expressive effect combines representational content with the medium’s way of representing it [158]. Sustained attention to dynamic properties can make dissonance resolving into consonance feel like tension resolving into relaxation [158]. Relevant senses, understanding, a qualified observer, and normal conditions are required; spatial setting, prior sensory experience, culture, memories, beliefs, will, and momentary disposition can modify response [158]. The response must be appropriate and analogous to human expression, not incidental irritation—the piano-student case is excluded. “Dry-eyed” criticism challenges the necessity of conscious feeling, although Matravers allows for suppressed or below-conscious incipient feeling [158].

**Genealogy and roster**

The documented genealogy runs from Francis Hutcheson’s *An Inquiry concerning Beauty, Order, Harmony and Design* (1725), through Archibald Alison’s *Essays on the Nature and Principles of Taste* (1790), to Colin Radford’s “Emotions and Music: A Reply to the Cognitivists” (1989) [154]. Later figures include Jerrold Levinson (1990) and Aaron Ridley (1995), who describe truncated, context-poor aroused emotions, and Jenefer Robinson’s “The Expression and Arousal of Emotion in Music” (1994), which distinguishes directly aroused primitive emotions from complex emotions inferred from them [154]. Derek Matravers is the directly documented main supporter here through *Art and Emotion* (1998) and “The Experience of Emotion in Music” (2003); the discussing article was published on 2012-02-29 but does not establish a first formulation date [158]. Peter Kivy and Eduard Hanslick are adjacent formalist or cognitivist critics; Hanslick’s *On the Musically Beautiful* (1891) rejects making emotional arousal music’s purpose [154]. Daniel E. Berlyne is not discussed in the supplied evidence, so his roster status remains unverified [154].

**Qur’anic application and evidence status**

The direct-use result is **not verified**: the supplied Qur’anic sources discuss affect, aesthetics, recitation, or psychology without explicitly applying the label Arousalism. They therefore provide adjacent or inferential evidence, not proof of exact-label Qur’anic scholarship. One account of Sayyid Qutb describes Qur’anic inimitability as felt before argued—beauty, solemnity, urgency, or peace for readers and listeners—and identifies sound structure, internal rhythm, assonance, repetition, and cognitive, emotional, aesthetic, and moral effects as possible mechanisms [39]. The same source points to lexical, syntactic, semantic, and phonological analysis and invokes rule-governed *qirāʾāt* and *tajwīd* transmission, but does not provide an Arousalist reading [39].

Rhetorical questions at Q. 6:114 and Q. 18:71 are said to prompt reflection, perplexity, inquiry, or surprise; Q. 78:19–20 links cosmic disruption—an opened sky and moving mountains—to fear; Q. 58:10 presents trust in God as a response to sadness [44]. These are textual-affordance claims, not evidence that every reader or listener responds identically.

## Situationist Constructs: Participation, narrative, and lived encounter

### Definition, origin, and primary texts

**The standard concept is “constructed situation.”** “Situationist Constructs” is best understood here through that term, rather than as the name of a separate canonical theory. In the Situationist International’s 1958 definitions, a constructed situation is a moment of life deliberately organized through a collective ambience and a developing set of events. The emphasis falls on lived experience rather than an autonomous object viewed at a distance. [17]

**Guy Debord supplied the central program.** His June 1957 *Report on the Construction of Situations* proposes the construction of momentary ambiences of life and their transformation into a heightened “passional quality.” Material environments and the behavior they generate are understood as interacting components. The project concerns the reorganization of everyday life, not simply the production of more absorbing artworks. [18]

**The collective formed around that ambition.** The Situationist International was founded in July 1957 at Cosio d’Arroscia, Italy, following earlier collaboration among avant-garde groups. The 1958 journal developed the language of constructed situations, participation, and experimental behavior. The organization dissolved in 1972. Ken Knabb’s anthology documents both the founding context and this later institutional history. [17]

**A situation is more than atmosphere.** The 1958 “Preliminary Problems in Constructing a Situation” expressly rejects limiting the concept to the integrated use of artistic means to create an ambience. It defines the situation as an ensemble of behavior through time: actions arise from a temporary environment and, in turn, generate further environments and actions. [19]

**Participation distinguishes it from spectacle.** Debord and the Situationists opposed the nonintervention associated with spectatorship. Their proposed participants were not simply actors playing assigned characters or audiences identifying with a hero; they were “livers,” people living and helping construct the situation. The public’s passive role was to diminish as participants’ practical role increased. [19]

**Its practices organized different relationships.** Three related terms show why the concept should not be reduced to unusual sequencing or sensory immersion.

| Practice | Basic meaning | Participant’s activity | Connection to situations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dérive | Experimental passage through varied ambiences | Following terrain, encounters, and attractions | Reveals and changes habitual experience |
| Psychogeography | Study of environments’ effects on feeling and behavior | Attending to emotional qualities of places | Supplies knowledge for constructing experiences |
| Unitary urbanism | Combining arts and techniques in a dynamic milieu | Living within changing spatial arrangements | Extends aesthetic construction into everyday life |

**Dérive provides a useful concrete example.** Brian Elliott’s 2009 study explains Debord’s *dérive* as “playful-constructive” movement through varied urban ambiences, departing from ordinary work, leisure, and transport purposes. Participants attend to terrain and encounters, developing an understanding of places’ psychological and emotional traits. Elliott also describes Debord’s collaboration with Constant Nieuwenhuys on unitary urbanism during 1957–60. [20]

**Immersion illustrates only one component.** Giuseppe Pinot-Gallizio’s *Cavern of Anti-Matter* of 1959 covered a Paris gallery’s ceiling, walls, and floor with industrial painting. Constant’s *New Babylon* project, developed during 1956–74, imagined flexible environments for wandering and new sensations. Such examples clarify environmental scope, but the Situationist definition adds an essential criterion: what participants do must help constitute the experience. [21]

### Qur’anic narrative: Participation without a continuous chronicle

**The closest Qur’anic approaches are literary.** Elisabeth Stefanidis’s 2008 “The Qur’an Made Linear” describes the Qur’an as an open text inviting readers to participate in creating meaning through different reading itineraries. Angelika Neuwirth emphasizes communication, dialogue, and the relationship between recitation and an emerging community. These provide documented accounts of textual openness and participation; the Situationist connection is the comparative interpretation developed from them here. [22][23]

**The useful analogy concerns reader positioning.** A continuous chronicle can place an audience outside a completed sequence, observing what happened next. Qur’anic allusion, questions, compressed scenes, recurring narratives, and changing communicative positions can require a different activity: remembering, connecting, anticipating, and reconsidering. The audience’s movement through these relationships contributes to the realized experience.

**Three kinds of order differ.** The order of revelation concerns when passages emerged; narrative order concerns how events are presented within a story; reading order concerns how a reader encounters and connects textual units. Calling all three “non-linear” conceals important differences. A chronological reconstruction of revelation can coexist with a literary analysis of narrative gaps, and neither determines how a recited passage feels on a particular occasion.

**Chronological scholarship supplies one contrast.** Stefanidis traces modern Western reconstruction of revelation chronology through Gustav Weil’s 1844 work, Theodor Nöldeke’s *Geschichte des Qorâns* of 1860, and the revision begun by Friedrich Schwally in 1909. Nöldeke’s periodization organized stylistic differences into a developmental sequence. This made chronology an influential explanatory framework, rather than establishing that every Qur’anic narrative itself follows—or rejects—a single chronological pattern. [22]

**Neuwirth changes the organizing relationship.** As Stefanidis explains, Neuwirth’s model presents textual growth as ongoing dialogue: questions receive responses that are themselves questioned and answered again. Here, the important unit is not simply one monologue following another, but a communicative process. This supports an experiential reading because an utterance is understood in relation to the response it addresses or anticipates. [22]

**The alternatives emphasize different relationships.** They are not mutually exclusive descriptions of the same feature.

| Approach | Principal object | Organizing relationship | Reader’s principal task |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chronological reconstruction | Emergence of revelations | Earlier and later passages | Reconstruct historical development |
| Dialogic interpretation | Communicative exchanges | Questions, responses, and reformulations | Identify interlocutors and developing concerns |
| Reader-response interpretation | Encounter with textual form | Gaps, expectations, and revised understanding | Actualize connections invited by the text |
| Situationist analogy | The lived interpretive event | Setting, participation, and changing experience | Examine how engagement alters the encounter |

**The comparison need not abolish history.** A literary account of participation asks how a passage positions its audience, not whether its historical setting should disappear. Neuwirth’s own analysis combines historical questions with close attention to textual communication. The difference from a chronicle is a matter of explanatory emphasis: the event is not merely reported but addressed to someone whose response matters.

### Sūrat Yūsuf: Foreknowledge, gaps, and emotional participation

**Sūrat Yūsuf is a revealing counterexample.** Q. 12 has a sustained plot, recognizable progression, and eventual resolution. It therefore prevents the easy assertion that Qur’anic participation requires fragmentation or chronological disorder. Its temporal shifts, omissions, recurring objects, and distribution of knowledge can solicit active engagement while preserving a coherent story.

**Pandikkad makes participation explicit.** His 2025 article, “Qur’an as Script,” combines Wolfgang Iser’s reader-response theory, Qutb’s artistic depiction, and Aristotelian dramatic concepts. Iser’s “gaps” are places where the text leaves connections or experiences to be imaginatively realized. Pandikkad characterizes this activity as disciplined contemplation, *tadabbur*, rather than unrestricted invention. [11]

| Passage | Narrative device | Reader’s activity | Affective significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q. 12:4 | Joseph’s celestial dream | Anticipating significance beyond present appearances | Hope and dramatic irony |
| Q. 12:15 | Compressed well scene and divine assurance | Imagining distress while tracking promised knowledge | Vulnerability joined to trust |
| Q. 12:18 | Jacob’s “beautiful patience” | Connecting grief, restraint, and faith | Emotion becomes moral endurance |
| Q. 12:99–101 | Dream fulfillment | Reinterpreting the opening through the outcome | Recognition and retrospective coherence |

**The dream distributes knowledge unevenly.** In Q. 12:4, Joseph reports eleven stars, the sun, and the moon prostrating before him. Pandikkad calls this “divine stage direction”: it establishes a clue to the plot’s larger significance. Readers receive an anticipatory perspective that other characters do not share, producing dramatic irony as attempts to defeat Joseph contribute to his eventual rise. [11]

**Foreknowledge changes how misfortune feels.** The dream does not make every subsequent event predictable. It gives the reader a reason to hold apparent disaster and anticipated significance together. Betrayal can thus be experienced simultaneously as immediate harm and as part of a story whose meaning remains unfinished. Participation consists partly in maintaining that tension.

**The well scene requires imaginative completion.** Q. 12:15 compresses the brothers’ action and the assurance given to Joseph instead of providing a long psychological description. Pandikkad reads the compression as an invitation to imagine anguish and faith. The reader performs connective work between physical vulnerability, abandonment, and divine support. [11]

**Concision offers a competing explanation.** A narratological study of distance in Sūrat Yūsuf emphasizes the narrator’s consistently abridged presentation, favoring mentioning over visually detailed showing. This is compatible with participatory interpretation, but it changes the mechanism: involvement may arise because sensory detail is withheld, rather than because the text supplies an exhaustive immersive scene. [24]

**The distinction matters for “immersion.”** An immersive reading need not resemble a fully furnished virtual environment. A compressed phrase may absorb a reader through memory, inference, or emotional recognition. The Situationist comparison is therefore strongest when “environment” describes an evolving arrangement of attention and participation, not an assumption that every scene is visually complete.

**Jacob’s response directs emotional practice.** In Q. 12:18, *ṣabrun jamīl*, “beautiful patience,” condenses grief and endurance into a brief expression. Pandikkad treats it as a prompt to imagine a disciplined response to suffering. Readers are invited to understand patience through an emotionally charged situation, rather than receive an abstract definition of patience. [11]

**Narrative objects sustain interpretive activity.** Pandikkad follows the shirt as it changes evidentiary significance across the story: an apparent sign of Joseph’s death becomes, in another episode, an object through which innocence is established. Recurring objects encourage readers to compare scenes and reassess what visible signs mean. The resulting participation is interpretive and ethical, not simply an increase in sensory intensity.

### Temporal patterning: More than a choice between linearity and disorder

**Ring composition proposes another organizing principle.** Jawad Anwar Qureshi’s 2017 study argues that the 111 verses of Q. 12 form an overarching concentric arrangement, represented in his detailed outline as ABC/D/C′B′A′. Corresponding sections frame a substantial Egyptian center, while narrative developments undo earlier misfortunes in reverse order. [25]

| Unit | Verses | Narrative function | Correspondence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Q. 12:1–3 | Preface | A′: concluding reflection |
| B | Q. 12:4–6 | Joseph’s dream | B′: dream fulfilled |
| C | Q. 12:7–18 | Separation | C′: reunion |
| D | Q. 12:19–57 | Joseph in Egypt | Central narrative development |
| C′ | Q. 12:58–98 | Reunion | C: separation |
| B′ | Q. 12:99–101 | Dream fulfillment | B: opening dream |
| A′ | Q. 12:102–111 | Conclusion | A: preface |

**Correspondence activates memory across time.** Qureshi explains that balanced relations give listeners something to “latch on to.” The audience follows what happens next while also recognizing what the present recalls or reverses. A narrative can consequently be chronological in its broad movement and non-linear in the relationships through which it acquires significance. [25]

**Temporal leaps can preserve continuity.** Maramia, Ghasem Azizi Morad, and Mina Nikjoo’s 2022 narrative-time study emphasizes disruption of linear sequence, retrospection, and compression in Joseph’s story. Anwar Abd al-Karim al-Sayid’s 2025 literary reading instead stresses smooth, logical transitions and describes the arrangement as resembling a cinematic scenario. These accounts direct attention to different scales: temporal omission and reordering need not destroy the larger continuity of a plot. [26][27]

**Structural patterns require compelling correspondences.** Qureshi discusses Nicolai Sinai’s criticism that ring analyses can become arbitrary or selectively supported. He consequently emphasizes specific textual markers rather than resemblance alone. This matters to the present comparison: a participatory reading gains explanatory force from identifiable connections, not from calling every recurrence a constructed situation.

**Q. 101 creates a different temporality.** Sells argues that the opening questions and the ambiguous placement of *yawma*, “a day” or “on a day,” defer closure and allow judgment to be conceived beyond a single distant future moment. His reading presents the passage as an event becoming immediate to the hearer. This is experiential temporal disruption, rather than the rearrangement of events in a continuous historical story. [3]

**That immediacy changes the audience’s position.** Judgment is no longer encountered only as information about what will happen later. The transformation of familiar reality becomes imaginatively present. A Situationist analogy highlights this shift from observing a description to inhabiting a consequential moment, while the underlying Qur’anic analysis remains Sells’s account of language, sound, and temporality.

### Where the Situationist comparison helps—and where it changes

**Actual recitation strengthens the environmental analogy.** Frishkopf describes Egyptian listening sessions in which audience exclamations encourage the reciter toward greater expressive intensity. Here, participants’ behavior affects the event as it unfolds. This closely resembles the Situationist relationship between an environment that shapes action and actions that reshape the environment, although the religious setting has its own purposes and rules. [9]

**Occasion also reorganizes textual experience.** Osborne’s discussion of Q. 30:21 at weddings shows how a passage acquires an experiential setting through repeated communal use. The verse’s reference to spouses becomes foregrounded within a marriage ceremony, while the sūrah’s larger historical and cosmic concerns remain part of its broader context. The environment does not rewrite the verse; it changes which relationships are most immediately felt. [1]

**Nevertheless, identification creates a real difference.** The Situationist program seeks to break spectators’ identification with a hero and draw them into practical activity. Pandikkad’s reading of Joseph, by contrast, often depends on sympathy and emotional identification with characters. These are not equivalent mechanisms. The comparison concerns active engagement, but the respective theories explain that engagement differently.

**Scriptural authority creates another difference.** Situationist participants aspire to construct their own lived conditions. In Pandikkad’s Qur’anic model, readers imaginatively realize a text within divine guidance; they do not collectively author its canonical wording or determine its authority. “Participation” therefore names an activity under different conditions in the two settings.

**The original politics resist easy transfer.** Debord’s report supports a noncontinuous conception of life and rejects the pursuit of artistic or personal eternity. The 1958 text even presents the construction of situations as superseding religion. A Qur’anic encounter oriented toward enduring revelation and final judgment cannot simply be identified with that revolutionary project. [17]

**Participation itself is not automatically liberating.** Gavin Grindon’s 2016 critique argues that Situationist accounts of agency contain coercive possibilities, including the ambition to “make people live.” The primary 1958 text also envisages pushing passive spectators into action. For Qur’anic interpretation, this raises a useful question about who directs an encounter and what kind of agency listeners exercise; it does not establish that religious participation and Situationist coercion are the same. [28]

**The resulting judgment is selective.** The analogy is strongest where textual form solicits active connections or where communal reception visibly reshapes a performance. It is weaker when “constructed situation” merely means that a text is vivid, repetitive, or nonchronological. Coherent linear narration, contemplative silence, and emotional identification can also sustain active reception.

**The Situationist lens’s practical gain:** it directs attention to how readers and listeners help realize an encounter, while keeping that participation distinct from collectively constructing revelation itself.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Terminology and definition**

**Supported result.** Here “Situationist Constructs” is used as shorthand for the primary terms *situation*, *constructed situation*, and *construction of situations*. A constructed situation is “a moment of life concretely and deliberately constructed by the collective organization of a unitary ambiance and a game of events”; it deliberately creates an objective context in which subjects can act freely, rather than producing another autonomous artwork. The definition was not exhaustive: in 1960 Debord called “situation,” though central, the group’s “least clear” concept. [203]

**Origin and primary texts**

Guy Debord is the primary theorist, but the genealogy is collective. The term appears in his first Lettrist *Ion* texts (1952), including *Howling in Favour of Sade* and *Prolégomènes*, which proposed a “science of situations” combining psychology, statistics, urbanism, and ethics. A 1953 manifesto project was abandoned; *dérive* and psychogeography became early Lettrist International experiments, alongside *détournement* during 1952–57. Debord and Wolman founded the Lettrist International in June 1952; at Cosio d’Arroscia in 1957 Debord called “the hypothesis of constructions of situations” the movement’s main insight; the Situationist International was established in July 1957. [203]

The SI’s first phase was 1957–62; later it developed revolutionary social theory, and a third phase beginning in 1968 sought realization in street-level struggle. Primary anchors are Debord’s *Report on the Construction of Situations* (1957), the SI’s *Manifeste* (June 1960) and *The Use of Free Time* (1960), Debord’s *Perspectives for Conscious Changes in Everyday Life* (1961), *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967), and “The Organization Question for the SI” (April 1968). [203]

**Mechanism and application boundary**

Situationist Constructs join spatial, temporal, social, and sensory organization. Against cultural “decomposition” and socially arrested experience, the SI sought to use all arts and modern techniques to overcome the split between artistic play and finished artefacts, dissolve culture into life, re-appropriate reality, and replace standardized experience with movement, change, and enriched lived time. *Dérive* means drifting or aimless walking that reads the urban landscape and treats space as a site of self-construction; *détournement* alters and recontextualizes meanings. “Unitary urbanism” provides experimental spatial practice; the wider aim is collective self-management of everyday life and self-determination of lived time. [203]

The affective mechanism is specific: *milieu* includes atmosphere, lighting, signs, sounds, social dynamics, and nourishment; for the SI it is urban, aesthetic, mutable, and technological. Psychogeography studies a milieu’s effects on emotion and behaviour through drifting, theoretical elaboration, and counter-mapping; ambiance is a sensory, qualitative whole resistant to simple representation. Audiences are not merely spectators but active elements or collective agents. Debord’s spectacle supplies the negative counterpart—passive contemplation and “spectator consciousness”—while *détournement* rewrites inherited material to extract new meaning. [203]

## Theater of Cruelty: Apocalyptic imagery and visceral address

### Definition, origin, and Artaud’s meaning of cruelty

**Artaud sought a theater addressing bodies.** His Theater of Cruelty uses voice, rhythm, gesture, image, sound, and spatial arrangement to disturb habitual perception. It challenges theater organized principally around scripted dialogue, an orderly plot, and psychologically intelligible characters. “Cruelty” names the rigor of this encounter, not simply the presence of violent subject matter. [29][30]

**His own definition rejects a shortcut.** In a letter dated 13 September 1932, Artaud explains cruelty through rigor, implacable intention, and irreversible determination, explicitly rejecting an exclusively sadistic or bloodthirsty meaning. Calling a Qur’anic passage “Artaudian” merely because it depicts pain would therefore miss the theory’s central claim. The relevant question is how a configuration of form and performance acts upon sensibility. [29]

**The theory developed through theatrical experimentation.** Artaud participated in Parisian surrealist and experimental circles during the 1920s, later founding the Théâtre Alfred Jarry with Roger Vitrac and Robert Aron. Scholarly accounts trace its experiments during 1926–31 into his early-1930s formulations. Those ideas appear in *Le Théâtre de la cruauté* associated with 1933 and in *Le Théâtre et son double*, published in 1938; the English *The Theater and Its Double* followed in 1958. [31][32]

**Voice is more than spoken information.** In the First Manifesto, Artaud proposes extending the voice and using its vibrations, rhythms, and qualities to sharpen perception. Words can function as incantations rather than merely convey dialogue. The intention is not simply to remove language, but to release possibilities that ordinary speech conventions leave unused. [29]

**Space changes the audience’s relationship.** Artaud proposes replacing the separated stage and auditorium with a single site, placing spectators within the action so that it engulfs and physically affects them. The audience loses the assurance of occupying a protected viewing position outside what happens. Spatial organization is thus an active element of the theory, not scenery added to an otherwise complete script. [29]

**Production history makes the sonic ambition concrete.** Adrian Curtin’s 2011 analysis of Artaud’s 1935 *Les Cenci* examines sound design intended to affect audiences physiologically through new sonic technologies. Curtin describes a surround-sound aspect and an aesthetic arguably influenced in part by Balinese gamelan. This is a historical account of theatrical means and intentions, rather than a general equation between loudness and transformation. [33]

### Qur’anic applications: What the comparison explains

**The closest Qur’anic studies examine sensory force.** Sells analyzes apocalyptic transformation through sound and language; Kathryn Kueny’s “Tasting Fire” examines punishment through embodied taste; a 2008 article in *The Arabist* emphasizes the vividness of Paradise, Hell, and Judgment scenes. These are the documented Qur’anic arguments supporting the controlled Artaudian comparison below. [3][34][35]

**The most useful comparison concerns positioning.** Qur’anic apocalypse can remove an audience’s imagined security through cosmic disruption, the opening of personal records, direct address, and images of heat or ingestion. The listener is not invited merely to inspect an extraordinary landscape. The landscape becomes relevant to the listener’s body, conduct, and eventual accountability.

### Q. 81:1–14: Cosmic upheaval ends in personal recognition

**The passage destabilizes familiar reality immediately.** Q. 81:1–3 sequences the sun being put out, the stars falling, and the mountains being blown away in Quran.com’s displayed translation. The images successively undo features that normally orient vision, distance, and solidity. The effect is not simply greater scale; it is the failure of the stable setting within which ordinary life makes sense. [36]

**Accumulation prevents an easy resting point.** The repeated “when” construction carries the audience through successive scenes before the passage reaches its principal concluding statement. Each image opens another condition rather than supplying a complete explanatory pause. In this report’s Artaudian comparison, the listener is drawn through an unfolding disturbance instead of being offered a single disaster to contemplate at leisure.

**Ordinary care and boundaries also collapse.** Q. 81:4–6 depicts pregnant camels left untended, wild beasts gathered, and seas set on fire. These scenes move from interrupted human care through altered animal relations to the transformation of water into a site of burning. Their cumulative force comes from the reversal of ordinary expectations across different domains of experience. [36]

**That reversal resembles environmental engulfment.** Artaud’s spectator is affected from within a transformed spatial arrangement. The Qur’anic listener imagines a world in which no familiar exterior remains unchanged: sky, land, animals, water, and human arrangements all enter the upheaval. The resemblance concerns the loss of an untouched observational refuge, not a historical connection between the scripture and Artaud.

**The ethical question interrupts pure spectacle.** Q. 81:8–9 asks the girl buried alive for what crime she was killed. This passage changes the meaning of the surrounding intensity. Cosmic disruption is joined to the exposure of an injustice and to a question that reveals the victim’s innocence. [36]

**This is more than sensory assault.** If the sequence were read only as an accumulation of shocking images, the accusatory function of the question would disappear. Its power depends on intelligibility: the audience understands the wrong being exposed. Affective intensity here carries moral discrimination rather than bypassing it.

**Personal and cosmic exposure converge.** Q. 81:10–11 places opened records of deeds beside a stripped-away sky. Read comparatively, the removal of the cosmic covering and the disclosure of human action reinforce one another. What seemed concealed or protected is brought into view at both scales. [36]

**Hell is not the only endpoint.** Q. 81:12–13 places the fiercely flared Hellfire beside Paradise brought near. Fear operates within a contrasting field of judgment and possible destination. Omitting Paradise would turn a structured moral contrast into an undifferentiated sequence of horror. [36]

**The conclusion is an act of knowing.** Q. 81:14 ends with each soul knowing what deeds it has brought. The repeated conditions culminate not in permanent perceptual confusion but in unavoidable recognition. This is the decisive adjustment to an Artaudian reading: the disturbance is organized toward accountability and understanding. [36]

**The resulting judgment is specific.** Artaud helps illuminate the passage’s removal of distance and protection. Qur’anic rhetoric explains the destination of that process more precisely: an audience is made to imagine the exposure of action, the vindication of victims, and the self’s knowledge of its own moral history.

### Q. 101:9–11: The disturbance of falling, “mother,” and fire

**The ending unsettles more than vision.** Q. 101:9–11 connects *ummuhu hāwiya* with the question of what *hiya* is and the final phrase *nārun ḥāmiya*, which Sells renders “raging fire.” Common eschatological interpretations understand the sequence in terms of Hell as an abode or destination. Sells examines the additional possibilities generated by the maternal term, grammatical gender, and the shifting sense of *hāwiya*. [3]

**The maternal association changes emotional register.** Sells discusses interpretations involving a bereaved mother alongside those involving an abyss or falling. The following question turns the word toward an unsettling identification with fire. In this reading, familiar associations of origin, refuge, and intimacy are disturbed from within, rather than replaced by an entirely unfamiliar monster or landscape.

**Language performs part of the disturbance.** Sells describes the transition as jarring the hearer and figuratively opening the word into further associations. Its acoustic relations also recall earlier parts of the sūrah. The ending’s force consequently arises through the convergence of semantic uncertainty, remembered sounds, and a compressed final declaration. [3]

**An Artaudian comparison highlights that convergence.** Artaud wants language to recover physical and perceptual force beyond ordinary explanatory dialogue. In Q. 101, the audience’s effort to understand a troubling expression becomes part of the encounter: the wording does not remain a transparent label placed over a fully settled scene. The analogy is strongest at this point of active linguistic disturbance.

**Sells preserves a significant interpretive alternative.** He emphasizes that the final phrase contains indefinite “fire,” rather than simply treating every term as a synonym for a fixed topographical region of Hell. He also argues that the temporal possibilities exceed a straightforward future description. These are features of his literary interpretation, which preserves eschatological resonance while resisting its reduction to a single paraphrase. [3]

**His emotional conclusion complicates “cruelty.”** Sells’s analysis moves from proclamatory awe toward intimacy, sadness, and the realization of unavoidable loss. This matters because a purely shocking or terrifying reading would suppress part of the very affective complexity it seeks to explain. The passage can disturb through grief and recognition as well as fear. [3]

**The passage therefore tests the analogy.** “Theater of Cruelty” is illuminating if it directs attention to a difficult, embodied encounter with language. It is reductive if it makes raging fire the explanation of everything else, overlooking the passage’s acoustic tenderness, maternal associations, or changing emotional register.

### Q. 56:41–56 and Q. 78:21–30: Relief becomes bodily threat

**Q. 56 constructs an inhospitable environment.** The companions of the left are placed amid scorching wind, boiling water, and the shadow of dark smoke, described as neither cooling nor beneficial. This descriptive paraphrase of Q. 56:41–44 presents more than a collection of painful objects. It forms a surrounding environment in which ordinary expectations of air, water, and shade are reversed.

**Shade becomes a frustrated promise.** The passage introduces something normally associated with protection from heat, then withdraws its expected quality. The listener imaginatively reaches toward relief only to encounter its negation. In the Artaudian comparison proposed here, the force comes from the repeated loss of bodily refuge.

**Food and drink extend that reversal.** Q. 56:51–55 addresses the erring deniers, describes eating from the tree of zaqqūm, filling the belly, and drinking boiling water like thirsty camels. The sequence moves inward: surroundings become ingestion. The imagined body is affected not only from outside by heat, but through the acts by which it ordinarily preserves itself.

**The final hospitality is grimly inverted.** Q. 56:56 identifies this as their reception on the Day of Judgment. Read as a sensory construction, the passage reworks a recognizable cluster—shelter, food, drink, reception—into an experience of denied sustenance. Its visceral force depends on the audience understanding what these things ordinarily offer.

**Ethical causation remains in the passage.** Q. 56:45–48 connects the scene with prior indulgence, persistent wrongdoing, and denial of resurrection. The bodily description is therefore embedded in a moral and theological argument. An aesthetic account gains explanatory value by showing how that argument is made imaginable, not by declaring the imagery independent of the conduct it addresses.

**Q. 78 makes denied relief explicit.** Q. 78:21–25 describes Hell as an ambush or place awaiting transgressors, followed by prolonged residence without cooling relief or ordinary drink, except boiling water and *ghassāq*. The sensory arrangement combines confinement, duration, thirst, and the failure of refreshment. This is a passage-level comparison offered here from the Qur’anic wording.

**The audience encounters a closed sequence.** In this proposed Artaudian reading, the important feature is the relationship among the details. Remaining in the place intensifies the desire for relief; the promised categories of coolness and drink are then negated or transformed. The environment becomes difficult to imagine from a position of bodily indifference.

**Accounting gives the sequence its purpose.** Q. 78:26–29 calls the punishment a fitting recompense, relates it to rejection of accountability and denial of the signs, and invokes the recording of everything. The passage’s bodily force and its concern with records belong together. It addresses the belief that conduct might escape reckoning.

**The command to taste closes distance.** Q. 78:30 turns punishment into a direct imperative to taste. A viewer can imagine remaining outside an image, but taste requires contact and incorporation: the thing experienced enters the mouth and body. That is why the metaphor offers a particularly powerful way to translate an abstract consequence into embodied address.

**The shared mechanism is reversed sustenance.** Q. 56 develops an environment and sequence of ingestion; Q. 78 emphasizes confinement, denied relief, and direct reckoning. Both can be compared with Artaud’s effort to unsettle protected spectatorship. Their Qur’anic organization, however, makes the disturbance answerable to prior conduct and final judgment.

### Taste, horror, and the possibility of moral change

**Kueny directly theorizes embodied punishment.** In “Tasting Fire,” Kathryn Kueny argues that several Qur’anic passages make divine punishment experiential through taste. Her abstract contrasts taste in earthly and paradisiacal settings with its especially forceful function in punishment. She describes abhorrent flavors pressed onto the tongue and down the throat, connecting horror with the cultivation of a more receptive character. [34]

**Taste offers a distinctive affective mechanism.** Seeing a threatening object permits an imagined separation between observer and object. Taste makes that separation harder to sustain because experience requires bodily contact. Kueny’s account consequently supplies a more precise explanation than the general claim that hell imagery is “graphic”: the imagery organizes an intimate, invasive relationship between sensation and moral knowledge.

**Q. 54:39 provides a concise anchor.** “Taste My punishment and My warnings” joins sensation directly to warning. Thomas Hoffmann’s 2019 study takes this verse as its title and examines painful metaphors of taste in the Qur’an. The bibliographic connection identifies an established sensory-punishment discussion, while Kueny’s argument supplies the explicit account of taste, horror, and receptivity used here. [34]

**The desired endpoint exceeds punishment alone.** Kueny frames the movement toward righteousness through the possibility of “tasting mercy.” In that account, horror is not an autonomous aesthetic achievement. Its significance lies in a possible transformation of receptivity and character, which connects it with the ethical work described by Bauer and Hamza.

**Emotional practice gives transformation content.** Bauer and Hamza distinguish obedience and endurance, repentance after disobedience, and continued disobedience. Their analysis links narrative outcome with how emotions are practiced and disciplined. This makes an important distinction: feeling strongly is not identical to responding well. [2]

**Shock therefore has no automatic moral value.** An intense passage might prompt recognition, fear, resistance, grief, or renewed attention. The textual argument concerns which responses are directed toward repentance, endurance, or acknowledgment of accountability. Artaud’s vocabulary helps identify the disturbance, but Qur’anic moral analysis helps distinguish its possible meanings.

### The comparison’s decisive theological tension

**Artaud and the Qur’an diverge sharply.** Artaud’s ambition to unsettle inherited cultural authority is not equivalent to a scripture’s call to recognize divine authority. His resistance to text-bound theater also differs from recitation in which the exact verbal form is constitutive of the event. A resemblance in sensory means should not erase an opposition in purpose.

**His apocalyptic language can be anti-theological.** A 2003 interpretation of Artaud’s radio work *To Have Done with the Judgment of God* describes an “apocalypse of the apocalypse”: an attack on a theological conception of salvation history rather than a prediction of the world’s end. Qur’anic apocalypse, by contrast, brings divine judgment and the recognition of deeds into the foreground. [37]

**The same mechanism can serve opposing ends.** Both projects may unsettle an audience’s ordinary relation to language and bodily security. Yet one can challenge the authority of judgment while the other makes judgment unavoidable. The productive comparison concerns how attention and perception are reorganized; it does not establish a shared theology or philosophy of liberation.

**Reception also exceeds artistic intention.** Artaud’s *Les Cenci* closed after seventeen performances in 1935, according to the Poetry Foundation’s biographical account. That history cautions against treating theoretical ambition as achieved transformation. Similarly, a Qur’anic passage’s formal capacity for disturbance and a particular listener’s response are different objects of analysis. [31]

**Ordinary listening complicates the shock model.** Osborne’s reciter interviews describe contextual selection, including preferences for passages of mercy and encouragement at some public gatherings. A scripture that contains terrifying imagery can also be encountered through reassurance, familiarity, and remembered voices. The Theater of Cruelty is therefore a focused lens for particular passages, rather than a sufficient description of Qur’anic reception as a whole. [1]

**The strongest judgment preserves both sides.** Artaud illuminates how apocalyptic rhetoric can remove distance and make consequences bodily imaginable. The comparison loses force when every violent image is labeled cruelty, when disturbance is assumed to be beneficial, or when sensory intensity replaces the passage’s ethical and theological organization.

**The Theater of Cruelty’s practical gain:** it reveals how judgment can become an engulfing sensory encounter, while the Qur’anic passages show that such intensity culminates in accountability, recognition, and differentiated emotional responses.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Definition and the meaning of “cruelty”**

**The Theater of Cruelty (*Théâtre de la Cruauté*) is Antonin Artaud’s avant-garde proposal for an immediate, bodily theatre, not a synonym for violent subject matter.** It rejects theatre organized chiefly by written text, logical stories, and safe spectator distance. Instead, it seeks to collapse divisions between art and life, actor and spectator, and stage and audience, making performance an affective and physiological event rather than an object of detached interpretation. [298][179]

For Artaud, “cruelty” means spiritual and philosophical rigor: the disruption of complacency, false reality, fixed individuality, discursive reason, and protected spectatorship. It may involve discipline, shock, and exposure to danger, but it is not ordinary physical or emotional violence, sadism, or simply causing pain. Blood may appear “when necessary,” but not as the theory’s defining requirement. The decisive test is therefore formal and relational: disturbing content alone does not constitute a Theater-of-Cruelty reading. [297][179][298]

**Origin, chronology, and primary texts**

**Formal mechanism and audience**

**Artaud’s proposed mechanism is multisensory and non-discursive.** The mise-en-scène combines the human voice, machinic noise, visible objects, sound, light, verbal utterance, rhythm, timbre, intonation, gesture, screams, music, colored lights, images, ritual-like upheaval, and spatial volume. The intended audience is the **theatrical spectator**, not automatically a Qur’anic reader or listener. Artaud imagined abolishing the conventional stage–auditorium division, placing spectators at the centre while performers act around them in a shifting “vortex” that could leave them trapped, exposed, and physically affected. More broadly, the theory transgresses actor–spectator–stage boundaries and makes the audience an experiential part of the event. Theater of Cruelty’s distinct contribution is to make the Qur’an’s affective dimension an embodied event rather than only a message: its framework uses signs, words, sound, rhythm, gesture, imagery, and space to attack the spectator’s sensibility and dissolve stage–audience separation [328][338]. Its limitation is equally important—the intended relation can be one-directional and coercive, and the full theatre was never realized beyond the manifestos—so it is a risky comparative lens, not evidence that Qur’anic reception is controlled or universally visceral [316][342].

Artaud’s role in the collective perspective is to supply its Western avant-garde comparator: his theory rejects speech and text as the sole basis of theatre and seeks a whole-body sensorium through gesture, sound, ritual, and spectacle to provoke transformation [337][313]. Because later artists adopted and interpreted his ideas divergently, his role is a comparative heuristic for Qur’anic affect, not evidence that the Qur’an derives from Artaud or that all audiences respond alike [304].

Qur’anic apocalyptic and hell imagery grounds the collective perspective in a concrete visceral corpus: vivid fire, pain, despair, bodily agony, direct address, and imperatives make unseen judgment perceptible and place moral intensity alongside embodied response [61][94][280]. Q 85:1-9 and the wider hell corpus provide specific cases, but the distinction between sensory description, theological doctrine, and audience effect requires treating the Artaudian reading as an interpretive comparison rather than a settled fact [293][94].

## Conclusion: Three perspectives, one expanded account of understanding

**The collective contribution is substantial.** These theories redirect attention from scripture as a container of propositions toward scripture as an encounter enacted through sound, time, imagination, and bodily orientation. They illuminate different parts of that encounter: Arousalism foregrounds feeling, Situationist constructs foreground participation, and the Theater of Cruelty foregrounds sensory disturbance. Their usefulness depends on preserving those differences.

| Shared question | Arousalism | Situationist Constructs | Theater of Cruelty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intellectual starting point | Artistic expressiveness and audience response | Revolutionary construction of everyday life | Experimental, multisensory theater |
| Principal audience role | Receptive perceiver | Active participant and constructor | Spectator placed within disturbing action |
| Main mechanism | Form’s tendency to evoke emotion | Interaction of environment and behavior | Voice, rhythm, image, and spatial engulfment |
| Strongest Qur’anic connection | Recitation, phonology, and emotional reception | Narrative gaps, temporal relations, communal performance | Apocalypse, denied relief, fire, and taste |
| Central interpretive adjustment | Religious emotions often have objects | Participation does not mean authoring revelation | Sensory disturbance remains directed toward judgment |

**Their mechanisms are complementary, not sequential.** A listener need not first undergo arousal, then enter a situation, then receive an Artaudian shock. In Q. 101, sound, unanswered questions, temporal openness, imagery, and emotional transformation operate together. Each framework selects a different relationship within that integrated event.

**The main gain is explanatory specificity.** Instead of saying that a passage is moving, the analysis can identify how repetition sustains expectation, how long syllables prolong attention, how narrative gaps solicit imaginative work, and how taste turns a consequence into an intimate bodily prospect. These are more informative claims because they connect a particular formal feature with a particular mode of engagement.

**The main risk is conceptual substitution.** “Arousal” can flatten grief, recognition, tranquility, and fear into undifferentiated activation. “Participation” can obscure the difference between interpreting a sacred text and constructing its authority. “Cruelty” can make moral judgment look like sensory manipulation or imply that intensity explains a passage’s entire significance.

**Islamic aesthetic resources remain indispensable.** Tajwīd, rhetorical organization, artistic depiction, contemplation, and cultivated emotional practice provide their own accounts of how revelation is heard and taken to heart. Sells’s contextual sound analysis, Moqbel’s aural aesthetics, and Pandikkad’s discussion of Qutb demonstrate that Western concepts can enter a conversation with these resources rather than supply the first recognition of Qur’anic aesthetic power. [3][8][11]

**The governing alternative is therefore additive.** Affective interpretation does not require choosing how scripture feels over what it means. Bauer and Hamza’s account offers the more consequential formulation: feeling properly can be part of understanding, because emotions, judgment, and conduct are connected. The question becomes how a passage’s form helps make its address perceptible, memorable, and ethically demanding. [2]

**The evidence has distinct measurement bases.** The theoretical definitions are directly sourced; the Qur’anic connections combine published aural, literary, reader-response, and affective analyses with this report’s explicitly identified comparisons. Direct adoption of a named framework, or a unified three-framework account, requires an explicit scholarly argument linking theory and passages. Phonological claims concern Arabic and specified textual or recitational forms; corpus percentages are calculated classifications, not response rates. Interview accounts and EEG findings concern their stated participants and tasks, with the health review reporting substantial weaknesses in methodological reporting; no universal-response or causal-effect estimates are added.

**The final judgment is clear.** The three theories are most valuable as disciplined questions, not replacement labels: What does form make possible to feel? What must an audience actively do? How does language make judgment bodily present? Together, they offer a richer account of the Qur’an as something understood through hearing, imagining, responding, and living—not only through extracting statements from a page.
