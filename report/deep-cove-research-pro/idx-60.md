# Prediction, Uncertainty and Anxiety in Autistic Students: A Guide to School Support Through June 2023

**The central finding:** For some autistic students, sensory experiences that are difficult to filter or anticipate can make school feel repeatedly surprising. Intolerance of uncertainty (IU)—distress about ambiguity or not knowing what will happen—may turn that unpredictability into anxiety, while anxiety increases vigilance and makes sensory demands harder to manage. This is a useful explanatory model, rather than a settled explanation of every autistic student’s anxiety. [1]

**The practical answer:** Schools should combine three kinds of support: managing sensory input, making events and expectations explicit so students have less to predict, and gradually teaching students to anticipate, update expectations and cope with change. Teaching the hidden curriculum, incorporating student-chosen special interests, supporting peer understanding and providing adapted anxiety treatment can complement these approaches.

**How to use this report:** The report first explains the theory and its evidence, then develops each strategy category with concrete school applications and mechanisms. It finishes with related supports and guidance for judging outcomes. The evidence boundary is **30 June 2023**, with school studies distinguished from parent-mediated, clinical, preschool and adult evidence.

## A quick starting point for teachers

**Match support to the trigger:** The same outward response—leaving, refusing, becoming quiet or becoming distressed—can arise from different problems. Start with the student’s account, accessible communication and observations of what happens before and after the difficulty. The table offers starting hypotheses, not diagnoses.

| Observed pattern | First school support to consider | Intended mechanism | Outcome to monitor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Difficulty following speech in noise | Remote microphone, quieter position or selected hearing protection | Reduce competing sound and listening uncertainty | Understanding, comfort and participation |
| Discomfort around lighting or displays | Adjust lighting and remove irrelevant visual clutter | Make relevant information easier to select | Comfort, attention and learning |
| Repeated questions about what happens next | Comprehensible schedule, finished marker and timer | Make sequence and duration explicit | Independent transitions and reassurance needs |
| Distress when a familiar routine changes | Change cue, preview and a rehearsed alternative | Support updating rather than an abrupt mismatch | Recovery, help-seeking and participation |
| Confusion during group work or playground interaction | Explicit roles, hidden-curriculum teaching and peer support | Clarify otherwise implicit social expectations | Understanding, inclusion and chosen interaction |
| Low engagement or need for familiar calming activity | Student-chosen interest materials or planned interest access | Increase meaning, motivation and perceived control | Engagement, autonomy and wellbeing |

**Different explanations require different responses:** A student may understand exactly when lunch starts yet still find the dining hall painfully loud. Another may tolerate its noise but become anxious because seating and social expectations are unclear. Predictability supports and sensory adjustments address different parts of those situations. [2][3]

## The theory: how prediction, sensory attention, IU and anxiety interact

### Prediction means anticipating events and using context

**Prediction supports ordinary school life:** Here, prediction means using previous experience and current cues to anticipate what is likely to happen. It includes expecting what follows a classroom instruction, recognizing a transition cue, anticipating the end of an activity and judging how a peer might respond. It is broader than consciously answering “What happens next?”

**Predictive processing combines two information sources:** In predictive-processing theories, the brain combines expectations based on experience—often called *prior expectations* or *priors*—with incoming sensory information. Expectations operate from the “top down,” while sounds, sights, touch and other signals arrive from the “bottom up.” A mismatch between expectation and input produces a *prediction error*: information that something differs from what was anticipated. [4]

**Prediction errors are normal learning signals:** An error is not a mistake by the student. If a familiar teacher is replaced by a substitute, the difference provides information that expectations need updating. The important questions are how strongly that difference captures attention, whether it signals a meaningful change and what the student learns from it.

**School prediction involves several operations:** A student must notice a useful cue, connect it with an outcome, distinguish reliable patterns from incidental details and apply learning when circumstances vary. Difficulty at any one of those points can make a particular school situation demanding. This does not imply an inability to recognize patterns or form expectations generally; some predictive tasks show comparable performance between autistic and non-autistic participants. [5]

### Sensory attention determines which information becomes prominent

**Sensory attention selects competing information:** Attention helps a student select a teacher’s instruction from conversation, chair scraping, corridor movement and classroom displays. Selection is influenced both by the student’s goals and expectations and by the physical properties of stimuli, such as suddenness or intensity. The classroom literature describes difficulties for some autistic students in filtering irrelevant auditory and visual information and selecting one sound source among competing sources. [2]

**Unexpected input can capture attention:** A sudden noise may draw attention automatically, even when the student is trying to listen. Predictive-coding accounts describe prediction error or “surprise” as a potential trigger for this externally driven attention. If many small changes remain highly salient, the student may repeatedly have to reorient rather than allowing familiar background information to recede. [4]

**Missed cues can compound unpredictability:** Consider a hypothetical classroom example: a student attends to an unexpected scraping sound and misses the teacher’s announcement that the class is moving. The transition then appears abrupt. The resulting difficulty need not reflect a failure to understand the routine; access to the cue was interrupted.

**Sensory differences are not interchangeable:** Sensory *hyperreactivity* means a strong response to input; *hyporeactivity* means a reduced or slower response; sensory *seeking* involves pursuing particular experiences. A student can show different patterns across senses and situations. The strongest sensory–IU–anxiety findings discussed here concern hyperreactivity, so “reduce all sensory input” is not an appropriate general prescription. [6]

### Precision concerns how much weight a mismatch receives

**Not every difference deserves equal weight:** In these theories, *precision* means the estimated reliability of information. A highly trusted prediction error has more influence on attention and learning than a discrepancy judged to be random background variation. This is a proposed computational process, not a classroom score that a teacher can directly observe.

**Incidental variation may resemble rule change:** Suppose a teacher usually begins a lesson after placing a particular folder on the desk. The folder’s colour may be irrelevant, while the instruction to begin is reliable. If incidental details receive excessive weight, a different folder could make the whole situation feel changed. Conversely, if useful contextual expectations exert too little influence, the familiar lesson may provide less reassurance than staff expect.

**Autism theories locate the difference differently:** Three overlapping accounts help explain why “prediction difficulty” should not be treated as one established deficit.

| Account | Proposed difference | Expected consequence | School-relevant implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypo-prior account | Prior expectations exert less influence | Context provides less help interpreting input | Make relevant contextual information explicit |
| HIPPEA | Prediction errors receive high, inflexible precision | Incidental differences strongly influence learning | Highlight reliable cues and support generalization |
| Context-sensitive updating accounts | Precision or learning rates adjust differently across situations | Difficulty depends on cue reliability and environmental change | Examine when prediction becomes difficult |

**Weak priors need not mean weak perception:** Pellicano and Burr’s 2012 hypo-prior account proposed that perception might be less constrained by prior experience, potentially supporting accuracy in some tasks while making contextual interpretation harder. A student’s attention to detail can therefore be a strength and a source of additional processing demands, depending on the task. [7]

**HIPPEA emphasizes interpretation rather than detection:** Van de Cruys and colleagues’ 2014 account—*High, Inflexible Precision of Prediction Errors in Autism*—does not propose that autistic people cannot compare predictions with events. Its proposed difficulty concerns deciding how much to learn from each mismatch, particularly when cues compete. Treating noisy exceptions as important may produce very specific expectations that transfer less readily to a changed context. [8]

**Context matters to the judgment:** Accounts emphasizing recalibration ask whether the learner adjusts the influence of new information when a pattern becomes stable or starts changing. Their practical implication differs from assuming uniformly weak prediction: the teacher should examine task complexity, competing cues and the reliability of information. Across these accounts, the educational goal is access to useful information and flexible learning—not “correcting” a presumed neural abnormality. [5]

### IU explains why uncertainty can become threatening

**Uncertainty and IU are different:** Uncertainty is a property of a situation: the student does not know who will supervise lunch or whether outdoor activities will proceed. IU concerns the student’s response to that uncertainty, including negative emotional, cognitive and behavioural reactions. A student can encounter uncertainty without becoming highly anxious, while another may find even a small unknown difficult to tolerate. [1]

**Anxiety adds anticipated threat:** Anxiety involves apprehension about possible danger or difficulty, often accompanied by physiological arousal and heightened monitoring. In an IU account, “I do not know what will happen” can become “Something bad may happen, and I will not manage it.” This appraisal helps explain why repeated questions, avoidance or attempts to preserve sameness may accompany anxiety. [6]

**Real discomfort must remain visible:** An uncertain situation may genuinely contain painful noise, confusing communication, difficult work or unkind peer behaviour. Teaching a student to reinterpret uncertainty does not remove those conditions. A useful formulation distinguishes distress about the unknown from distress about an already known, aversive experience.

### A core model connects the components through feedback

**The integrated model is a synthesis:** South and Rodgers’ sensory-functioning–IU–anxiety model can be combined with predictive-processing accounts to organize the requested components. The resulting loop explains how sensory attention and prediction errors could contribute to perceived unpredictability, and how IU and anxiety could amplify the experience. [1][6]

```text
Sensory input that is difficult to filter or anticipate
                         ↓
Captured sensory attention ↔ salient prediction errors
                         ↓
Greater perceived uncertainty
                         ↓
High IU: uncertainty feels threatening or unmanageable
                         ↓
Anxiety, hypervigilance and avoidance
                         ↺
Further attention to possible threats and fewer opportunities
to learn that some changes are manageable
```

**The first interaction concerns information access:** Competing or aversive sensory input can draw attention away from useful cues. Events then become harder to anticipate, increasing perceived mismatch and surprise. A clearer auditory signal or a visible transition cue can interrupt this part of the loop.

**The second interaction concerns meaning:** Prediction error signals difference, but difference does not automatically mean danger. IU provides a proposed bridge: when not knowing feels unacceptable or unmanageable, uncertain events receive more threatening interpretations. A student may begin monitoring the environment for further signs that the expected routine is breaking down.

**The third interaction concerns maintenance:** Anxiety can increase vigilance, making unusual sounds, movements and social cues harder to ignore. Avoidance may provide immediate relief while reducing opportunities to discover that selected changes can be managed. Nevertheless, leaving a painful sensory environment can be an appropriate protective response; its function must be understood before it is treated as anxiety-maintaining avoidance. [6]

**Sameness can provide useful control:** Familiar routines reduce the number of unknowns and may support regulation. The relevant educational distinction is between preserving necessary accessibility and requiring complete certainty before participation. Predictable supports can remain available while the student learns manageable ways to respond when some details change. [9]

### What the empirical findings support

**IU has a substantial anxiety association:** Jenkinson, Milne and Thompson’s 2020 systematic review included 12 studies with 656 autistic participants. Ten studies, involving 562 participants aged 4–24 years, entered the meta-analysis; the sample-weighted IU–anxiety correlation was **r = 0.62**, with a 95% confidence interval of **0.52–0.71**. The association was comparable in strength to findings from non-autistic populations. [9]

| Evidence source | Population or design | Main finding | Relevant interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jenkinson and colleagues, 2020 | Meta-analysis; 10 studies, 562 participants | IU–anxiety correlation r = 0.62 | Uncertainty tolerance is a plausible support target |
| Neil and colleagues, 2016 | Autistic children; parent-report analyses | Sensory sensitivity, IU and anxiety were interrelated | Sensory and uncertainty needs can coexist |
| Vasa and colleagues, 2018 | 57 autistic and 32 comparison children, ages 7–16 | Higher IU; anxiety findings differed by informant | Student and adult accounts both matter |
| MacLennan and colleagues, 2021 | Autistic preschoolers, ages 3–5 | Both IU-mediated and anxiety-mediated pathways fitted | Reciprocal relationships deserve consideration |
| Green and colleagues, 2012 | Longitudinal autistic toddler study | Sensory hyperreactivity predicted increasing anxiety | Sensory difficulties may precede anxiety development |

The Neil findings are summarized in South and Rodgers’ review [1]; the Vasa findings appear in the school-age study [10]; MacLennan and colleagues discuss both their preschool findings and the earlier [6].

**Reciprocal findings challenge a simple chain:** MacLennan and colleagues found that IU statistically mediated the relationship between sensory hyperreactivity and anxiety, but anxiety also mediated the relationship between hyperreactivity and IU. In plain language, the pattern fitted more than one proposed explanation. It supports attending to all three constructs, rather than assuming that changing just one will necessarily resolve the others. [6]

**Developmental findings support early sensory attention:** The Green toddler study, as discussed by MacLennan and colleagues, found that sensory hyperreactivity predicted an increase in anxiety, whereas anxiety did not predict changes in sensory hyperreactivity. This makes early sensory support particularly relevant. It does not make sensory hyperreactivity equivalent to anxiety or establish that every sensory-seeking or under-responsive student follows the same pathway. [6]

**Informant differences have practical significance:** In Vasa and colleagues’ school-age study, IU correlated with anxiety at **r = 0.46** for child report and **r = 0.40** for parent report. Autistic children showed higher IU than comparison children on both reports, but the group difference in anxiety was significant for parent report, not child report. A teacher should therefore avoid substituting an adult interpretation for the student’s account or assuming that outward behaviour fully represents internal experience. [10]

**The resulting judgment is practical:** The evidence justifies considering uncertainty when supporting anxiety, alongside sensory access, communication and task demands. It supports a flexible formulation for individual students, not a universal prediction-deficit explanation.

**Prediction, attention and mismatch**

**Supported result.** The evidence used here treats predictive coding/processing as a theory, not a tested school intervention: George Musser’s explanatory article (1 May 2019) describes an internal model that forecasts sight, sound, touch, smell and taste and checks those forecasts against reality [130]. Prediction is therefore an expectation-forming process, not motivation or compliance.

Prediction difficulty is not one universal deficit. The account leaves open whether it reflects difficulty forming expectations or sensory input overriding them; the experiments discussed favor the latter [130]. It also reports counterevidence: on some tasks—habituation, some optical illusions, and patterns unfolding over time—autistic people perform as readily as non-autistic people. The direction of causation with social cognition is therefore unclear [130]. The directly illustrated domains are sensory and social; teachers should identify the particular demand rather than assume generalized inability.

Sensory attention means selecting or weighting incoming information, distinct from sensory sensitivity (experienced intensity). In the model, “precision” weights discrepancies between input and expectation, rising during learning and falling once a model is judged complete [130]. Processing is hierarchical, building details into global features; the proposed autism account involves greater weighting of current sensory data, heavier detail focus, or external input overriding internal models [130]. These are hypotheses, not universal characteristics of autistic students.

A prediction error is the mismatch between expected and incoming information. A highly weighted mismatch may drive model updating, whereas a low-weight discrepancy may be treated as variation [130]. This computational definition does not by itself explain a student’s distress or establish a single cause.

**Uncertainty, IU and anxiety**

Uncertainty is an environmental or situational lack of reliable knowledge; intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is a person-level tendency to find that state difficult or aversive; anxiety is the resulting symptom, distress or avoidance outcome, not a synonym for either construct. The cautious integrated model is: sensory weighting shapes predictions → mismatch or prediction error → experienced uncertainty; IU is hypothesized to amplify its significance → anxiety may follow. This arrow structure is a proposed organizing model, not a settled causal law; the evidence represented here is theory-based and mixed rather than direct school-intervention evidence [130].

For a teacher, the model supports asking what sensory or social expectation was violated, what information was irrelevant, and what time or support would permit updating. The source suggests shifting attention from low-level details to higher-level patterns as possible coaching, but not as an evaluated school treatment [130].

Evidence through mid-2023 supports an interconnected—not established causal—pathway. Predictive-coding theory proposes that autistic people may struggle to predict sensory input from past experience; mismatches can be unpleasant, and novel or uncertain classroom events threatening, potentially increasing IU over time [132]. IU is aversion to unpredictable events, distinct from uncertainty, measured here with caregiver-rated IUS-12 (12 items; 1–5; higher = greater IU) [132]. The source reports anxiety in >40% of autistic youth, but this study measured stress, not standardized anxiety [132].

Evidence links sensory difficulty, IU and anxiety/stress reciprocally. In young autistic children, IU mediated sensory-hyperreactivity→anxiety and anxiety mediated hyperreactivity→IU; hyperreactivity did not mediate anxiety→IU [132]. Routine/sameness may control arousal and reduce anxiety; disruption can distress [132]. Among 47 autistic children aged 6–15 surveyed by caregivers during COVID, 89% had significant sensory difficulties pre-pandemic and IUS increased; tables report 33.68→36.53 and 33.77→36.65 (p<.001; effect .61). Sensory-difficulty children (n=42) rose 33.43→37.52 (p<.001; .73), versus typical-sensory children (n=5) 35.80→28.20 (p=.18; .28) [132]. SSP–IUS was weak/non-significant during COVID (r=−.26, p=.08), but change scores correlated (r=−.599, p<.001) [132].

In predictive coding/predictive processing, sensory attention is precision optimization: it increases the weight of selected sensory signals and prediction errors, so attention helps determine what updates the model; when bottom-up input outweighs top-down prediction, the sensory world may be more surprising and cognitively demanding [115][231][229][253]. For autistic students, this supports foregrounding relevant cues and coaching shifts from low-level details to higher-level patterns, while individualizing because competing predictive-processing accounts allow different attention and weighting profiles [130][177].

A prediction error is the mismatch between an expected and observed event or sensory input; it recruits processing and attention and can update the internal model, especially when the mismatch is large or salient [229][105][131][115]. In autism, high or inflexible weighting of unexpected sensory, social, and task events is proposed; an apparent-motion sample found 55% (6/11) with shorter median reaction times to unpredictable targets, descriptively, but precision findings are mixed across tasks, so teachers should explain meaningful deviations and not infer a global prediction failure from one reaction [233][123][155][177].

Predictive coding/predictive processing proposes that difficulty forming or flexibly using predictions makes sensory and social events less predictable; the intolerance-of-uncertainty framework then treats uncertainty as a negative emotional, cognitive, and behavioural experience that can lead to anxiety, while IU is also proposed as a mediator and sensory processing, IU, and anxiety can be reciprocal [189][192][220][132][174]. The empirical pattern is primarily associative—a report gives r(62) = .74 across 12 studies—and the evidence does not establish moderation or causal direction, so teachers should use this as a working formulation rather than a settled explanation [220][243][240].

Predictive coding/predictive processing, often described as active inference, supplies the core model: hierarchical priors generate predictions, sensory attention sets precision, and sensory input is compared with predictions to produce errors; when irrelevant errors are given high precision, sensory overload can result [115][231]. In the proposed autism pathway, attenuated or poorly contextualized top-down prediction increases mismatch and uncertainty, IU is a negative emotional, cognitive, and behavioural response to that uncertainty, and anxiety emerges; because this is a proposed, non-established model, use it to individualize support for overload and predictability rather than as a settled causal explanation [174][171][170][116].

Attention and precision can amplify locally salient prediction errors; when sensory environments are unpredictable, repeated mismatches increase uncertainty, IU, and anxiety, while anxiety-related hyperfocusing, avoidance, or repetitive behaviour disrupts sensory processing and contingency assessment, reducing opportunities to update later predictions [72][131][184][216][221][123]. The proposed feedback loop is therefore error salience → uncertainty/IU → anxiety → avoidance or rigidity → poorer updating → further error salience, but the evidence describes reciprocal and proposed relationships rather than an established causal loop [132][174][189].

Prediction errors can update models, but persistent or unusually precise mismatches capture attention, remain unpleasant or surprising, and can create longer-term uncertainty and IU; relevance estimates determine whether they are treated as useful signals or noise [131][233][156][128]. Support comes from predictive-processing accounts and a reported IU mediation between sensory sensitivities and anxiety, while competing fixed-high-precision and volatility explanations leave the mechanism unsettled; teachers should distinguish meaningful changes from irrelevant variability and explain the former [174][152].

Empirical evidence is positive but primarily associative: across 12 studies, a report gives r(62) = .74 for the IU–anxiety link, and another estimate is r = 0.62 [243][220]. The available intervention evidence includes n = 18 in an intervention group but lacks an active control, blinding, and formally measured treatment integrity, so the relation is not established as experimentally causal [239].

Anxiety can be an outcome of failed prediction and anticipation and uncertainty, but also a feedback factor: when certainty is unknown or control and planning are limited, it heightens threat, narrows attentional control or increases hypervigilance, disrupts sensory processing and contingency assessment, and promotes avoidance that preserves later anxiety [72][216][239][221][117]. Because autistic anxiety may be atypical and may instead reflect sensory overload, specific fears, routine or environmental change, or social and academic demands, teachers should use individualized observation and student accounts rather than assume a single prediction-based cause [83][171][97][103][223].

Because school supports for autistic students show positive effects but reviews provide limited evidence about which methods suit which pupil, teachers should individualize choices around the student’s observed sensory, learning, communication, and anxiety needs and monitor the response rather than use a universal package [61].

Prediction difficulty is described as reduced ability to detect relationships between events in a sequence and to anticipate sensory information; in school this can make novel sequences, changing contingencies, and social or sensory events feel unpredictable or threatening, so explicit cues, structured preparation, and practice across contexts are indicated [131][132][189][220]. It is not a universal deficit—some studies report intact predictive performance or no autistic/non-autistic difference on particular tasks—so teachers should target the individual domain rather than assume global impairment [140][130][170].

Because predictive processing is domain- and task-dependent, teachers should match support to the relevant cueing problem: decrease unnecessary unpredictability, restore routines, pre-warn changes, and direct attention to predictive cues rather than rely on a single autism-wide strategy [132][174][177]. Prediction can also be taught explicitly with rules, modeling, prompting, reinforcement, and multiple exemplars, followed by direct in-vivo practice because generalization was not observed until that practice [107].

Uncertainty—such as not knowing what will happen or how sensory, social, plan, and routine contingencies will unfold—should lead teachers to make school more predictable and controllable with clear routines, advance warnings, explanations of contingencies, and gradual preparation for changes [216][126][60][118]. Because uncertainty should be made manageable rather than treated as something to eliminate completely, assess whether distress reflects uncertainty or fear and tailor the response to sensory and contextual triggers [202][106][131].

IU is a lower threshold for tolerating ambiguous or unknown situations and a tendency to react negatively emotionally, cognitively, and behaviourally; it includes overvaluing predictability and prospective or inhibitory responses to uncertainty [179][192][147][221]. In school, use this profile to determine whether distress follows absent or changed safety contingencies or anticipated unknowns, make contingencies explicit, and offer information and preparation while treating IU as a possible mediator or vulnerability rather than a fixed cause [118][135][189].

Anxiety in autistic students may reflect failed anticipation or uncertainty, sensory reactivity or overload, specific fears, environmental or routine change, and social or academic demands; because signs can overlap with autism and may be atypical, teachers should not infer a single cause [171][216][83]. Use individualized observation and the student’s account to decide whether to change sensory conditions, increase transition predictability, provide coping support, or address the relevant social or academic demand [103][51][97][223].

Prediction errors are expected–actual mismatches that can update the model, but their relevance and reliability should determine whether they are treated as useful signals or noise; unusually precise or small deviations can consume attention and produce disproportionate responses [105][128][156][229]. Teachers can therefore mark which changes are meaningful, reduce irrelevant surprises, and use manageable mismatches for explicit updating while monitoring anxiety; the evidence is mixed across tasks and mechanisms [177][174].

For autistic students, sensory unpredictability is presented as a proposed route by which events become more surprising and less predictable, IU and anxiety rise, and learning conditions deteriorate [253]. The practical conclusion is to individualize support to the pupil rather than assume a universal prediction-deficit explanation, because reviews provide limited evidence about which methods fit specific pupils [61].

Prediction difficulties are proposed to contribute upstream to the uncertainty–anxiety topic: reduced sensitivity to event relationships or attenuated top-down predictions can make sensory and social situations novel or threatening, with sensory hyperreactivity, IU, and anxiety linked in a proposed mediation pathway [131][189][220][184]. The role is not universal or proven causal—some autistic participants show intact predictive performance or no group difference on particular tasks—so supports should target the observed domain and context [140][130][170].

Predictive processing connects prediction with uncertainty and anxiety by having priors generate expectations, comparing them with sensory and social input, and using context-weighted error correction; when contingencies are hard to infer or mismatches remain salient, events can feel less predictable and threatening [85][173][132][193]. This is a proposed, context-dependent account rather than an established causal law, so teachers should match support to the relevant prediction domain and not infer prediction impairment from anxiety alone [222][174].

Uncertainty is not knowing which environmental contingency will occur or being unable to prepare; impaired integration of predictive cues can therefore make sensory, social, and routine changes less controllable, while IU evaluates the unknown negatively and anxiety rises [203][223][216][183]. The relationship is reciprocal rather than unidirectional: sensory-processing difficulty can increase uncertainty and anxiety, and anxiety can disrupt contingency assessment, so schools should make routines and contingencies explicit with clear communication, information, and preparation [132][221][131][126].

## How the three strategy categories complement one another

**Each category targets a different problem:** Sensory management changes the information reaching the student. Reduced prediction demand makes necessary information explicit. Prediction-related teaching develops ways to use cues, anticipate alternatives and respond when expectations need updating.

| Strategy category | Main problem addressed | What the school changes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Managing sensory input | Input is competing, aversive or difficult to regulate | Sensory conditions and access to regulation | Improve speech-to-noise ratio |
| Reducing prediction demand | Sequence, duration or expectations are implicit | Visibility and clarity of information | Show the next activity and its endpoint |
| Teaching prediction-related skills | Change requires updating or flexible responding | Supported practice and feedback | Rehearse Plan A and Plan B |

**Accessibility creates conditions for learning:** Asking a student to practise flexibility while overwhelmed by noise may add demands without creating a useful learning opportunity. Conversely, a quieter setting alone does not teach how to find information about a changed lesson. These supports can work together because they address different parts of the proposed loop.

**The same tool can serve different purposes:** A schedule reduces prediction demand when it simply shows the day’s sequence. It becomes part of active instruction when the student learns to interpret a change cue, identify the replacement activity and practise an alternative response. The distinction lies in what is being taught, not whether a picture or timetable is present. [11]

**Support need not disappear with progress:** Independence can mean independently consulting a schedule, selecting hearing protection or requesting clarification. It need not mean functioning without those resources. The overall aim is greater comfortable participation and agency, with teaching added where it serves the student’s goals.

## Category 1: Strategies for managing sensory input

### Improve auditory access and manage noisy places

**Auditory access is a strong starting point:** Classroom sensory research repeatedly identifies competing sound as a barrier to listening and engagement. The most directly evaluated supports in this category improve the *signal-to-noise ratio*: how clearly useful speech stands out from background sound. [2]

| Named strategy | Concrete school action | Prediction, uncertainty or anxiety mechanism | Evidence focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remote microphone or classroom amplification | Assess whether clearer teacher speech improves access | Reduce uncertainty caused by missed auditory information | Listening, speech recognition and listening stress |
| Headphones or ear defenders | Offer selected use during distressing noise | Reduce uncontrolled auditory input | Participation across everyday settings |
| Remove avoidable sensory triggers | Use chair-leg pads, quieter equipment or suitable clothing alternatives | Reduce aversive events competing for attention | Individualized practice guidance |
| Accessible calm space | Provide a known quiet location and an agreed way to request it | Make regulation and recovery more controllable | Practice guidance |
| Quieter lunch and movement arrangements | Offer earlier corridor movement, quieter lunch or staggered arrival | Avoid unpredictable peaks of noise and crowding | Practice guidance |

The auditory intervention findings come from Rance and colleagues, 2017 [12] and the signal-to-noise systematic review [13]. Headphone participation findings are summarized in Leifler and colleagues, 2022 [14]; the environmental examples appear in National Autistic Society guidance first published in 2019 [15].

**Improve clarity rather than overall loudness:** A remote microphone transmits a speaker’s voice more directly to the student, while classroom amplification distributes speech through the room. The relevant hypothesis is that clearer speech reduces listening effort and missed information. Simply making every sound louder would not serve that purpose.

**School auditory studies report several benefits:** Van der Kruk and colleagues’ 2017 review identified **five eligible studies**, all reporting benefits from improved signal-to-noise ratio, including listening behaviour, on-task behaviour, speech recognition or listening stress. Rance and colleagues’ study involved **26 school-aged autistic children** and reported improved listening, communication and social interaction, alongside lower physiological stress measured through salivary cortisol. [13][12]

**Review-level judgments remain outcome-specific:** Queensland’s 2023 evidence summary was favourable toward several speech-perception and listening outcomes but judged broader communication, social-interaction and physiological-stress findings inconclusive overall. A positive cortisol result in one study and an inconclusive review-level judgment can coexist: they concern different amounts and combinations of evidence. For a school, the immediate question is whether the selected device improves that student’s access and comfort. [16]

**Hearing protection serves a different purpose:** Headphones and ear defenders reduce exposure rather than selectively improving the teacher’s signal. They may help during bells, hand dryers, crowded activities or independent work, depending on preference. Staff should check that the student can still communicate, receive essential instructions and participate in the intended activity. [14]

**Recovery should be predictable and accessible:** Show the student where a calm space is, how to request it and which trusted adult can help. A card, symbol or other established communication method can make that option clear. The mechanism is partly sensory relief and partly control: the student knows what can happen when demands become too much.

**Quiet alternatives should preserve belonging:** A quieter lunch arrangement can support participation when the main dining hall is overwhelming, but quiet should not automatically mean solitary. The student might prefer a small lunch group, an earlier meal or a quieter location with chosen peers. Similarly, leaving class early can reduce corridor crowding without making the student miss the lesson itself.

### Adjust visual load and provide regulation options

**Reduce competition while retaining useful cues:** An accessible classroom is not necessarily an empty classroom. The aim is to reduce irrelevant or aversive stimulation while keeping instructions, schedules and learning materials easy to find.

| Named strategy | Concrete school action | Prediction, uncertainty or anxiety mechanism | Evidence focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individualized lighting adjustment | Compare comfortable lighting arrangements and reduce troublesome glare | Reduce visual discomfort and competing sensory demands | Attention and engagement |
| Visual-load reduction | Simplify displays around teaching and work areas | Make relevant cues easier to identify | Attention and learning |
| Reduce unnecessary simultaneous input | Avoid adding touch, noise and moving visuals without purpose | Reduce competing surprises and processing demands | Theory-informed accommodation |
| Taught regulation menu | Practise student-selected calming or movement options when calm | Provide a known response to rising arousal | Practice guidance |
| Occupational-therapy consultation | Link sensory assessment to specific school participation goals | Improve management of difficult sensory interactions | Individualized functional support |

Lighting and display findings are summarized in the classroom sensory and attention review [2]. The multisensory rationale appears in South and Rodgers, 2017 [1], and the regulation menu in Hume and colleagues’ 2020 uncertainty toolkit [17].

**Lighting findings favour individual testing:** A study summarized in the classroom review reported improved attention and engagement for **three autistic adolescents** after halogen replaced fluorescent lighting. Ellner’s 2011 classroom study involved **four boys aged 12–21**; after sound-absorbing and lighting modifications, **three of four** described improved learning-task performance and positive emotional responses in their journals. These findings favour attending to the student’s experience, rather than prescribing one lighting technology for every classroom. [2][18]

**Relevant visuals differ from decorative clutter:** High background visual displays have been associated with poorer learning scores, while limited relevant displays can assist learning. Keep the schedule and current task information prominent; remove competing decoration from the immediate work area when it distracts. A visually crowded “support wall” can defeat its own purpose if the student cannot identify the useful cue. [2]

**Multisensory support requires purposeful selection:** A spoken explanation accompanied by a simple picture may improve access. The problem is unnecessary simultaneous load—for example, instructions delivered amid loud audio, moving decoration and unexpected touch. Sensory-challenge research discussed by South and Rodgers found particularly heightened responses when auditory and tactile stimuli occurred together, providing a rationale for checking combinations as well as individual triggers. [1]

**A regulation menu makes coping concrete:** Options may include rocking, listening to chosen music, deep breathing, brief vigorous exercise, a preferred video or access to a favourite material. Teach what each option involves during calm periods, and represent the options in a comprehensible format. This turns “calm down” into an actionable choice and reduces uncertainty about how to obtain support. [17]

**Student preference determines the useful option:** Movement may regulate one student but increase another’s arousal; breathing exercises may be welcome to one student and uncomfortable to another. Safe self-regulatory movement should be understood in terms of function rather than appearance. The desired outcome is improved comfort and access, not simply a quieter-looking student.

### Distinguish specialist sensory work from equipment prescriptions

**Sensory approaches have different evidence bases:** Occupational therapy can help identify barriers and adapt the interaction between the student, task and environment. Ayres Sensory Integration is a specific therapist-delivered approach; it should not be treated as interchangeable with adding a vest, cushion or “sensory activity.”

| Approach or equipment | Intended target | Evidence judgment | School implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ayres Sensory Integration | Functional sensory-motor participation | Specific evidence recognized in the 2020 NCAEP review | Use specialist assessment and defined goals |
| Alternative seating | Attention, engagement or seating-related regulation | Fifteen studies yielded inconclusive evidence in Queensland’s summary | Select for a specific functional need |
| Weighted vests | Classroom attention or behaviour | Queensland’s summary did not recommend routine use for these goals | Do not treat as a default anxiety support |

The distinctions are supported by the 2020 NCAEP evidence-based-practice report [19] and Queensland’s sensory evidence summary [16].

**Functional goals keep sensory work meaningful:** A goal might concern participating comfortably in art, accessing the playground or following spoken instructions. The theory-based rationale is that better management of sensory experiences may reduce perceived unpredictability and subsequent anxiety. South and Rodgers and a 2021 sensory–IU–anxiety paper identify this as a plausible intervention route. [1][20]

**The sensory-category takeaway:** Change the sensory conditions that interfere with this student’s comfort and participation, and judge each tool by its specific function rather than its “sensory” label.

## Category 2: Strategies for reducing students’ need for prediction

### Make sequence, duration and expectations explicit

**External information reduces inference demands:** These supports answer questions that otherwise require guessing: What is happening? Where should I go? How long will it last? How will I know it is finished? What happens afterwards?

Schedule implementation is described by TRIAD [11]; work-system findings by Macdonald and colleagues, 2018 [21]; transition examples by Autism CRC [22]; and communication, routines and choices by Hume and colleagues, 2020 [17].

**Comprehension comes before display format:** A written timetable is helpful only if the student understands it. Some learners need objects or photographs; others prefer symbols, text or a digital list. Begin with a manageable sequence and teach the student how to consult it, rather than assuming that displaying a schedule is sufficient. [11]

**Schedules need clear completion information:** A finished pocket, tick, removable symbol or other marker separates completed events from upcoming ones. Checking before and after activities helps connect the representation with what actually happens. A consistent prompt such as “Check the schedule” can transfer information-seeking from repeated adult explanation to an accessible resource.

**Work systems clarify the task itself:** A student may know that mathematics comes next but remain uncertain about which questions to complete or when the work ends. A work system makes the sequence and endpoint visible—for example, identifying the assigned task, how to mark completion and what follows. Its role is to reduce the amount of task organization the student must generate while learning the content.

**Mainstream classroom findings support engagement:** Macdonald and colleagues evaluated teacher-implemented visual schedules and work systems for **four autistic students** in their ordinary mainstream classrooms. All four improved on-task behaviour; productivity and other secondary outcomes varied. The practical result is support for clearer work organization, with outcomes chosen to match the student’s actual difficulty. [21]

### Make time understandable and keep cues trustworthy

**Warnings should identify the next event:** Autism CRC’s transition guidance gives examples of a **five-minute warning followed by a three-minute warning**, supported by visual routine prompts and countdown timers. The useful information is not merely that the current activity is ending, but what will happen next and how the student can prepare. [22]

**Concrete time information reduces ambiguity:** “In a little while” or “just a second” may be difficult to interpret, especially when adults do not use the words literally. A visible countdown, a marked endpoint or a clearly defined remaining part of the activity can make the expectation easier to understand. The format should fit the student’s understanding of time. [17]

**Repeated steps build dependable associations:** A transition might consistently begin with finishing the current item, placing materials in a designated location and checking the next cue. Reusing those opening steps reduces the number of decisions required at a demanding moment. Autism CRC recommends explicitly explaining and walking through routines and using the same initial steps across transitions where possible. [22]

**Accuracy protects the value of support:** If adults repeatedly announce a transition and then delay it, or change the schedule without explaining, the cues become less trustworthy. Update the information when circumstances change. A useful schedule represents current expectations and how changes will be communicated, rather than making promises that staff cannot keep.

### Preview events and offer meaningful control

**Previews supply missing context:** Before a new activity, explain the location, people, likely sequence, unfamiliar features and available help. Suitable formats include photographs, a short narrative, a walkthrough or direct explanation. A qualitative mainstream-school review reported that previewing physical-education content helped autistic students prepare for participation and increased trust in the teacher. [14]

**Concrete language reduces interpretive work:** Replace an instruction such as “Get yourself sorted” with the specific actions required. Explain unfamiliar idioms, clarify whether a question is optional and distinguish “now” from “later.” Visual or written information can remain available after spoken language has passed. [17]

**Choice can reduce uncertainty through control:** Offer meaningful alternatives that the school can honour, such as choosing between two work formats, selecting an appropriate work location or deciding the order of agreed tasks. The mechanism is increased autonomy and motivation within an understandable structure. An unlimited question such as “What do you want to do?” may create additional uncertainty when the student needs clearer boundaries. [17]

**Previews and choices serve different needs:** A preview tells the student what is likely; a choice gives the student influence over part of what happens. Someone may benefit from both. Neither should be used to disguise a non-negotiable demand as a choice or to promise that every detail can be known beforehand.

### When schedules and warnings are not enough

**Transition difficulty is not always uncertainty-driven:** Ending a valued activity, beginning difficult work or entering an aversive location can remain distressing even when perfectly predictable. Advance-notice reviews describe both positive findings and failures linked to these other controlling variables. [3]

**A two-child study illustrates the contrast:** Waters and colleagues evaluated transitions for **two six-year-old autistic boys**. Both showed problem behaviour during **100% of baseline transitions**, and high levels persisted when visual schedules were the sole treatment. A function-based package involving extinction and differential reinforcement of other behaviour reduced responding by **69% and 83%**, respectively; schedules could add benefit within the package. [23]

**The mechanism determines the useful addition:** Differential reinforcement provides a positive consequence for a selected alternative pattern or, in that study, the absence of specified behaviour. Extinction means that the consequence previously maintaining a behaviour is no longer delivered. These are descriptions of the evaluated treatment, not a recommendation for teachers to improvise extinction or disregard distress.

**Assessment should protect communication and access:** If greater predictability does not help, examine task difficulty, sensory conditions, the loss involved in stopping and the student’s way of asking for help or a break. An individualized plan may combine instructional adjustments, accessible communication and positive reinforcement. Qualified support is important where transitions involve severe distress or safety concerns. [19]

**The prediction-demand takeaway:** Make the relevant information clear, but change the underlying task or environment when lack of information is not the main problem.

## Category 3: Strategies for actively teaching prediction-related skills

### Teach cue use, updating and coping—not perfect forecasting

**Active instruction adds a learning objective:** Reducing prediction demand supplies information; teaching prediction-related skills helps the student use that information. Useful objectives include recognizing a cue, anticipating a likely next event, identifying an alternative, checking what actually happened and selecting a response when expectations change.

**A preview is not automatically instruction:** Watching a route video can make tomorrow’s trip more familiar without producing a transferable skill. To add an instructional element, a teacher might pause at a meaningful cue, ask the student to indicate what comes next, and then compare the expectation with the event. That is a proposed classroom application of priming and guided practice, not a separately established treatment.

The visual-predictor, story and video methods are described in Hume’s 2006 practice article [24]; Social Story anxiety outcomes in Hanrahan and colleagues, 2021 [25]; and contextual variation in autistic perspectives on HIPPEA [26].

### Use visual predictor strategies and graded change

**Teach what the change cue means:** Once a student understands the routine and schedule, introduce a comprehensible marker for a cancelled or replacement activity. Show the original item, the change cue and the new event together, then support the student in finding the updated information and completing the transition. Depending on comprehension, the cue may be an object, photograph, symbol or written word. [24]

**Begin with manageable changes:** Hume proposes introducing change first through a shift from a less preferred to a preferred activity, then a neutral change and later a more demanding change. The intended learning is that “change” does not invariably predict loss or danger. The progression should be adapted with the student, rather than used to manufacture distress.

**Keep the response procedure stable:** The event can vary while the procedure remains familiar: notice the cue, check the update, ask for clarification if needed and choose an available coping response. This offers a useful form of predictability—knowing how to find out and what to do—even when the exact activity differs.

**Separate flexibility from silent compliance:** Successful learning might include independently asking what has changed, expressing disappointment, selecting a break and then deciding how to participate. Merely suppressing an outward response does not demonstrate understanding or confidence.

### Use Social Stories to explain novel events

**Individualized stories make expectations explicit:** A Social Story or appropriately designed social narrative can explain a substitute teacher, excursion, new classroom procedure or unfamiliar social event. Match language and illustrations to comprehension, include information the student genuinely needs and describe possible responses without implying that one emotional reaction is required. [24]

**Priming studies provide concrete examples:** Hume summarizes Ivey, Heflin and Alberto’s work with **three young autistic students**, whose stories were read for **three to five days** before novel community events. Increased participation and reduced challenging behaviour were reported. The examples included a birthday party, pond visit and gift shop, so the direct evidence concerns preparation for novelty rather than ordinary classroom lessons alone. [24]

**A school-linked camp study measured anxiety:** Hanrahan and colleagues studied **10 autistic children aged 7–11** in a specialist autism unit. Each read an individualized digital Social Story daily for **five days** before a **four-day summer camp**. Mean teacher-rated anxiety fell from **5.30 to 3.10 on the study’s rating scale**, while ratings of understanding and closeness to the child’s goal increased. [25]

**Results vary substantially across stories:** Hanrahan and colleagues summarize an earlier review that classified **51%** of Social Story interventions as highly effective and **44%** as ineffective. This contrast argues for selecting a precise informational target and checking whether the student understands and uses the story. Widespread use alone is not a reason to retain an ineffective story. [25]

**Practice extends the story’s function:** After reading, rehearse a relevant action and provide an opportunity to use it in the actual setting. A story about joining an activity can be followed by practice with a willing peer, rather than remaining a discussion with an adult. Portable stories may remain accessible while prompts are gradually reduced. [27]

### Use video priming and transition rehearsal

**Short videos make sequences concrete:** Hume describes **one- to four-minute** narrated walkthroughs, viewed several times over several days before a novel activity. A school could use this format to preview a route, room or excursion sequence. The reported research examples involved several autistic boys visiting unfamiliar community stores, where disruptive behaviour decreased as routines became more predictable. [24]

**Useful videos highlight meaningful cues:** Show the information that predicts an action: the entrance used, where the group meets or how the next step is signalled. Avoid requiring the student to remember every incidental detail. If circumstances may differ, explain that possibility rather than teaching the recording as an exact promise.

**Rehearsal teaches an available response:** Explain the transition sequence, model it and walk through it with the student before the demanding occasion. Practise the relevant communication as well: asking where to go, checking a cue or requesting help. Autism CRC’s guidance combines explicit explanation, visual supports, repeated initial steps and positive reinforcement for completing transitions. [22]

**Feedback should identify the useful connection:** Instead of only saying “Good transition,” describe what worked: the student noticed the cue, checked the replacement activity or asked for clarification. This is a proposed teaching application that makes the relationship between information and action more explicit.

### Broaden learning through controlled variation

**Generalization requires more than repetition:** Repeating an identical sequence may teach that exact sequence very well. To support broader use, vary selected people, materials or locations while preserving the important relationship. The teaching question becomes: which features predict the outcome, and which can change without changing the task?

**Scaffolding can bridge simple and natural situations:** HIPPEA’s developmental discussion proposes explicit support that progresses from simple to more naturalistic situations. A 2023 qualitative study of autistic perspectives also described continuous exposure as supporting generalizable concepts and discussed adding minute contextual differences within controlled situations. That study involved autistic adults and one parent, making it an indirect contribution to school practice. [8][26]

**Choose variation that serves a goal:** A hypothetical application would be practising the same help-seeking routine with another trusted adult before trying it in a different room. This changes one meaningful feature while retaining familiar communication and support. Increasing several demands simultaneously would make it harder to identify what the student has learned.

**Distress should change the teaching decision:** If variation overwhelms the student, restore sufficient support, reduce the change or reconsider whether the activity is accessible. The intended learning is that selected uncertainty can be managed—not that painful input or unclear demands must be endured.

### Use Unstuck and On Target for flexibility and planning

**A named school programme supports alternatives:** *Unstuck and On Target* (UOT) teaches flexibility, goal-setting and planning using scripts such as **Plan A/Plan B** and **Big Deal/Little Deal**, supported by visual materials, games, videos, role-play and guided practice. Its relevance to uncertainty is that an obstacle need not leave the student without an available next action. [28]

**The comparison tested more than adult attention:** In Kenworthy and colleagues’ 2014 trial, **14 elementary schools** were randomized: **10** to UOT and **four** to a social-skills comparison. The groups included **47 UOT students** and **20 comparison students** in grades 3–5. Both received **28 lessons of 30–40 minutes** over a school year, with comparable intervention and training time. [28]

**Flexibility gains appeared in ordinary classrooms:** UOT produced greater improvements in flexibility, problem-solving and reported planning/organization than the social-skills programme. Treatment-blind classroom observations also favoured UOT for following directions, transitions and avoiding getting stuck. Both groups improved similarly in social skills, so UOT’s advantage was most relevant to its flexibility-related targets. [28]

**Planning results depended on the measure:** Parent and teacher planning ratings favoured UOT, whereas the treatment-blind direct planning task did not show an advantage. This distinction helps schools choose expectations: the programme provides evaluated support for flexibility and classroom functioning, rather than an assurance that every planning outcome will improve.

**Match delivery to the learner:** The trial required an IQ above 70 and a verbal mental age of at least eight years. Its language-rich scripts should therefore be considered in relation to the individual student’s communication and learning profile. School and home staff used common language and modelled the scripts across contexts, making consistent implementation part of the programme. [28]

### Teach social prediction through In My Shoes

**Whole-class teaching changes the social environment:** *In My Shoes* uses **look–think–decide**: notice observable clues, consider what another person may be thinking or feeling and decide how to support participation. Interactive videos, comic strips, role-play and discussion revisit school situations such as playground activities, excursions, assemblies and sports events. [29]

**Its evaluation included peers and teachers:** The programme was delivered in 2020 and published on **1 June 2022**. The feasibility study included **10 autistic students and 200 peers**, across **eight classrooms and six schools**. Teachers generally delivered **one 45-minute lesson each week for 10 weeks**, with professional learning, peer training and family information accompanying the lessons. [29]

**Preliminary outcomes concerned participation and experience:** Findings were encouraging for self-reported school engagement, autistic students’ classroom participation and their subjective school experiences. This is particularly relevant where difficulty arises from mutual misunderstanding or insufficient peer support, because the intervention does not place all responsibility on the autistic student.

**Social prediction should allow multiple interpretations:** Body language is informative but not infallible. A useful classroom application teaches students to consider possibilities and ask respectful questions, rather than assuming that another person’s thoughts can be read with certainty. Participation and helpful responding are more appropriate goals than requiring one “correct” interpretation of an ambiguous social cue.

### Test transfer across everyday contexts

**Learned skills need ordinary opportunities:** Bellini and colleagues’ 2007 synthesis covered **55 school-based social-skills studies involving 147 autistic students**. Overall treatment and generalization effects were low, while maintenance effects were moderate; outcomes were more favourable when intervention occurred in students’ typical classrooms than in pull-out settings. [30]

**Transfer should be observed directly:** After a student uses a change cue with one teacher, examine a similar situation with another teacher or activity. After rehearsing help-seeking, check whether it occurs during real lessons. Record understanding, communication, comfort and the level of prompting, not just whether the outward action occurred.

**Fade teaching prompts selectively:** Adult reminders may become less necessary as the student learns to consult a resource independently. The resource itself can remain. Generalization should increase the student’s options, not become a reason to withdraw an accommodation that continues to provide access.

**The prediction-teaching takeaway:** Teach students how to use cues, revise expectations and obtain support across manageable variations, rather than expecting accurate forecasts of an inherently changeable school day.

## Other related support methods

### Teach the hidden curriculum explicitly

**Unspoken rules create avoidable uncertainty:** The *hidden curriculum* consists of expectations that are assumed rather than directly taught: idioms, slang, body language, contextual rules, group-work conventions and assumptions about others’ intentions. Explicit teaching makes these expectations discussable instead of requiring intuitive acquisition. [31]

| Named method | Concrete school action | Mechanism relevant to uncertainty | Evidence focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explicit contextual-rule teaching | Explain an unwritten expectation using real examples | Replace ambiguous inference with accessible information | Heterogeneous school literature |
| SOCCSS | Analyse a situation, options and consequences, then rehearse | Make social cause-and-effect explicit | Structured teaching method |
| SOLVE | Teach observation, listening and clarification questions | Provide a repeatable information-seeking process | Structured teaching method |
| Trusted social interpreter | Identify an adult or willing peer who can clarify situations | Offer a reliable route out of social ambiguity | Practice guidance |
| Clear cooperative-group roles | Define each member’s responsibility and turn-taking expectations | Reduce uncertainty about participation and overlap | School practice literature |

These methods are described in Ishaq’s 2019 scoping review [31].

**Explain rules as contextual information:** A rule may differ between a classroom discussion, assessment, friendship group and playground game. Teaching only a rigid script can create new confusion when the context changes. Use examples of when the expectation applies, when it differs and how the student can check.

**SOCCSS makes alternatives visible:** The sequence is **Situation, Options, Consequences, Choices, Strategies and Simulation**. A teacher helps the student describe what happened, identify possible responses, consider likely consequences, choose a strategy and rehearse it. This supports social prediction by making the links between actions and possible outcomes explicit. [31]

**SOLVE supports active clarification:** The prompts are **Seek, Observe, Listen, Vocalize and Educate**. They encourage noticing the situation, listening for information, asking questions and retaining what was learned. Its useful aim is not greater guessing about hidden meanings, but a repeatable way to obtain information when meaning is unclear. [31]

**A trusted person provides interpretation:** Agree who can clarify confusing words, incidents or expectations and how the student can approach them. Possible people include a teacher, counsellor, paraprofessional, mentor or close friend. That relationship can reduce social uncertainty while the student develops their own information-seeking strategies.

**Clear roles make group work accessible:** Identify who gathers materials, records ideas, reports back or completes each component, and check that students understand how contributions fit together. Roles can draw on the autistic student’s knowledge or interests without fixing them permanently into one position. Understanding peers can also help clarify cues and prevent competing claims over the same task. [31]

**The evidence supports attention to hidden demands:** Ishaq’s master’s-level scoping review included **nine heterogeneous studies**, comprising **five quantitative and four qualitative studies**, and described generally useful outcomes. Moyse and Porter’s ethnographic study of **three autistic girls** in mainstream primary schools showed how their personal adjustments to hidden expectations could go unnoticed, masking support needs. These findings give teachers a reason to ask about effort and confusion even when a student appears to cope. [31][32]

**Teach understanding without demanding masking:** The student should be able to question an expectation, decline an unwanted interaction and report unfair treatment. Some problems require peers or adults to change their behaviour. Hidden-curriculum instruction is most useful when it improves comprehension and agency, rather than teaching the student to conceal distress.

### Combine explicit social teaching with peer support

**Social learning benefits from concrete practice:** Direct explanation, modelling, role-play, rehearsal and feedback make social contingencies more understandable. Practice with real peers then allows students to apply learning under the conditions in which it matters.

| Related approach | Concrete application | Prediction or participation mechanism | Evidence focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| PEERS | Teach explicit social steps, rehearse and practise between sessions | Make social options and expectations concrete | Social knowledge and peer contact |
| Peer-mediated support | Train peers to notice, respond and include during ordinary activities | Make interaction more responsive and understandable | Interaction and engagement |
| Role-play with real-context practice | Rehearse a chosen interaction, then use it in school | Connect instruction with actual social cues | Generalization-oriented teaching |
| Whole-class inclusion teaching | Teach differences, help-seeking and supportive responses | Reduce misunderstanding across the peer environment | Participation and connectedness |

PEERS is described in the 2022 Polish trial [33]; peer and rehearsal methods in generalization guidance [27]; and whole-class teaching in In My Shoes [29].

**PEERS offers a structured example:** Its curriculum presents social skills as concrete rules or steps, followed by demonstrations, rehearsal, feedback and homework supported by parent coaching. A Polish randomized trial included **29 autistic adolescents aged 11–16**, reporting large improvements in social skills, social-skills knowledge and peer get-togethers; most effects were maintained at **six months**. [33]

**School use requires contextual fit:** PEERS is a parent-assisted programme, not simply a list of classroom rules. Its relevance is the explicit, sequenced teaching approach and opportunities to practise. The trial report also warns that social skills lacking ecological validity can worsen peer responses, so examples should fit the student’s age, culture, local peer context and chosen goals. [33]

**Peers can carry part of the change:** Teach willing peers how to respond to communication, offer understandable invitations and include students in ordinary activities. “Stay–play–talk” buddy approaches use adult modelling, guided practice, feedback and opportunities for independent interaction. The mechanism is partly environmental: the autistic student encounters a more responsive and interpretable social setting. [27]

**Peer support is not automatically superior:** The mainstream-accommodation review found differing results across school peer interventions. One trial favoured a peer component on several outcomes, while another found didactic social-skills teaching outperformed a differently organized peer-group approach on engagement and solitary play. The useful judgment concerns the actual training, context and outcome, rather than the label “peer support.” [34]

### Use special interests as meaningful, student-chosen resources

**Interests can supply motivation and structure:** A familiar interest can make an academic task worth approaching, provide a predictable context for exploration or establish common ground with peers. These are distinct mechanisms. An improvement in engagement should not automatically be interpreted as reduced anxiety. [35]

| Interest-based application | Concrete school action | Intended mechanism | Evidence focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Academic materials | Incorporate chosen interests into texts, examples or assignments | Increase relevance and persistence | Engagement and task completion |
| Shared-interest lunch clubs | Organize activities that genuinely interest participating students | Provide common ground for interaction | Social engagement and initiations |
| Power cards | Use an interest-linked figure to cue a chosen response | Make a social or transition cue salient | Conversation and transitions |
| Joint-attention activities | Share or discuss preferred materials with a partner | Make another person’s attention meaningful | Responding to shared attention |
| Planned calming access | Make familiar interest activities predictably accessible | Support regulation through familiar, valued activity | Qualitative accounts and guidance |
| First–Then arrangements | Clearly connect an agreed task with a chosen later activity | Clarify sequence and increase motivation | Classroom case examples |

These applications appear in the secondary-school interest review [35], Mancil and Pearl’s classroom examples [36] and qualitative autistic accounts [26].

**Academic examples preserve the learning objective:** An interest can change the context without replacing the curriculum. For a student interested in hurricanes, mathematics might involve wind speed, distance or landfall time, while writing addresses a report about storms. Mancil and Pearl’s **July 2008** case story described interest-based adaptations for **three students** across elementary, middle and high school, including improved persistence and engagement. [36]

**Small academic studies report positive engagement:** Dahlbäck’s **Autumn 2021** degree project used a multiple-baseline design with **three high-school students**, adapting assignments to their individual interests; on-task behaviour increased for all three. Mancil and Pearl also summarize an earlier **five-child preschool study** in which interest-embedded tasks increased correctly completed items within a limited period. These provide concrete examples across developmental stages, with different levels of direct relevance to school-age teaching. [37][36]

**First–Then combines clarity with motivation:** In Mancil and Pearl’s example, a student completed an agreed amount of reading before access to a Thomas the Train activity. The board made the sequence and consequence visible, and reading increased over subsequent weeks. Its mechanism differs from embedding the interest in the task itself: the interest follows the task rather than providing its content. [36]

**Interest clubs can support shared participation:** Koegel and colleagues’ 2013 multiple-baseline study included **seven autistic high-school students aged 14–16**. Preferred interests were incorporated into lunchtime activities; engagement and peer initiations increased, and autistic students and peers reported enjoying the activities. Transfer was reported to other similar activities. [38]

**The secondary-school literature concentrates on social outcomes:** A 2021 scoping review included **six studies**, all conducted in the United States and published during **2010–2013**, involving **17 pupils aged 11–17**. The three applications were **power cards, lunch clubs and responding to joint attention**. This explains why the more developed peer-reviewed evidence concerns social participation, while broader academic examples also include case stories and degree-level work. [35]

**Power cards are interest-linked prompts:** A card uses a meaningful character or figure to illustrate a response, such as joining conversation or moving between activities. Its proposed value is that the prompt attracts attention and makes the response sequence memorable. The student should help decide whether that format feels useful and age-appropriate.

**Joint attention can build common ground:** Interest-related materials can make looking at, discussing or responding to another person’s focus more meaningful. The purpose is shared engagement with something valued, rather than requiring a particular style of eye contact. The reviewed studies used interest-related activities as social reinforcers during instruction. [35]

**Generalization depends on the new context:** The review reported power-card transfer across classes for **two of three participants** in one study. Other findings varied with how similar or preferred the new activity was; activities dissimilar to the original lunch clubs did not show the same transfer. A school should therefore test whether benefits extend beyond the exact interest-based arrangement. [35]

**Calming access should remain student-led:** Autistic participants in the 2023 qualitative HIPPEA study described special interests as supporting exploration and feelings of calm and relaxation. Schools can provide planned opportunities to use a familiar interest for regulation, guided by the student’s preference. Access needed for regulation should not automatically be made conditional on completing work. [26]

**Respect and feasibility determine usefulness:** Ask whether the student wants an interest included in lessons or shared with classmates. Dahlbäck reported that some interests did not fit curriculum requirements efficiently and that adaptation required teacher time. The 2021 review also criticized approaches that used interests to secure socially desirable behaviour without changing difficult environments. A strengths-based label is therefore not enough; the student’s experience and agency matter. [37][35]

### Add anxiety treatment, uncertainty-tolerance work and implementation support

**Related supports target different mechanisms:** Some methods treat anxiety directly; others teach uncertainty tolerance or improve consistent delivery of accommodations. They should complement school access rather than replace it.

| Related support | Concrete school contribution | Intended mechanism | Evidence setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adapted CBT | Coordinate structured coping practice and agreed graded exposure | Address anxious appraisals, arousal and avoidance | School and clinical studies |
| Mindfulness-based support | Offer adapted, voluntary emotional-awareness and attention practices | Improve responses to strong feelings | Mainly broader treatment literature |
| CUES and Predictive Parenting | Align school prompts with agreed family intervention goals | Increase tolerance of everyday uncertainty | Parent-mediated programmes |
| Staff learning and coaching | Teach shared cueing, communication and sensory-support practices | Make support more reliable and individualized | School reviews and early-childhood studies |
| Self-management with peer training | Teach a daily task list, self-checking and evaluation | Make expectations explicit and increase independence | Small high-school study |

Sources include the school anxiety-intervention review [39], South and Rodgers’ treatment discussion [1], CUES development study [40], educator coaching study [41] and self-management evaluation [42].

**School CBT has direct anxiety evidence:** Perihan and colleagues’ review synthesized **six school-based studies involving 165 participants aged 5–21**, in special-education settings. The pooled anxiety effect was **Hedges’ g = −0.58**, with a **95% confidence interval of −0.96 to −0.20**, favouring intervention. This represents a moderate standardized difference, not a 58% reduction in anxiety. [39]

**The evaluated programmes shared recognizable components:** All six used CBT principles, combining education about feelings, anxiety-management techniques and graded exposure. Coping components included breathing, relaxation, problem-solving and cognitive self-control. Programme duration ranged from **six to 32 weeks**, with **60–90-minute sessions**. [39]

**Exploring Feelings provides a concrete example:** Clarke, Hill and Charman’s 2017 study involved **28 boys aged 11–14 across six schools**. The intervention used **six one-hour sessions** addressing anxious thoughts, relaxation, thinking tools and coping plans. Compared with controls, students reported fewer anxiety symptoms, less behavioural avoidance and greater problem-solving; effects were maintained at **six to eight weeks**. [43]

**Adaptation makes treatment more accessible:** Concrete explanations, visual materials, structured sessions and suitable interest-based activities can help students engage with CBT. Clinical work discussed by South and Rodgers also found that higher pretreatment IU was associated with less anxiety improvement during *Face Your Fears*. That supports considering uncertainty explicitly when conventional anxiety work does not address the student’s central difficulty. [1][44]

**Mindfulness targets responses to emotion:** Adapted mindfulness-based approaches aim to increase awareness of feelings and the ability to cope with strong emotions, with encouraging anxiety findings discussed in the pre-cutoff treatment literature. In school, a brief practice should be voluntary and accessible; it need not involve closing the eyes or focusing on uncomfortable bodily sensations. It is an emotion-regulation option, rather than a substitute for reducing sensory pain or clarifying expectations. [1]

**CUES addresses tolerance rather than total certainty:** *Coping with Uncertainty in Everyday Situations* aims to increase autonomy, flexibility and tolerance of uncertainty, while reducing avoidance and excessive certainty-building. The parent programme used **eight weekly two-hour sessions**, visual prompts, comic strips, individualized situations and home practice. Its target differs from a schedule: the student practises managing some unknowns rather than removing them all. [40]

**Feasibility findings support cautious coordination:** The CUES report presented descriptive child-IU effect sizes of **d = 0.56** and **d = 0.31** in two outcome groups, with a reported pooled value of **d = 0.43**. These were feasibility findings from a small parent-mediated programme. *Predictive Parenting* was also identified by MacLennan and colleagues as a manualized parent-group approach intended to increase uncertainty tolerance; a school’s contribution is to coordinate suitable prompts and practice with the family and relevant professionals. [40][6]

**Staff consistency affects predictability directly:** Staff learning should cover the student’s communication, sensory profile, schedule format, change cues and response to help-seeking. If one adult honours a break signal and another does not, the support itself becomes uncertain. A qualitative review associated autism-focused professional development with more positive attitudes and greater teacher self-efficacy. [14]

**Coaching offers an implementation example:** Chrétien-Vincent and colleagues’ study, published online on **19 April 2023**, involved **six educator–child dyads in six daycares**, with autistic children aged **three to five years**. The intervention included **five educational videos and three to five coaching sessions**; implemented strategies facilitated performance on **63% of identified goals**. This is early-childhood participation evidence, relevant to the value of individualized coaching rather than a direct school-age anxiety effect. [41]

**Self-management can transfer control to students:** Roberts and colleagues’ 2019 study used a daily to-do list, self-monitoring, self-evaluation and self-reinforcement with peer training for **two high-school students**. The list made work-completion expectations visible, while the students learned to manage their own participation. The ABAB withdrawal design provided moderate evidence of a causal relationship with academic engagement, and the autistic peer trainer implemented training with fidelity. [42]

**The related-support takeaway:** Make social expectations understandable, use interests with permission, improve the responsiveness of peers and staff, and add targeted anxiety or uncertainty-tolerance work when those are the student’s needs.

## Choosing and evaluating support in school

**Judge success by the intended outcome:** A sensory adjustment should improve comfort or access; a schedule should improve understanding of sequence; flexibility teaching should support responses to manageable change. If anxiety is the target, include an accessible account of anxiety rather than inferring it solely from task completion. A student can participate more while still experiencing substantial internal effort.

**Environmental support deserves equal consideration:** The mainstream-school accommodation review identified **37 eligible studies**, of which **14 met What Works Clearinghouse standards**. It tentatively supported some pedagogical and psychosocial accommodations, while no study specifically targeting the physical environment met that review’s eligibility scope. This does not contradict the auditory and lighting examples above: the reviews covered different settings, definitions and study criteria. [34]

**Combine supports around one meaningful situation:** In a hypothetical example, a student anxious about an assembly might need a less noisy seating position, a clear account of the sequence and rehearsal of what to do if the usual teacher is absent. Those address sensory load, prediction demand and flexible responding respectively. If the assembly remains painful or inaccessible, more rehearsal is not the only response; the environment or participation arrangement should change.

**Evidence should guide interpretation:** The integrated loop is a theoretical synthesis. In the 2020 IU review, all extracted data were cross-sectional, and none of its 12 studies used an IU measure validated for autistic people; many school studies instead measured listening stress, engagement, transitions or flexibility. Correlation *r*, standardized effects *g* and *d*, teacher-rating points and cortisol therefore describe different outcomes, not interchangeable anxiety reductions. Establishing the full causal ordering would require longitudinal or experimental school-age work measuring sensory attention, prediction-error precision, IU and anxiety together. Small samples and differences in language, intellectual ability and setting affect transfer. White and colleagues’ review was published **11 July 2023**, outside the cutoff; the National Autistic Society webpage first published in 2019 also displays a later 2025 review date and is treated here as practice guidance, not new outcome evidence. [9][45][15]

**The overall school judgment:** Reduce unnecessary sensory and informational uncertainty while teaching supported ways to manage the uncertainty that remains. The most useful package is the one that improves the individual student’s comfort, understanding, autonomy and chosen participation—not simply their ability to appear unaffected by an unpredictable school day.
