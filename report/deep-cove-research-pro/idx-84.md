# Managing Oil Wealth to Avoid the Resource Curse: Fiscal Rules, Revenue Funds, Public Investment, and Norway Through 2020

**A coordinated policy response.** Oil-dependent countries manage wealth most effectively when they separate volatile petroleum receipts from annual spending, preserve part of their exhaustible wealth, and turn domestic expenditure into productive assets and public services. The resource curse is the broad failure to achieve those outcomes; Dutch disease is a narrower mechanism through which oil inflows weaken other internationally competitive industries. Fiscal rules, Natural Resource Funds, and Public Investment Management Systems address different parts of this problem and work best together. [1]

**Norway’s institutional benchmark.** Norway combined substantial public capture of petroleum profits, saving through the Government Pension Fund Global, foreign investment, a spending guideline linked to expected real investment returns, and parliamentary oversight. Its original 2001 guideline used a 4% expected real return; the public framework described in 2020 used 3%. Its achievement illustrates a coordinated institutional arrangement—not proof that establishing a sovereign wealth fund alone prevents the resource curse. [2]

**The report’s four questions.** The analysis first explains the economic and institutional damage associated with oil dependence. It then compares three fiscal-rule families, examines how revenue funds and investment-management systems operate, and shows how Norway connects these elements within one budgetary and governance framework.

## 1. Core Challenges: Resource Curse and Dutch Disease

### The resource curse: weak development despite valuable resources

**The broad development problem.** The *resource curse*, also called the paradox of plenty, describes the failure of many resource-rich countries to translate natural-resource wealth into sustained development and effective public welfare. It encompasses economic, political, and institutional problems rather than one particular disease or an inevitable relationship between petroleum production and poverty. NRGI’s 2015 synthesis associates resource-rich economies with lower economic stability and growth, greater authoritarianism, and more conflict than their non-resource-rich neighbors. [1]

**Dependence rather than endowment alone.** Having oil reserves is different from depending on oil for exports, government revenue, or economic activity. An economy with a diversified tax base and substantial non-oil production can withstand petroleum shocks more readily than one in which public expenditure and foreign-exchange earnings rely overwhelmingly on oil. This distinction matters because the policy problem is not simply the physical presence of petroleum; it is the way petroleum income interacts with the rest of the economy and the state. [3]

**Evidence of a growth penalty.** Kakanov, Blöchliger, and Demmou’s 2018 OECD working paper analyzed 24 oil-exporting countries over 1982–2012 using an error-correction model. A 10-percentage-point increase in oil’s share of exports was associated with approximately 7% lower GDP per capita in the long run. That is an estimated difference in the long-run income level—not a seven-percentage-point reduction in annual growth and not a prediction for every exporter. [4]

**Several mechanisms behind the pattern.** The growth association can arise through different channels: unstable public spending, weak investment decisions, declining non-oil competitiveness, rent seeking, or disruption from conflict. These channels need not appear together or carry equal weight in every country. A useful diagnosis therefore asks which mechanisms connect petroleum dependence to disappointing outcomes, rather than attributing every economic weakness to oil.

### How oil dependence damages budgets and economic stability

**Volatile receipts, persistent commitments.** Petroleum revenue changes with international prices, production volumes, costs, and the fiscal regime. Government commitments often adjust much more slowly: salaries, subsidies, pensions, and infrastructure operating costs continue after an oil-price boom ends. Treating a temporary increase in receipts as permanent income creates a mismatch between unstable financing and durable expenditure obligations. [5]

**Procyclical spending amplifies shocks.** Fiscal policy is *procyclical* when government actions reinforce rather than moderate the economic cycle. In an oil boom, the state expands spending, adding demand when activity is already strong; during a bust, it cuts expenditure when private activity is weakening. Public contractors then face the same cycle, expanding employment and investment during the boom and contracting sharply when government payments disappear. [6]

**Cross-country evidence of amplification.** Ter-Minassian’s 2012 review reports findings by Villafuerte and López-Murphy for 31 oil-exporting countries: fiscal policy was procyclical in most countries during rising oil prices in 2003–08 and remained procyclical in many during 2009. The review also found substantial differences across income groups and countries. The existence of a fiscal rule or resource fund did not, by itself, establish a clear relationship with countercyclical policy. [7]

**Borrowing can deepen the bust.** High oil prices can improve perceived creditworthiness and encourage governments to borrow against expected future receipts. If prices or production subsequently fall, revenue declines while debt service remains. NRGI identifies the debt crises experienced by Mexico, Nigeria, and Venezuela in the 1980s as examples of the danger of increasing expenditure and borrowing during favorable resource conditions. [1]

**Investment quality suffers during booms.** A sudden windfall does not instantly produce more engineers, procurement specialists, construction firms, or competent project managers. Rapid expenditure growth can therefore raise prices and encourage poorly prepared projects rather than increase useful infrastructure proportionately. When the boom ends, incomplete projects may be abandoned, while completed facilities lack maintenance budgets. This connects macroeconomic instability directly to public-investment performance. [6]

**Exhaustibility creates a separate challenge.** Even perfectly stable oil prices would not remove the problem that petroleum is finite. Revenue from extraction partly represents the conversion of an underground asset into cash, rather than a renewable addition to national income. Spending all those receipts without accumulating financial, physical, or human capital reduces the wealth available after production declines. [5]

**Stabilization and sustainability differ.** A government may smooth spending successfully for several years yet still consume its petroleum wealth too rapidly. Conversely, it may accumulate long-term savings while allowing disruptive annual spending changes. Avoiding the resource curse therefore requires both a short- to medium-term response to volatility and a long-term response to depletion.

### Institutional weaknesses and unequal economic benefits

**Rents can weaken accountability.** Resource rents are returns above the costs necessary to produce the resource. When governments receive substantial rents from a concentrated petroleum sector, they can become less dependent on taxing citizens broadly. One political-economy explanation is that this weakens the connection between taxation, public scrutiny, and demands for responsive government—especially when petroleum receipts and spending decisions are secret. [1]

**Competition shifts toward rent capture.** Large, concentrated revenue streams can make gaining control of public resources more attractive than building productive businesses. NRGI describes rent seeking and “rent seizing”: elites may pursue access to revenues, contracts, or regulatory privileges, and sometimes weaken checks on their authority to obtain them. The economic consequences include distorted investment incentives, corruption, and weaker public-service delivery. [1]

**Conflict destroys productive capacity.** Control over valuable resources can provoke or sustain internal conflict, while resource income can finance armed groups. NRGI’s 2015 reader reports that, since 1990, oil-producing countries had been twice as likely to experience civil war as non-oil-producing countries. Conflict can damage infrastructure, displace workers, discourage private investment, and divert public funds from development toward security expenditure. [1]

**Large output, limited employment.** Petroleum extraction is capital-intensive and often depends on specialized equipment and skills. Consequently, a large oil sector need not create broad employment opportunities or strong linkages with domestic suppliers. If other industries weaken at the same time, national export earnings can rise without a corresponding improvement in livelihoods for much of the population. [1]

**Environmental damage reduces net benefits.** Extraction can also impose costs on health, water, land, and existing livelihoods. These costs matter to the assessment of wealth management because public receipts are not the same as net social gains. A country can report substantial petroleum revenue while leaving affected communities worse off if environmental damage and compensation obligations are inadequately addressed. [1]

### Dutch disease: how oil inflows weaken other tradable sectors

**The narrower competitiveness mechanism.** *Dutch disease* describes the adverse effects that a resource boom can have on other tradable industries through real-exchange-rate appreciation and the movement of labor and capital. Tradable industries produce goods or services that compete internationally, including manufacturing and much of agriculture. Non-tradable activities, such as many construction and local services, primarily meet domestic demand. [8]

**Real appreciation raises relative costs.** A real appreciation means that domestic goods, services, or production costs become more expensive relative to foreign alternatives. It can occur through nominal currency appreciation, higher domestic prices and wages, or both. Thus, maintaining a fixed nominal exchange rate does not necessarily protect an oil exporter from Dutch disease: domestic inflation can still erode competitiveness. [6]

**Two interacting transmission channels.** The literature distinguishes a *resource-movement effect* from a *spending effect*. The first concerns production factors moving toward the booming resource sector; the second concerns the additional domestic demand generated when resource income is spent. Adam’s policy analysis explains both mechanisms and their consequences for non-oil industries. [3]

| Transmission channel | Initial change | Effect on other sectors | Main economic risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resource-movement effect | Oil-sector demand for labor and capital rises | Factor costs increase elsewhere | Non-oil production contracts |
| Spending effect | Oil income raises domestic demand | Non-tradable prices and wages rise | Tradable competitiveness weakens |
| Fiscal amplification | Government spends windfalls rapidly | Demand exceeds available capacity | Inflation and boom-bust investment |

**The resource-movement effect.** Expanding petroleum activity raises demand for relevant workers, investment, and services. Higher remuneration in oil-related activities can draw resources away from other producers or increase their costs. This effect depends on how strongly the petroleum sector competes for domestic inputs: an industry importing much of its equipment and specialist labor may transmit the boom differently from one with extensive local linkages. [3]

**The spending effect.** Government and private recipients spend part of the additional oil income on domestic goods and services. Demand for non-tradables increases, but their supply may be slow to expand, pushing up prices and wages. Producers of internationally traded goods generally have less freedom to raise prices because they compete with imports or sell at world-market prices; their margins are squeezed as domestic costs rise. [3]

**A reinforcing dependence cycle.** Manufacturing and agricultural exporters may lose market share, while imports become relatively attractive and construction or local services expand. If non-oil export capacity weakens, the economy becomes more reliant on petroleum for foreign exchange and public revenue. An oil-price decline then arrives in an economy with fewer alternative sources of activity and income.

**Documented sectoral consequences.** NRGI identifies Iran, Russia, Trinidad and Tobago, and Venezuela as cases where resource-related effects were associated with stunted or sharply declining manufacturing. Bauer also emphasizes reduced living standards among people disconnected from the resource sector. The distributional consequence is important: higher national income from oil does not guarantee that workers in displaced industries share the gains. [1][6]

### What follows from distinguishing the two concepts

**An outcome versus a mechanism.** The resource curse is the broader development problem; Dutch disease is one possible mechanism contributing to it. A country can experience corruption or fiscal crises without pronounced exchange-rate appreciation. It can also experience appreciation without a general development failure if it uses resource income productively and maintains strong institutions.

**Sectoral change requires interpretation.** Some movement toward a newly profitable industry is a normal response to changing economic opportunities. The relevant concern is not that the industrial structure changes at all, but whether the adjustment undermines durable productive capacity, broad employment, and welfare. The policy objective should therefore be a competitive, productive non-oil economy rather than preserving every existing industry regardless of its economic value.

**Different diagnoses imply different responses.** Volatility calls for expenditure smoothing and financial buffers. Depletion calls for preserving wealth in other forms. Dutch disease calls for managing the pace of domestic spending and improving supply capacity. Rent capture and weak accountability call for transparency, budgetary control, and effective oversight; a foreign investment portfolio does not directly solve those institutional problems.

**Evidence against inevitability.** NRGI reports that Norway, Indonesia, and the United Arab Emirates largely overcame Dutch-disease pressures over the preceding 25 years; it also names Chile, a copper-based comparison. The 2018 Dutch-disease survey similarly emphasizes policy, political regime, and institutional quality as determinants of vulnerability. These cases support a conditional judgment: petroleum wealth creates substantial risks, but policy choices influence their consequences. [1][8]

**The central economic task.** Avoiding the resource curse means stabilizing expenditure while converting depleting petroleum wealth into lasting financial assets, productive capacity, and accountable public services.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Scope**

Oil dependence is not oil production: dependence is commonly resource exports/GDP, while abundance is extraction income or rents [25]. One World Bank comparison used oil exports/total exports because fiscal/GDP shares were unavailable, excluded cases below 25% of exports and 5% of GDP, and measured gross rather than net revenue [28]. An EIA-based study defines oil-producing countries as producing more than they consume; its 1996–2022 sample is later than this report’s cutoff [27].

**Concepts and impacts**

The resource curse is the broader, conditional association between resource wealth and adverse development, fiscal, distributional or political outcomes—not an automatic effect; Ross (2015) is the cited pre-2021 survey [25]. Concentrated ownership or restricted access can channel rents to connected elites; volatility destabilizes growth, fiscal receipts and foreign exchange; rent-seeking, patronage, political selection, corruption and weak capacity can reduce public efficiency, education, health, transfers and non-resource taxation [25]. The review cites work on rent-seeking, inequality, corruption and resource specialization [25].

Dutch disease is narrower: resource exports appreciate the exchange rate, reducing non-resource employment and potentially manufacturing or agriculture; unemployment, inequality, poverty and low diversification may follow, although worker mobility and forward/backward linkages can mitigate it [25]. It is therefore a transmission channel within, not a synonym for, the resource curse.

Evidence is mixed. Descriptive 2014–18 data measured rents as a percentage of GDP: Malaysia’s enrolment exceeded 70% versus Chad’s approximately 20%; Egypt’s under-five mortality was below 30 per 1,000 live births versus Somalia’s about fivefold higher [25]. Comparability, sample-selection and identification limits weaken causal claims; health evidence is mixed, while linkages, regulation, taxation and redistribution can yield gains [25]. Nigeria’s 2004 benchmark-price rule saved excess receipts in a Central Bank account; about 70% of its 2000–05 windfall was spent amid inflation and real-appreciation pressure, while debt overhang dominated voracity after 1984. Sustainable design projects net oil income, spends permanent income and caps the non-oil deficit or debt with ring-fenced saving [28]. A later locator associates a 1% oil-price-uncertainty rise with a 0.74% investment fall during the 1997 and 2007–09 crises [27].

Oil-producing is broader than oil-dependent: IMF’s MENAP/CCA grouping names exporters and importers but sets no dependence threshold [30]. Screening therefore uses hydrocarbon dependence plus production per capita (barrels/person/year) and production/GDP; cases are illustrative. A 2020 classification separates highly resource-rich economies (extractive rents per capita in the 10th decile) from moderately resource-rich economies (8th–9th deciles), spanning Angola/Azerbaijan, Venezuela/Iran/Russia and Mexico/Indonesia/Nigeria [29][32]. Norway produced 1.77 million barrels/day; hydrocarbons were 23% of GDP in 2015, alongside state ownership, democratic governance and reserve accumulation [29].

Negative impacts include volatility, fiscal dependence, structural displacement and weak investment. Nigeria’s growth exceeded 10% in 1974 but contracted 8% in 1986; per-capita GDP fell from $1,456 in 1965 to $1,304 in 2000 (2010 dollars), while oil’s per-capita share became eight times higher. Capacity utilization fell from 77% in 1975 to 50% in 1983 and 35% in the 1990s; oil-funded investment reached 10–15% of GDP, yet Ajaokuta produced no steel by the late 1990s [29].

Azerbaijan’s oil and gas accounted for 93% of exports in the 2000s, more than 50% of GDP in 2007 and 72% of budget revenue in 2013; State Oil Fund transfers exceeded 50% of GDP in 2010–14. Its non-oil deficit reached 53.8% of non-oil GDP in 2013, production fell to 850,000 barrels/day in 2015, and the manat lost 57% by January 2017 after $15 billion in outflows and $5 billion in reserves [29]. The UAE combined oil revenues of up to 75% of total revenue with an Abu Dhabi deficit above 11% of GDP in 2016 and annual productivity declines of 0.1–0.5%; its sovereign funds exceeded $1 trillion, but replacing oil revenue would require tripling non-oil taxes. Iran’s oil represented 60% of exports and just under 50% of budget revenue in 2013; inflation reached 45% in October 2012 and growth was 0.4% in 2015 [29].

Norway is a qualified, not automatic, success: its fund was established in 1990, first paid in 1996, received hydrocarbon revenue net of the non-oil deficit, invested abroad, held over $875 billion in mid-2016 and grew 3.8% annually since 1999; nevertheless, FDI was 0.3–0.8% of GDP, R&D 1.4% versus over 2.2% in the OECD, and the source calls the results ambiguous [29]. Resource rents are not deterministic: rapid investment can exceed absorptive capacity, crowd out labour and capital, and raise depreciation; spending can appreciate the real exchange rate and shrink traded output, whereas efficient public investment can raise non-resource tradable productivity. Institutions and politics mediate these outcomes [32].

For the resource curse, sound institutions and robust public financial management can turn resource wealth into sustainable development rather than inevitable harm [75][104]; the management purpose is to strengthen rule of law and accountability and invest and diversify rents for the non-resource economy [46][49][93].

Dutch disease creates an oil-wealth management problem when a resource or export boom brings foreign exchange, appreciates the real exchange rate, raises demand and prices for non-tradables, and pulls labour and capital away from other tradables, reducing their competitiveness [113][68][105]. Management should therefore limit the domestic spending effect by holding fund assets abroad and applying fiscal rules to windfalls, while recognizing that lasting mitigation depends on institutional quality and cannot be secured by a temporary boom response alone [124][148][109].

For oil-dependent and oil-producing countries, Dutch disease is the specific economic channel: oil or export inflows appreciate the currency and weaken or contract non-resource tradables, whereas the resource curse is the broader set of economic, institutional, and political outcomes that can follow resource dependence [42][78][48]. The channel can contribute to the wider curse, but tests are heterogeneous and often inconclusive; the implication is to combine spending smoothing, productive public investment, and diversification with stronger institutions rather than assume a uniform effect [62][26][32].

Resource-curse risks for oil-producing countries are conditional rather than automatic: concentrated rents and weak institutions can generate inequality, poverty, corruption, and poor development, whereas sound institutions and public financial management can prevent those outcomes [25][75]. Dutch disease is the narrower exchange-rate channel in which higher oil revenue raises demand for non-tradables; this distinction makes oil-wealth management a matter of containing domestic spending and protecting the non-resource economy, not rejecting resource abundance [121][34].

Oil-dependent and oil-producing countries must also manage fiscal volatility and investment: commodity-price channels destabilize revenues, while windfall-funded public investment can face absorptive-capacity constraints, crowding out, and inefficiency, making public-investment management relevant [100][32]. Proposed responses include sterilising capital inflows, using natural-resource funds to invest oil income abroad, directing finance toward competitive tradables, and strengthening accountability and executive constraints [34][50].

The resource curse denotes adverse oil-dependent development outcomes such as slower growth, corruption, weak institutions, and conflict; Dutch disease is the specific currency-appreciation and competitiveness mechanism, and neither is automatic because institutional quality conditions the outcome [34][126][75].

For oil-producing countries, a resource fund can invest oil income abroad and sterilise capital inflows, helping reduce the Dutch-disease spending effect and support competitive tradables [34].

The resource curse is the broader, conditional development problem associated with resource abundance and weak growth or institutions; Dutch disease is a narrower macroeconomic channel in which resource exports appreciate the exchange rate and reduce non-resource competitiveness, so Dutch disease can be one route to resource-curse outcomes but is not synonymous with it [57][98][78].

## 2. Fiscal Policy Strategies: Three Rules for Managing Oil Revenues

### The three-rule comparison

**Three useful fiscal-rule families.** A source-supported way to organize the debate is to distinguish: the **permanent-income-hypothesis rule**, the **non-resource-primary-balance rule**, and the **expenditure-growth or smoothed-budget rule**. Bauer’s 2014 discussion uses these approaches when comparing fiscal frameworks for resource-rich economies with different production horizons and development needs. They are useful policy families rather than three mutually exclusive formulas: practical frameworks can combine elements of them. [6]

**A rule governs overall choices.** A fiscal rule places a multiyear constraint on government finances, normally through a numerical target or ceiling. It should determine how revenue translates into spending, saving, and borrowing across time. A fund’s deposit rule is narrower: requiring money to enter a particular account does not necessarily constrain the government’s consolidated expenditure or debt. [6]

| Main rule | Spending anchor | Treatment of oil receipts | Principal objective | Representative case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent-income hypothesis | Annuity from total petroleum wealth | Save receipts beyond sustainable income | Sustainability and intergenerational equity | Timor-Leste |
| Non-resource primary balance | Sustainable non-oil fiscal position | Separate receipts from spending decisions | Control fiscal dependence and demand | Norway’s related structural-balance approach |
| Expenditure growth or smoothed budget | Spending ceiling and underlying revenue | Save windfalls; finance temporary shortfalls | Predictable, stabilizing expenditure | Chile’s copper framework |

**The common underlying choice.** All three approaches reject the assumption that this year’s petroleum receipts should automatically determine this year’s expenditure. They differ in what replaces that assumption: a comprehensive wealth estimate, a sustainable non-oil fiscal position, or an expenditure path informed by underlying revenue. Their practical merits depend on forecast reliability, administrative capacity, development needs, and political adherence.

### Rule 1: The permanent-income-hypothesis rule

**An annuity from finite wealth.** The permanent-income-hypothesis, or PIH, approach treats resource revenue as proceeds from selling an exhaustible asset. It seeks to finance a sustainable stream of expenditure from the income generated by total petroleum wealth, rather than consuming receipts as they arrive. Total wealth includes accumulated net financial assets and the present value of expected future government petroleum receipts. [9]

**Stocks must become spending flows.** The present value of future petroleum revenue is a wealth stock, not an amount that can be spent again every year. In a simplified constant-return, perpetual-annuity representation, let \(A_t\) denote net financial assets, \(V_t\) the present value of future net government petroleum revenue, and \(r\) the real return or discount rate:

\[
W_t = A_t + V_t,\qquad C_t^{\mathrm{oil}} = rW_t
\]

**The arithmetic behind the rule.** A constant annual payment \(C\), received indefinitely at a constant positive real discount rate \(r\), has present value \(C/r\). Setting that value equal to wealth \(W\) gives \(C=rW\). This schematic formulation explains the economic logic; actual implementations must specify valuation assumptions, uncertainty, and the relevant measure of expenditure or transfers.

**Saving bridges the production horizon.** During high production or favorable prices, actual petroleum receipts can exceed the sustainable annual amount. The excess is accumulated in financial assets or used to improve the public balance sheet. When production declines, those accumulated assets help support expenditure after underground wealth has been depleted. The intended result is a smoother stream of benefits across time and generations. [6]

**Its strongest policy advantage.** The PIH approach directly addresses exhaustibility. A rule concerned only with annual expenditure growth may postpone a fiscal adjustment without ensuring that enough wealth remains after oil production ends. PIH instead asks whether today’s spending can be sustained over the full resource and saving horizon.

**Its central practical vulnerability.** Estimates of future petroleum wealth depend on prices, production, extraction costs, government revenue shares, and discount rates. An optimistic forecast can inflate calculated wealth and justify higher spending immediately. Bauer warns that governments can create apparent fiscal space by raising price or production assumptions, making independent scrutiny of the assumptions especially important. [6]

**Published estimates illustrate forecast sensitivity.** Budina and van Wijnbergen’s 2011 Azerbaijan study estimated oil-and-gas wealth at US$165 billion under February 2008 World Bank price forecasts, with an annual permanent-income equivalent of US$5.1 billion, both in constant 2007 dollars. Under older price forecasts, the corresponding estimates were US$143 billion and US$4.4 billion. These are modeled estimates showing how the same rule can produce different spending space when its assumptions change. [10]

**Development needs complicate strict saving.** Capital-scarce countries may have opportunities to improve electricity, water, transport, education, and health whose social returns exceed those available on foreign financial assets. Saving heavily abroad while such bottlenecks remain can impose substantial opportunity costs on the current population and future economic growth. The IMF debate therefore moved toward more flexible approaches that allow carefully managed investment scaling rather than treating a strict financial annuity as universally optimal. [6]

**Country examples and their meaning.** Adam’s 2010 policy brief identifies São Tomé and Príncipe and Timor-Leste as PIH examples, while distinguishing Nigeria, Gabon, and Ghana as countries for which the approach had been recommended. Timor-Leste provides a concrete operational illustration: Bauer reports a guideline limiting Petroleum Fund transfers to 3% of national petroleum wealth, with additional withdrawals requiring justification to parliament. These examples identify the design approach, not automatic evidence of uninterrupted compliance. [3][6]

**Best fit and deciding conditions.** PIH is particularly useful when depletion is approaching, wealth can be estimated credibly, and a government seeks a clear long-term sustainability benchmark. Its case becomes less decisive when high-return domestic investment is constrained by financing rather than project quality, or when uncertain future receipts dominate the wealth estimate.

### Rule 2: The non-resource-primary-balance rule

**Focus on the underlying budget.** The non-resource primary balance, or NRPB, excludes resource revenues and interest payments from the relevant fiscal balance. Its purpose is to reveal how much expenditure depends on petroleum financing, rather than allowing a temporary oil windfall to make an expansionary budget appear prudent. In a simplified non-oil expenditure formulation:

\[
\mathrm{NRPB}_t = R_t^{\mathrm{non\text{-}oil}} - G_t^{\mathrm{primary,\ non\text{-}oil}}
\]

**Why the exclusion matters.** An oil exporter can record an overall surplus while expanding expenditure much faster than its non-oil revenue base. If the oil-price increase is temporary, that surplus can conceal a fiscal position that will be difficult to finance later. Looking at the non-oil balance exposes this dependence and provides a more informative basis for setting the domestic fiscal stance. [11]

**The target needs an anchor.** Merely calculating the NRPB does not create a fiscal rule. The government must specify a target or adjustment path consistent with resource wealth, debt, development priorities, and economic capacity. Depending on national circumstances, the target can allow a sustainable non-resource deficit rather than requiring the non-oil budget to balance immediately.

**Norway’s related return-based application.** Norway is the representative case in Bauer’s discussion: the structural non-oil central-government deficit is linked to the expected long-run real return on the petroleum fund. Its operational indicator is a *structural non-oil deficit*, not literally the same measure as an unadjusted non-resource primary balance. “Structural” means that temporary influences, including cyclical conditions, are adjusted so that the indicator better reflects the underlying fiscal stance. [6]

**Revenue and expenditure become separated.** Under this approach, current petroleum receipts are saved through the fund, while the permitted contribution to the budget follows a sustainable anchor. A price spike can therefore increase assets without immediately authorizing equivalent extra spending. A temporary revenue decline need not force matching expenditure cuts if the fund and fiscal framework provide adequate financing.

**A conservative wealth base.** Norway’s return-based guideline focuses on accumulated financial wealth rather than immediately spending against the estimated value of all future extraction. This makes it more cautious than a comprehensive PIH calculation when large petroleum receipts are expected but have not yet been realized. The advantage is reduced reliance on uncertain underground-wealth forecasts; the trade-off is slower access to wealth during the early production period.

**Measurement still requires judgment.** Structural balances depend on estimates of economic capacity and temporary revenue influences. Ahmadov and colleagues’ 2018 study shows why both oil-price fluctuations and non-oil business cycles matter when interpreting fiscal positions. Their analysis of Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, and Russia also illustrates that similar headline balances can conceal different underlying fiscal stances. [11]

**Best fit and deciding conditions.** This family is useful when the principal objective is controlling petroleum-financed domestic demand and maintaining a sustainable non-oil budget. Its effectiveness depends on credible targets, transparent adjustments, sufficient buffers, and control over borrowing and off-budget expenditure—not simply publication of a non-oil balance.

### Rule 3: The expenditure-growth or smoothed-budget rule

**Start with a predictable path.** An expenditure rule limits the level or growth of government spending rather than allowing expenditure to follow volatile current receipts. It can be combined with a structural or smoothed-budget balance that estimates underlying resource revenue using a longer-term commodity-price assumption. This approach gives ministries and suppliers a more stable expenditure envelope. [6]

**A ceiling rather than a windfall.** A schematic real-expenditure-growth ceiling can be written as follows, where \(g^*\) is the chosen sustainable growth ceiling:

\[
G_t^{\mathrm{real}} \leq (1+g^*)G_{t-1}^{\mathrm{real}}
\]

**Saving follows the expenditure constraint.** If revenue rises faster than permitted expenditure, a surplus emerges. The government can save it in financial assets, repay debt, or reduce taxes. Bauer notes these alternatives explicitly, while warning that tax reductions based on a temporary windfall may create longer-term fiscal difficulties. The key stabilizing feature is that additional oil receipts do not automatically become additional spending. [6]

**Smoothing protects spending during downturns.** A smoothed-budget framework distinguishes actual receipts from a measure of underlying or permanent revenue. When commodity prices are temporarily high, receipts above that benchmark are saved; when prices fall temporarily, buffers can finance a deficit without immediate, disruptive cuts. This is different from requiring a zero headline deficit every year.

**Chile’s copper-based example.** Chile illustrates the structural-budget side of this family, rather than an oil-specific rule or a simple fixed expenditure-growth percentage. Its framework adjusts for economic conditions and copper prices, allowing spending to follow underlying revenue rather than the current copper-price cycle. Iacono describes the use of a long-term reference price, while the OECD’s 2019 account explains the role of independent expert committees in estimating copper prices and trend economic conditions. [9][12]

**Its strongest practical advantage.** Predictable expenditure supports multiyear projects, staffing, and maintenance planning. It also directly restrains the speed of spending increases, which is valuable where administrative and construction capacity cannot expand as quickly as petroleum revenue. In this respect, expenditure rules connect fiscal stabilization closely to absorptive capacity.

**Its principal sustainability weakness.** A growth ceiling does not necessarily preserve wealth after depletion, and a smoothed price assumption does not eliminate permanent changes in prices or production. If underlying revenue falls persistently, the expenditure path must eventually adjust. Otherwise, a framework designed to smooth temporary shocks can become a mechanism for running down assets or accumulating debt.

**Best fit and deciding conditions.** This approach is attractive when expenditure volatility and implementation capacity are the immediate problems, especially with a long production horizon. It needs a complementary assessment of debt and long-term resource wealth; predictable spending is not automatically affordable spending.

One country can combine a wealth-based transfer guideline, an expenditure ceiling, and a resource fund without fitting exclusively into a single category.

**Different labels emphasize different choices.** Adam also contrasts “hand-to-mouth,” “bird-in-hand,” and medium-term-price approaches. These labels describe alternative ways of classifying the same spending-and-saving problem; they do not change the three main families used above. Their value is to make the contrasts between spending current receipts, spending returns on realized wealth, and smoothing commodity revenue especially clear. [3]

**Norway crosses the classifications.** Norway can be described as bird-in-hand because its expenditure anchor is based on realized financial wealth. It can also be described through the non-resource structural balance because that is the fiscal indicator governing the use of fund resources. One label describes the wealth base; the other describes the budget variable.

### Choosing among the three approaches

**The decisive trade-off.** A strict wealth-preservation rule offers a clear defense against depletion but may postpone high-return development spending. An expenditure rule offers operational simplicity and predictability but needs a separate sustainability check. A non-resource-balance framework makes petroleum dependence visible, but its target must be justified and monitored.

**Country circumstances change the balance.** Bauer and the OECD organize the choice around development needs, productive capacity, and the production horizon. A capital-rich country nearing depletion has a stronger reason to accumulate financial assets; a capital-scarce country with long-lasting resources has a stronger case for productive domestic investment, provided spending remains manageable. [6][12]

| Economic setting | Stronger policy emphasis | Suitable fiscal orientation | Deciding condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ample capital; short resource horizon | Long-term financial saving | PIH or conservative wealth anchor | Sustainable investment returns |
| Scarce capital; short horizon | Balance investment and saving | Flexible wealth-based framework | Project quality and depletion speed |
| Ample capital; long horizon | Macroeconomic stabilization | Non-resource balance or smoothing | Exposure to revenue shocks |
| Scarce capital; long horizon | Productive domestic investment | Expenditure path with fiscal anchor | Absorptive capacity |

**Compliance outweighs the label.** Nigeria’s experience in the IMF’s 2012 account is instructive. An oil-price rule and stabilization fund helped constrain spending and accumulate buffers during 2004–07; after the arrangements lost traction in 2009–10, spending and reserve losses contributed to renewed inflation and exchange-rate pressure. The comparison demonstrates the importance of sustained operation, rather than isolating a universal causal effect of one instrument. [13]

**The fiscal-policy judgment.** The strongest rule is the one that credibly reconciles stabilization, depletion, and productive investment under national conditions, with transparent assumptions and enforceable controls.

**Fiscal-rule taxonomy and common logic**

**Intertemporal sustainable-income.** PIH limits resource-funded consumption to the return on the present value of future resource revenue; extraction receipts build non-resource capital for post-depletion returns [154]. It promotes intergenerational equity and stable spending, but uncertain reserve or price estimates can inflate fiscal space and early infrastructure or social needs may justify more spending [206]. Bird-in-hand is a variant; the evidence does not establish “return-on-assets” as a separate rule. It uses extracted wealth and accumulated assets, not oil underground, raises early saving and suits mature producers, but may constrain early consumption or become procyclical with market valuations. The cited description gives Norway’s version a smoothed 4% return estimate and a fund above 100% of GDP [135].

Practice is mixed: PI rules appeared in 6/17 countries in one survey and 4/11 in another; only Norway adhered strictly in the latter, while Azerbaijan, Russia and Timor-Leste suspended or exceeded transfers [135]. Kazakhstan’s rules, adopted in 2010 and modified in 2012, used an annual nominal $8bn fund transfer, a 4.5% debt-interest cap whose denominator is described as fund balance or imputed return, a 20% of projected-GDP minimum balance, a 15% of total receipts ten-year debt-service ceiling, a 3% of GDP non-oil-deficit target by 2020 and no off-budget finance; Decree 289 allowed $6.8–$9.2bn (±15%) according to the cycle [135]. The $8bn rule was PI-consistent: a 3% long-run return and 1970–2010 Brent data implied an $8.4bn annuity [135]. The fund was $27bn (21% of GDP) at end-2008 and over $64bn, with buffers above 32% of GDP, at end-2012; it limited exchange-rate volatility, although real appreciation was about 20% in 2000–08 [135]. Mexico supplies only a large oil hedge, an insurance alternative with no dates or operating details established here [154]. In Chad, no explicit stabilization account let savings return to the budget; the CEMAC rule, with a −1.5% of GDP target, stabilized finances rather than activity because borrowing was limited, whereas a rainy-day account could fund downturn withdrawals [154]. Simulations find flexible rules destabilizing under forecast errors and leaving fewer assets; a fixed escape-clause rule nearly matches an optimal rule [135].

**Expenditure/non-resource-primary-balance:** Ceilings/structural balances save booms, stabilize downturns and protect capital spending [148][208][209]. Peru: 1% GDP deficit/2% spending growth (2000–06); 3% consumption growth (2007–11); ≤1% structural deficit (2012–19); Poland used debt-triggered tightening (2013) [148][209]. Buffers/calibration are needed; current-spending bias and shock risk remain [148][209].

**Intertemporal sustainable-income (bird-in-hand variant):** Net wealth = financial plus underground resource wealth; the non-resource primary balance stabilizes it. Bird-in-hand spends asset returns; permanent income uses reserves, price and a 3% return (Kazakhstan annuity ≈$8.4bn) [148][207]. Fixed escape clauses limit forecast-error risk; the rule preserves wealth across generations but may mean low early spending or procyclicality; 6/17 countries (4/11 resource-rich) used it [207]. Kazakhstan’s National Fund: most oil receipts in and annual transfers out; $8bn fixed transfer since 2010 (~$11bn proposed), with a proposed 15% escape for two years; $27bn (21% GDP) at end-2008 and sterilized inflows [159][160][207][221]. Examples: Russia/Chile—procyclicality; Norway—Dutch disease; Norway/Timor-Leste—intergenerational equity; the roster is illustrative [148][209].

**Representative-country evidence**

- **Kazakhstan (2000–2020):** Its National Fund, created in 2000 and controlled by the Ministry of Finance with the National Bank as manager, combined stabilization and savings. Oil tax and non-tax receipts entered the Fund except crude-oil export customs, while guaranteed and targeted transfers financed the budget [218][224]. In 2017, 3.7% of GDP flowed into the Fund and transfers reached 8.3% of GDP; 2020 rules planned a 30% of GDP minimum balance and approximately US$6 billion guaranteed transfer, but the crisis prompted an additional US$4.7 billion, taking projected transfers above US$10.7 billion (17% of current assets) [219][223]. The country is therefore a supported long-run-price example [44], although withdrawals exceeding inflows create depletion risks; a permanent-income/debt-sustainability design was only an advisory option [170]. Transparency evidence conflicts: one account says quarterly reports were unpublished, while another reports external audits and published monthly and annual reports [218][223].

- **Russia (through 2020):** The Reserve Fund merged into the National Wealth Fund on 1 January 2018; deficit financing during 2015–2017 reduced it from RUB4.3 billion to RUB994 billion. The cited assessment finds that Russian funds were not long-term strategic investments [162].

- **Timor-Leste (through 2020):** The Petroleum Fund used Estimated Sustainable Income of 3% of total petroleum wealth; expenditures were budget transfers, fees and tax refunds, with Parliament required to approve transfers [222].

- **Ghana (2011–2020):** A Stabilization Fund is listed from 2011, but political pressure reportedly weakened such funds; Ghana’s petroleum-contract clause changed from broad stabilization to freezing [183][184][220]. ## 3. Revenue Management Tools: Natural Resource Funds and Public Investment Management Systems

### Natural Resource Funds: purpose and operation

**A dedicated revenue-management arrangement.** A Natural Resource Fund, or NRF, is an arrangement that sets aside revenue from nonrenewable resources for specified fiscal or investment purposes. It may be a separate institution with substantial operational autonomy or little more than a designated government account. NRFs form part of the broader sovereign-wealth-fund family, but not all sovereign wealth funds originate in oil or other natural resources. [5][7]

**Four connected management functions.** NRFs can stabilize the budget, preserve wealth for future generations, make revenue flows and assets more transparent, and—in development-oriented designs—finance priority socioeconomic investment. Their practical operation involves decisions about which receipts enter, when withdrawals occur, what assets are held, who makes decisions, and how performance is reported. [7]

**A buffer between receipts and expenditure.** The fundamental mechanism is intertemporal: a fund allows money received today to finance expenditure later. This can separate an oil-price boom from an immediate spending boom and provide financing when revenue falls. However, the decision about sustainable expenditure still belongs to the fiscal framework; the fund supplies the account, assets, and transfer mechanism.

**The three objective-based types.** For resource-revenue management, the IMF identifies three main purposes corresponding to **stabilization funds, savings funds, and development funds**. These are functional types: one institution may maintain portfolios serving more than one purpose. The IMF’s wider sovereign-wealth-fund taxonomy also contains arrangements with other origins and obligations. [13][14]

| NRF type | Primary objective | Typical deposit logic | Withdrawal purpose | Named illustration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilization | Cushion revenue shocks | Accumulate during favorable years | Support budgets during shortfalls | Chile’s copper-funded FEES |
| Savings | Preserve wealth across generations | Save a defined share or fiscal surplus | Sustainable long-term budget support | Norway’s GPFG |
| Development | Build productive domestic capacity | Allocate resource wealth to investment | Finance priority projects | Nigeria Infrastructure Fund |

### Type 1: Stabilization funds

**Insurance against leaner years.** Stabilization funds accumulate assets when resource revenue is abundant and make them available when receipts decline. Their principal purpose is to insulate the budget and economy from commodity-price volatility, usually involving oil in the IMF’s description. Deposits and withdrawals can be triggered by price, revenue, or fiscal-balance conditions. [14]

**The budget relationship matters.** A stabilization fund should finance an expenditure path determined within the budget, not independently choose public spending. If ministries receive predictable appropriations while the fund absorbs temporary revenue fluctuations, implementation becomes less vulnerable to commodity prices. Conversely, a withdrawal formula tied mechanically to every shortfall can exhaust the fund if a supposedly temporary downturn proves persistent.

**Liquidity before maximum return.** Stabilization assets must be accessible when public revenue falls. The OECD’s 2019 guidance therefore favors safe, liquid foreign assets, including suitable fixed-income securities and cash. Domestic investments are often a poor match because their value and liquidity may deteriorate at precisely the time that an oil-dependent government needs the fund. [12]

**Chile demonstrates purpose alignment.** Chile established its Economic and Social Stabilization Fund, FEES, in 2007 under its fiscal-responsibility framework. The OECD describes its integration with the budget and its use to finance deficits and counter external shocks after the global financial crisis. Its significance is the alignment of the fiscal objective, withdrawal arrangements, and liquid investment portfolio—not its applicability to oil alone, since Chile’s relevant resource is copper. [12]

### Type 2: Savings funds

**Replace underground wealth with assets.** Savings funds convert nonrenewable wealth into a diversified financial portfolio intended to benefit future generations or meet other long-term objectives. Deposits may follow a fixed revenue share, a fiscal-surplus rule, or a broader financing arrangement. Withdrawals are subordinate to maintaining a durable source of future income. [14]

**A longer investment horizon.** Unlike a dedicated stabilization reserve, a savings fund can generally accept more short-term market volatility if it does not need to liquidate assets quickly. This supports broader investment in equities and other long-horizon assets. The corresponding governance obligation is to establish credible risk limits and maintain political support through periods of poor returns. [12]

**Savings need a net-wealth perspective.** A growing fund does not necessarily mean that the public sector is becoming wealthier. If fund accumulation is offset by new debt or depletion of other public assets, the gross fund balance can give a misleading impression. The IMF emphasizes that liabilities elsewhere in the public sector can undermine a sovereign fund’s objectives. [14]

**Norway’s combined role.** The GPFG is an important savings example, but it also supports fiscal stabilization through budget financing. This illustrates why purpose and legal structure should be distinguished: a long-term savings portfolio can support a framework that allows temporary countercyclical spending without becoming a narrowly defined short-term stabilization fund.

### Type 3: Development funds

**Finance lasting productive capacity.** Development funds allocate resource wealth to priority socioeconomic projects, such as infrastructure. Their rationale is that financial saving abroad is not the only way to preserve wealth: useful domestic infrastructure and stronger human capital can also support income after petroleum production declines. The relevant test is whether spending creates lasting value rather than merely changing its accounting label. [13]

**Project selection becomes decisive.** Development-oriented funds require procedures for identifying projects, evaluating costs and benefits, authorizing financing, and monitoring delivery. An infrastructure allocation does not guarantee a productive asset, just as a completed building does not guarantee an effective public service. The development purpose therefore makes strong public-investment management especially important.

**Nigeria’s three-part illustration.** The OECD’s 2019 account describes the Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority as managing a Future Generations Fund, a Stabilisation Fund, and a Nigeria Infrastructure Fund. The infrastructure component invests domestically on commercial principles and seeks both financial and development returns, often through co-investment. The three components demonstrate how distinct policy objectives can be housed under one institutional umbrella. [12]

**A legitimate but demanding mandate.** Combining commercial returns and development outcomes creates harder evaluation problems than a single financial objective. A project might offer substantial public benefits but limited cash revenue; another might be commercially profitable without addressing a priority development constraint. Governance must specify how those objectives are assessed, rather than allowing a vague development mandate to justify weak investments.

**Budget coordination remains essential.** A development fund should not become a parallel spending authority that bypasses appraisal, procurement, parliamentary scrutiny, or consolidated fiscal reporting. The OECD warns that domestic sovereign-fund investment can fragment public investment and duplicate government programs if coordination and accountability are weak. [12]

### Reconciling classifications and making funds effective

**Financing describes another operating design.** Ter-Minassian uses a different three-part classification: stabilization, savings, and combined stabilization-and-savings or *financing funds*. A financing fund receives resource revenues and makes reverse transfers to finance the non-resource budget deficit. This is a description of the fund-budget relationship and can overlap with the objective-based types above; Norway is a prominent example. [7]

**The fiscal rule supplies discipline.** A financing fund makes the connection between the budget and asset accumulation explicit, but it does not independently determine an appropriate deficit. Likewise, stabilization and savings funds do not automatically constrain expenditure. The IMF treats them as complements to a sound medium-term fiscal framework, not substitutes for one. [5]

**Fungibility permits circumvention.** Public money is interchangeable. A government can comply with a deposit requirement while financing additional expenditure through borrowing, other accounts, or public entities. As a result, a fund can accumulate assets even while the consolidated fiscal position deteriorates. Effective oversight must therefore consider the whole public balance sheet rather than the fund in isolation. [5]

**Earmarking does not ensure additionality.** Requiring oil revenue to finance infrastructure may simply replace non-oil financing already allocated to infrastructure, freeing the displaced money for other uses. Bauer explains this problem directly. The meaningful questions are whether total investment rises appropriately, whether projects are valuable, and whether recurrent obligations remain affordable. [6]

**Unified budgeting protects accountability.** The IMF recommends coherent budget integration, parliamentary approval of fund-financed expenditure, consolidated reporting, and treasury execution of spending. A virtual fund within the budget can sometimes preserve the political benefits of a named reserve without introducing another institution. Institutional complexity should serve a clear purpose rather than become an end in itself. [5]

**Governance extends beyond disclosure.** A credible fund needs clear objectives, professional investment decisions, transparent appointment processes, an appropriate investment mandate, independent audit, and evaluation against its purpose. Transparency helps citizens distinguish a deliberately conservative stabilization portfolio from a long-term savings portfolio accepting more market risk. Without that distinction, pressure to maximize short-term returns can undermine the reason the fund exists. [12]

**The empirical judgment remains conditional.** Davis and colleagues’ 2001 IMF study found that some fund countries had less expenditure sensitivity, but fund establishment itself had no identifiable impact on government spending in their analysis. One interpretation was that more prudent governments were more likely to create funds; another was that funds helped sustain existing restraint. The practical conclusion is to evaluate rules and behavior, rather than infer fiscal discipline from the fund’s existence. [5]

### Public Investment Management Systems: why they matter

**Institutions governing the investment cycle.** A Public Investment Management System, or PIM system, comprises the institutions and processes governing public-investment planning, appraisal, selection, budgeting, implementation, monitoring, operation, and evaluation. It is broader than a project list, a capital budget, or a software platform. Its function is to ensure that public resources become useful assets and services. [15]

**Oil windfalls raise the stakes.** Resource-rich governments face pressure to increase investment quickly, often under uncertain future revenue conditions. Weak systems can turn additional financing into higher costs, unfinished projects, corruption, or infrastructure that does not address development needs. The IMF therefore treats public financial management and public-investment capacity as core institutions for resource-rich developing countries. [13]

**Efficiency substantially changes returns.** The IMF’s 2015 analysis of 134 countries estimated average public-investment inefficiency at around 30%, based on comparisons between public-capital inputs and infrastructure coverage and quality. The most efficient investors obtained approximately twice the growth return from public investment as the least efficient. These are cross-country efficiency comparisons, not an estimate that 30% of every oil-funded budget is stolen or wasted. [16]

**Absorptive capacity links micro and macro.** Better investment management helps determine how quickly expenditure can rise without exceeding the economy’s ability to deliver useful outputs. A government lacking a prepared project pipeline may save temporarily while strengthening appraisal and implementation. Where high-quality projects and delivery capacity exist, domestic investment can remove bottlenecks and support non-oil competitiveness. [6]

**The development trade-off becomes concrete.** Comparing foreign financial returns with the social return on domestic investment only makes sense when the domestic projects are well selected and maintained. A nominally high-return electricity project delivers little value if procurement fails or fuel, staffing, and maintenance are unfunded. PIM turns the saving-versus-spending debate from an abstract allocation argument into a test of actual project quality.

### The eight features of an effective investment cycle

**Eight features, with embedded monitoring.** Rajaram and colleagues identify eight “must-have” features of a functioning public-investment system. They span project origination through evaluation, with monitoring and control embedded in implementation and subsequent review. The framework identifies essential safeguards rather than prescribing an identical administrative organization for every country. [15]

| Feature | Main question | Essential control | Decision or output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Guidance and origination | Which problem needs investment? | Preliminary screening | Credible project pipeline |
| 2. Formal appraisal | Is the project worthwhile? | Costs, benefits, risks, alternatives | Appraisal decision |
| 3. Independent review | Is the appraisal credible? | Separate quality assessment | Validated or revised appraisal |
| 4. Selection and budgeting | Is it a priority and affordable? | Budget and multiyear integration | Authorized financing |
| 5. Implementation and monitoring | Is delivery on track? | Procurement, reporting, control | Verified progress |
| 6. Project adjustment | Should the project change? | Reappraisal and authorized decisions | Continue, revise, or stop |
| 7. Facility operation | Will the asset provide services? | Staffing and maintenance | Functioning public asset |
| 8. Completion and evaluation | Were benefits achieved? | Review, audit, ex-post assessment | Accountability and learning |

**Guidance and project origination.** The cycle begins with identifying a public-service problem and considering possible responses, not simply announcing a construction project. Strategic guidance should help ministries develop proposals consistent with development priorities, while preliminary screening rejects unsuitable or poorly justified ideas before expensive appraisal. This is especially important during oil booms, when political demand for visible projects can overwhelm analytical capacity. [15]

**Project choice before formal appraisal.** Dobes’s 2013 discussion identifies a weakness in relying too heavily on broad “nation-building” visions: projects selected for appraisal may not be the most welfare-enhancing alternatives. Efficiently delivering the wrong project is still a poor use of resources. Origination should therefore examine alternatives, including improving existing services or maintaining existing infrastructure rather than automatically building new facilities. [17]

**Formal project appraisal.** Appraisal evaluates the proposal’s justification, costs, benefits, risks, and alternatives before selection. For oil-funded investment, it should consider uncertain financing, implementation capacity, and lifetime operating obligations. Economic appraisal also differs from a commercial-return calculation: public projects can create important social benefits that do not appear as cash revenue to the implementing agency. [15]

**Independent review of appraisal.** A separate review challenges the project sponsor’s assumptions and helps counter optimism about costs, implementation time, or demand. Independence means a credible quality check outside the sponsoring team’s immediate incentives; it need not require the same organizational model everywhere. The reviewer should be able to return a weak proposal for revision or recommend rejection before political and financial commitments become entrenched. [17]

**Selection linked to budgeting.** Projects should compete for resources within an affordable medium-term expenditure framework. Approval needs to account for multiyear construction commitments, existing projects, and future operating costs rather than only the first annual allocation. Dabla-Norris and colleagues emphasize that weak integration between appraisal, selection, and the budget can encourage unsustainable borrowing and inadequate asset maintenance. [18]

**Implementation with continuous monitoring.** Delivery requires clear responsibilities, timely funding, competent procurement, contract management, and financial control. Monitoring should connect spending to physical progress, timetable, and emerging risks rather than record disbursement alone. Dabla-Norris and colleagues associate weak organizational arrangements, reporting, monitoring, and procurement with chronic under-execution, rent seeking, and corruption. [18]

**Adjustment when conditions change.** Projects need a formal route for revising cost, scope, schedule, or implementation arrangements. Monitoring has limited value if unfavorable findings do not trigger corrective decisions. Adjustment can include stopping a project whose remaining benefits no longer justify its remaining costs; money already spent is not, by itself, a reason to continue. [15]

**Operation and maintenance after construction.** A completed asset must have an operator, staff, supplies, and maintenance financing. For a road, that means preserving usability; for a hospital, it means functioning clinical services rather than an empty building. Maintenance is particularly vulnerable when oil revenue falls, so its funding should be considered during selection rather than left until construction ends. [15]

**Completion review, evaluation, and audit.** Completion review establishes what was delivered and at what cost; later evaluation tests whether the intended services and benefits materialized. Financial and external audits address compliance and accountability, while asset registers support continuing management. Dabla-Norris and colleagues include timely external audit, legislative scrutiny, ex-post evaluation, and asset information within the evaluation phase. [18]

### From formal procedures to effective investment

**Broad phases and detailed safeguards.** The IMF’s 2012 framework presents four broad PIM stages: appraisal, selection, implementation, and evaluation. The World Bank’s eight features provide the finer detail needed to locate origination, independent review, adjustment, and operation within that cycle. These are compatible levels of aggregation, not competing claims about whether those functions matter. [13][15]

**Bottlenecks vary across systems.** A government can select reasonable projects but still perform poorly in implementation and evaluation. Lassourd and Bauer’s 2014 Uganda analysis identified that pattern in a prospective oil-producing economy. The implication is that additional appraisal procedures alone will not solve a delivery bottleneck; reforms must address the stage actually preventing public expenditure from becoming productive services. [19]

**Formal design is not enough.** The IMF’s 2018 review of 30 Public Investment Management Assessments found that institutional design generally looked stronger than effectiveness. It also found that centralized monitoring rarely produced corrective action. This explains why an impressive set of written procedures can coexist with persistent cost overruns, delays, and weak asset management. [20]

**Funds and PIM protect different things.** A well-designed NRF protects the timing, custody, and investment of financial resources. A PIM system protects the allocation and productive use of resources released for domestic investment. A country with a strong fund but weak PIM can save prudently and spend poorly; a country with strong project appraisal but unstable financing can repeatedly interrupt valuable projects.

**The combined institutional judgment.** Fiscal rules determine a sustainable expenditure envelope, resource funds provide the associated saving and financing mechanism, and PIM determines whether domestic investment delivers lasting value.

## 4. Benchmark Case Study: Norway’s Oil Wealth Management

### The policy package and its development

**A substantial public wealth transformation.** Norway’s oil fund reached a market value of NOK 10,000 billion on 25 October 2019, a milestone reported in NBIM’s August 2020 institutional history. The same publication reports that fund transfers financed around 17% of government expenditure in 2019. These indicators show how petroleum wealth had become a significant financial resource supporting the budget, rather than only a stream of current extraction receipts. [2]

**Rent capture comes before saving.** Norway’s framework began with securing a substantial public share of petroleum profits. NBIM’s 2020 history describes a petroleum-profit tax rate of 78%, subject to depreciation, amortization, and other tax rules. This is a tax on the relevant profit base, not a claim that the state receives 78% of gross production value. [2]

**Ownership adds another revenue channel.** The government also participates through the State’s Direct Financial Interest, managed by Petoro, and state ownership in the petroleum company identified as Statoil in the historical sources. The Arctic Institute’s 2013 account reported a 67% state interest in Statoil. Taxation, direct participation, and company ownership therefore supplied complementary channels for capturing resource income. [21]

**The framework evolved gradually.** Norway did not move directly from discovery to a fully funded global portfolio. A fund was formally proposed in 1983, legally established in 1990, and first received capital in 1996. The later development of specialized investment management, fiscal guidelines, and a broader investment universe followed as the scale and horizon of savings became clearer. [2]

| Period | Principal development | Institutional significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1983 | Formal fund proposal | Separate volatile receipts from spending |
| 1990 | Petroleum Fund Act | Establish the legal saving framework |
| 1996 | First transfer, approximately NOK 2 billion | Begin actual financial accumulation |
| 1998 | NBIM and equity investment | Professionalize and broaden management |
| 2001 | Expected-return fiscal guideline | Anchor petroleum-financed expenditure |
| 2005 | Government Pension Fund legislation | Establish the pension-fund umbrella |
| 2017 | Decision for a 70% equity allocation | Adapt strategy to the long horizon |
| 2019 | Explicit intergenerational purpose | Recognize current and future beneficiaries |
| 2020 | Published 3% fiscal-rule formulation | Use the expected-real-return anchor |

The timeline follows [2][22], and NRGI’s Norway profile [23].

**A savings reserve, not individual pensions.** The Government Pension Fund structure placed the global petroleum savings vehicle alongside the domestic Government Pension Fund Norway. Despite the global fund’s name, NRGI’s 2013 profile explicitly described it as having no formal pension obligations. Its role is broader public saving and long-term budget support, rather than maintaining individual pension accounts. [23]

### How Norway’s fiscal rule operates

**A return-based expenditure anchor.** Norway’s guideline links the structural non-oil central-government deficit to the GPFG’s expected long-run real return. The original 2001 formulation used 4%; NBIM’s 2020 history presents the framework as the “3 percent rule.” In economic terms, the percentage applies to fund capital to estimate a sustainable annual contribution to the budget. It is not 3% of petroleum revenue, GDP, or the fund’s annual return itself. [23][2]

**The structural deficit is central.** Excluding petroleum receipts prevents current oil prices from determining the apparent room for expenditure. Structural adjustment then distinguishes the underlying use of petroleum wealth from temporary budget effects. The result is a fiscal anchor for the government’s contribution to domestic demand, rather than a requirement that every annual cash transfer equal a mechanically fixed amount.

**A simplified operating expression.** If \(F\) represents GPFG capital for purposes of the guideline and \(r^*\) the assumed long-run real return, the underlying relationship is:

\[
D^{\mathrm{structural,\ non\text{-}oil}} \approx r^*F,\qquad r^* = 0.03\ \text{in the 2020 formulation}
\]

**The scale of the adjustment.** An illustrative estimate using the observed NOK 10,000 billion fund milestone from October 2019 gives NOK 300 billion annually at 3%: \(0.03 \times 10{,}000\). The old 4% assumption applied to the same capital gives NOK 400 billion: \(0.04 \times 10{,}000\). Holding the capital base constant, the difference is NOK 100 billion, or 25% of the old amount; these calculations illustrate the rule’s scale rather than reproduce a budget appropriation. [2]

**Expected return is not current income.** The rule does not require spending only the dividends, interest, or rent received during that particular year. It uses an expected real total return over time. Consequently, the fund can experience a negative market year without implying that the budget contribution must immediately fall to zero; equally, an unusually strong year does not justify spending the entire gain.

**Preservation is an objective, not a guarantee.** Spending expected returns is intended to maintain the real value of accumulated wealth over time. Actual outcomes depend on realized returns, withdrawals, and the evolution of the asset base. Sustained spending above sustainable returns would eventually consume capital, while persistently lower expected returns would justify reconsidering the spending assumption.

**Flexibility supports stabilization.** The guidelines allow temporary deviations. Bauer identifies circumstances including an economy operating well below capacity and large changes in fund value. This flexibility helps avoid forcing abrupt expenditure adjustments during recessions or major financial-market movements. It is compatible with the long-run anchor, provided deviations do not become a permanent expansion of spending commitments. [6]

**Political commitment and legal authority differ.** The numerical fiscal guideline is not legally binding, but NBIM’s 2020 history emphasizes its broad parliamentary backing. By contrast, the legal framework governs where petroleum income goes and how fund resources can be transferred to the budget. Norway therefore combines a politically sustained expenditure norm with legally structured custody and authorization arrangements. [2]

### GPFG revenue flows and budget integration

**All net petroleum cash flow.** The fund’s income includes the government’s net petroleum cash flow—principally petroleum taxes and income from direct state participation—plus returns on its financial assets. The detailed deposit framework also encompasses relevant petroleum dividends and proceeds from sales of state petroleum equity, with petroleum-related investment and operating expenses deducted in calculating net cash flow. [2][23]

**Withdrawals only through the budget.** The fund is integrated into central-government finances rather than operating as a separate domestic spending agency. Transfers out require a parliamentary decision and go to the central-government budget. Ministries and public programs therefore compete within the normal budget process instead of obtaining independent allocations directly from the investment manager. [22]

| Flow | What moves | Governing arrangement | Economic function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petroleum sector to state | Taxes, ownership income, other receipts | Petroleum fiscal regime | Capture resource income |
| State to GPFG | Net petroleum cash flow | Fund legislation | Convert petroleum into financial assets |
| Portfolio to GPFG | Investment returns | Management mandate | Build long-term wealth |
| GPFG to central budget | Non-oil deficit financing | Parliamentary authorization | Support public expenditure |
| Budget to domestic uses | Approved spending | Budget institutions | Deliver services and investment |

**Gross deposits versus net accumulation.** Saving all net petroleum receipts in the fund does not mean that the entire annual amount remains invested after budget transfers. If \(P_t\) denotes net petroleum cash flow and \(D_t^{\mathrm{non\text{-}oil}}\) the non-oil budget deficit financed from the fund, the net fiscal transfer is:

\[
N_t = P_t - D_t^{\mathrm{non\text{-}oil}}
\]

**The budget determines the net flow.** If petroleum receipts exceed the budget’s use of petroleum wealth, the fund receives a net fiscal inflow; if budget use is greater, there is a net fiscal withdrawal. Investment performance and currency valuation changes then also affect the fund’s market value. This explains why a financing fund can receive petroleum revenue while simultaneously transferring money to the budget.

**Actual and structural balances differ.** The cash transfer finances the actual non-oil deficit, whereas the spending guideline is assessed through the structural non-oil deficit. Temporary economic conditions can make those measures differ. Keeping them distinct is essential to understanding how the fund can support stabilization without interpreting every fluctuation in withdrawals as a change in the long-term spending rule.

**Integration limits borrowing around the fund.** NBIM’s 2020 institutional history explains that the non-oil central-government deficit is financed by fund withdrawals rather than borrowing to finance that deficit while retaining fund capital. The important economic principle is consolidated control: the government should not present growing fund assets as saving while separately borrowing to expand the same budget expenditure. [2]

### Ownership, delegated management, and oversight

**A clear chain of responsibility.** Norway separates political decisions about public wealth from operational investment management. The Storting establishes the legal framework and authorizes spending; the Ministry of Finance has overall responsibility and sets the mandate; Norges Bank manages the fund, with NBIM responsible for day-to-day investment operations. [22]

| Institution | Principal responsibility | Main accountability mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Storting | Legislation, budget, major strategic scrutiny | Public parliamentary decisions |
| Ministry of Finance | Overall responsibility and management mandate | Reporting and parliamentary oversight |
| Norges Bank Executive Board | Govern delegated investment management | Supervision, reporting, internal controls |
| NBIM | Day-to-day portfolio management | Mandate compliance and performance reporting |
| Supervisory Council | Supervise Norges Bank’s compliance | Reports to the Storting |
| Council on Ethics | Advise on observation and exclusion | Decisions by Norges Bank’s Executive Board |
| Internal and external audit | Examine controls and financial reporting | Independent assurance and reporting |

The roles are documented in Matsen’s 2017 speech [22] and NRGI’s Norway governance profile [23].

**Political control over strategic risk.** The Ministry’s mandate sets the investment objective, benchmark, permissible universe, and limits on deviations. Major investment-strategy changes are presented to and discussed in Parliament. This means that the public authorities retain responsibility for the broad level of financial risk rather than attributing all outcomes to portfolio managers. [2]

**Operational independence within the mandate.** Matsen’s 2017 account states that the Ministry does not issue instructions on individual investments or the exercise of ownership rights. This limits short-term political intervention in security selection while preserving democratic authority over objectives and constraints. Independence is therefore delegated and bounded, not an exemption from accountability. [22]

**The principal-agent problem.** Delegation creates an information imbalance: the investment manager knows more about portfolio decisions and risks than the public owner. Norway addresses this through a detailed mandate, benchmark-based measurement, reporting, supervision, and controls. The objective is to preserve professional expertise without allowing the manager’s greater information to weaken public control. [22]

**Supervision runs back to Parliament.** The parliament-elected Supervisory Council monitors Norges Bank’s operations and compliance, including fund management. Matsen reports that, since 2010, the Council had reported supervisory results directly to the Storting. He also describes an internal audit function, an Audit Committee, and an external auditor as part of the strengthened control structure. [22]

**Multiple assurance functions.** NRGI’s Norway profile identifies regular internal and independent external audits, with the external auditor reporting to the Supervisory Council. It also describes the Auditor General’s role in auditing and reporting within the public accountability system. These mechanisms address different responsibilities: supervision checks whether the framework is respected, while audit provides assurance over reporting and controls. [23]

**Ethical decisions have assigned authority.** Norway’s investment constraints include ethical guidelines and observation or exclusion of companies. Matsen’s 2017 account specifies that Norges Bank’s Executive Board decides on observation and exclusion on advice from the Council on Ethics. This provides a defined institutional route for ethical restrictions rather than leaving exclusions to ad hoc political intervention. [22]

**Public reporting sustains legitimacy.** Reporting includes the fund’s size, transfers, returns, asset allocation, and investment-management information. Parliamentary scrutiny remained visible in 2020: NBIM’s notice for 30 October identifies an annual hearing on fund management involving Executive Board Chair Øystein Olsen and NBIM CEO Nicolai Tangen. [23][24]

### Investment mandate, foreign diversification, and risk

**Long-term return with moderate risk.** The investment objective is the highest possible long-term real return with moderate risk, within the Ministry’s mandate. This supports the fiscal framework by seeking a durable return on accumulated petroleum wealth. It does not give the manager a general mandate to pursue domestic industrial policy, regional development, or short-term budget objectives. [22]

**Foreign investment protects the mainland economy.** The GPFG’s assets are invested outside Norway. This both diversifies national wealth beyond the domestic petroleum economy and limits the immediate domestic demand pressure associated with spending petroleum receipts. Foreign saving does not eliminate all oil-sector effects, but it reduces the direct conversion of every revenue windfall into domestic expenditure. [23]

**The asset universe broadened over time.** Initial capital was invested in government bonds. Equities entered the portfolio from 1998, corporate and international-organization bonds were added from 2002, and real-estate investment began in 2011. In 2017, authorities decided to increase the strategic equity allocation to 70%, reflecting the fund’s long investment horizon and capacity to bear market fluctuations. [22]

| Asset category | Development in the framework | Intended contribution | Important exposure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed income | Initial portfolio; later broader bond universe | Income and portfolio diversification | Interest-rate and credit risk |
| Listed equities | Introduced from 1998 | Long-term growth participation | Market-price volatility |
| Real estate | Investment began in 2011 | Broader asset diversification | Valuation and liquidity risk |

**The benchmark dominates exposure.** The Ministry sets the benchmark and limits on departures from it, including tracking-error controls. Tracking error measures the expected variability of returns relative to the benchmark; it is not a ceiling on total losses. NBIM’s 2020 history states that the benchmark explained around 99% of historical variation in total fund returns, underscoring the importance of strategic asset allocation. [22][2]

In 2020, the Ministry’s mandate aimed for expected relative volatility (tracking error) no higher than 1.25 percentage points; expected relative volatility was 0.56 percentage point at year-end, while the benchmark largely determined portfolio return and risk.

**Diversification does not remove market risk.** A globally diversified portfolio reduces exposure to individual firms, sectors, and countries, but it remains vulnerable to broad market movements. As the fund grows, those movements can become a significant influence on the fiscal outlook. Norway therefore exchanges part of its concentrated petroleum exposure for diversified—but still risky—financial exposure.

**Domestic investment remains a budget choice.** Norway’s foreign-investment restriction does not mean that the government cannot finance domestic infrastructure, education, or health. It means that such choices occur through the budget, using resources released under the fiscal framework, rather than through GPFG portfolio decisions. This separation preserves the distinction between maximizing financial returns on savings and selecting public expenditure for social purposes.

### Why the Norwegian arrangements reinforce each other

**Revenue capture feeds a durable vehicle.** Petroleum taxation and state participation create public receipts; the fund converts those receipts into financial assets. Neither component would be sufficient alone. A well-managed fund with little revenue capture would accumulate less public wealth, while substantial tax receipts without expenditure discipline could still finance a boom-bust cycle.

**The rule governs the fiscal impulse.** The expected-return anchor moderates how quickly petroleum wealth enters the mainland economy. External investment then holds the unspent portion abroad rather than channeling it into additional domestic demand. Unified budgeting ensures that permitted spending is considered alongside the government’s other priorities.

**Oversight makes delegation sustainable.** Professional investment management depends on the ability to make operational decisions without continual political interference. That autonomy is more credible when the mandate is public, results are disclosed, and independent institutions can investigate compliance. Transparency also helps maintain support when a long-term portfolio experiences short-term losses.

**Institutions interact with favorable conditions.** Matsen’s 2017 assessment explicitly recognizes that Norway benefited from high oil prices during a period of substantial production. The fund’s success should therefore not be attributed exclusively to institutional design or investment skill. The stronger conclusion is that the institutions enabled favorable revenue conditions to be converted into lasting public assets and helped manage less favorable conditions. [22]

**The fund cannot supply political will.** NBIM’s 2020 history recalls the 1983 commission’s warning that a fund could encourage long-term policymaking but could not replace political authorities’ willingness to act on long-term considerations. This remains the central boundary of the Norwegian model: formal arrangements must be sustained by political behavior. [2]

### Lessons for other oil-dependent countries

**Transfer functions rather than percentages.** Other producers can adopt Norway’s separation of revenue collection, budget allocation, and investment management without copying its tax rate or spending percentage. Those numerical choices depend on resource characteristics, investment conditions, fiscal needs, and risk tolerance. The transferable principle is a coherent allocation of responsibilities and a credible consolidated fiscal constraint.

**Development conditions change the allocation.** The OECD treats Norway as a capital-rich economy with a strong case for financial saving. A poorer producer may have a stronger case for financing productive infrastructure or human capital. That changes the appropriate saving share, but not the need for stabilization buffers, public accountability, and rigorous investment selection. [12]

| Transferable principle | Norwegian arrangement | Decision for another producer |
|---|---|---|
| Capture public resource value | Petroleum taxation and ownership | Design a credible rent-capture regime |
| Separate receipts from spending | Fund deposits plus budget transfers | Choose a sustainable fiscal anchor |
| Match assets to purpose | Long-term foreign investment | Distinguish liquidity from long-term saving |
| Preserve unified public control | Parliamentary budget authorization | Prevent parallel or off-budget spending |
| Delegate with accountability | Ministry mandate and professional manager | Establish clear roles and independent oversight |
| Convert spending into benefits | Domestic choices through the budget | Apply the complete PIM cycle |

**A bounded benchmark judgment.** Norway’s model becomes less convincing as a guide if a country accumulates fund assets while borrowing around the rule, permits opaque withdrawals, or gives investment managers conflicting political mandates. Conversely, a different institutional structure can achieve the same objectives if it credibly stabilizes expenditure, preserves wealth, and delivers productive public investment. The economic functions matter more than the institution’s name.

**Evidence and measurement basis.** This report uses public and academic publications issued by 31 December 2020; historical observations retain their stated dates. Fiscal-rule and fund classifications describe functions, not exclusive legal categories. The equations simplify the underlying policy logic, and the NOK 300 billion and NOK 400 billion amounts are illustrative estimates from sourced anchors. Cross-country associations and institutional case studies support conditional judgments rather than a causal counterfactual for Norway.

**The final policy conclusion.** Oil wealth avoids becoming a curse when governments control both how much they spend and what that spending achieves. Norway’s strongest lesson is the connection among disciplined fiscal choices, a budget-integrated investment fund, professional management, and public oversight. For capital-scarce producers, equally strong public-investment management is necessary to ensure that spending rather than saving also leaves a durable inheritance.

**Chronology and policy formation**

Oil production generated positive income in the early **1980s**. In **1981**, a change of government brought deregulation and limited liberalization that helped attract investment into hydrocarbons, while the subsequent model retained a large welfare state but emphasized fiscal responsibility and financial discipline [60]. A later retrospective reports that foreign companies initially managed Norway’s oil activities, that operating and property rights were subsequently transferred to domestic companies, and that Norway faced current-account pressures in the **1970s** before the **1980s** oil shock increased petroleum revenues [236]. These facts qualify the chronology: the institutional response developed before the fund and fiscal rule were fully operational.

Norway established the Government Petroleum Fund in **1990**, accumulated its first net assets in **1996**, implemented the bird-in-the-hand spending rule in **2001**, and renamed the fund the Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG) in **2006** [235]. A World Bank note additionally records **1996** as the point at which the fund became effective when Norway moved into fiscal surplus, and reports that it became the world’s largest sovereign wealth fund in **2011** [258]. A 2016 comparative review characterizes Norway’s experience as a decade-long effort to establish fiscal institutions and rules for managing resource rents, with reported gains in macroeconomic and financial stability, growth, and equity [243].

**Fiscal rule and fund flows**

Norway’s rule was initially described as a **4%** annual reference: the government aimed to use the estimated long-term real return on the GPFG, and four percent of the fund’s previous-year value served as a reference for financing the non-oil deficit from **2001** [235]. The rule applied to the **cyclically adjusted or structural non-oil deficit**, not mechanically to the contemporaneous deficit, and allowed deviations for countercyclical policy while retaining a medium-term anchor [235]. A later retrospective records a revised estimated annual real-return parameter of **3% from 2017** [257]; therefore, the historical 4% formulation should not be presented as the unchanged operative parameter through 2020.

**Institutional chain, investment and assessment**

The fund was invested abroad to protect Norway’s domestic economy, diversify risk, and maximize returns, thereby reducing overheating and deindustrialization pressures [235]. A **2012** description reports a strategic benchmark of **60% equities**, **35–40% fixed income**, and **0–5% real estate**, with ownership in an individual company capped at **10%** and ethical guidelines applying [235]. Another account reports fund assets of **1.5 times Norwegian GDP** and ownership of more than **1% of publicly traded shares worldwide**, while noting that the source does not provide a comparable 2020 observation date [60].

Norway also retained exposure to depletion and Dutch-disease mechanisms. An estimate for **2008** lists **6.87 billion barrels** of proved oil reserves, production of **2.79 million barrels per day**, and a calculated reserve life of **7 years** [256]. A later study using quarterly data for **Norway from 1999Q1–2019Q4** reports Dutch-disease symptoms, including negative relationships involving oil-related variables, government spending, the real exchange rate, and manufacturing, although foreign direct investment was positively related to manufacturing [236]. Norway’s benchmark consequently demonstrates containment of volatility and preservation of wealth—not immunity from petroleum dependence or an absence of structural trade-offs.

Norway separated petroleum receipts from immediate spending. Its first oilfield was discovered in 1969; excessive domestic oil spending in the 1970s–80s and the 1967 national-insurance fund’s experience informed the design. The Tempo Committee proposed a fund invested abroad, with receipts returned at a fiscally suitable pace. An oil fund was announced in 1989, established by law in 1990, activated in 1995, first funded in 1996, and renamed GPFG in 2006. [253][249]

Norway’s Government Petroleum Fund Act no. 36 (22 June 1990) created a government account at Norges Bank; its first allocation came in 1996 after a fiscal surplus, and it became the Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG) in 2006. Net central-government petroleum receipts enter the fund, while budget-authorized transfers finance the non-oil deficit; the fund has no independent spending authority. Norway’s fund legal framework—the 1990 Act and 2001 White Paper—therefore separates saving from expenditure authorization. The cited evidence describes stringent transparency and accountability, but does not establish separate audit or ethics details. [246][250]

Outcomes are mixed. Government savings were 10–25% of mainland GDP in 2000–15, averaging 16.3%, versus 10.4% for private savings; one source reports end-2017 GPFG assets of 8.5 billion NOK, equal to 280% of mainland GDP, allocated 66.6% to equities, 2.6% to real estate and 30.8% to fixed income. [246] The non-oil deficit was 3.3% of mainland GDP in 2008 and 5.2% in 2009 without exceeding the rule, but fiscal policy was procyclical during 2012–14 and reached 5.8% in 2014. [246] Another study finds that spending, employment and wages became more responsive to oil-price shocks after 2001, although the framework likely moderated spending relative to “spend-as-you-go.” [237] A later account reports that, in March 2020, 71% of NBIM assets (US$814 billion) were listed equities; losses reached 21% (US$172 billion) by 31 March, with two-thirds recovered by 30 June. [261] Norway’s success therefore remains conditional on governance, accountability and political will; transparency alone is insufficient. [254]
