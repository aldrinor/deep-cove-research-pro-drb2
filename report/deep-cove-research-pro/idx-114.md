# Competition in Public Cloud Computing: IaaS and PaaS as of Early 2025

**Public cloud infrastructure is highly concentrated.** Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud are the principal hyperscale providers, although their relative positions differ across countries and service layers. Competition authorities link their advantages to infrastructure scale, broad service portfolios, software and developer ecosystems, and integration with adjacent digital services. The main competition concerns are technical and financial barriers to switching, egress charges, startup cloud credits, discriminatory software licensing and bundling. By January 2025, the UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) had provisionally found competition harms involving concentration, switching barriers, egress fees and Microsoft licensing; its assessment did not condemn every discount or integrated service. [1]

**These are source-specific percentage shares.** Square brackets reproduce the CMA’s disclosure bands; † identifies calculated outer bounds, explained below. Ranges are joint constraints, not independent endpoints to add together: each actual jurisdictional allocation totals 100%. In Japan, AWS, Microsoft and Google together account for 60–70%; the individual ceilings use the JFTC’s concentration index rather than an assumed allocation.

**The report follows two questions.** Part One establishes who competes, what the five country observations show, and why scale and ecosystems reinforce concentration. Part Two examines customers’ ability to move workloads, the competitive effects of egress pricing and startup credits, and the separate mechanisms through which Microsoft licensing and Teams bundling can influence neighboring markets.

## Part One: Market Structure and Competitive Dynamics

### Scope and the principal competitors

**The focus is public-cloud infrastructure.** Public cloud services are open to customers willing to pay and use computing resources shared among customers. This differs from private cloud, where resources are dedicated to one customer. The relevant services here are infrastructure as a service, or IaaS, and platform as a service, or PaaS—not the entire software-as-a-service market. These distinctions follow the CMA’s *Competitive landscape working paper* of 23 May 2024, paragraphs 1.3–1.6. [4]

| Service model | What the customer buys | Main customer responsibility | Role in this report |
|---|---|---|---|
| IaaS | Compute, storage and networking | Operating systems, applications and data | Core infrastructure competition |
| PaaS | Managed development and operating platforms | Applications and data | Core platform competition |
| SaaS | Ready-to-use hosted applications | Use and application settings | Adjacent-market and ecosystem context |

**IaaS transfers infrastructure management.** The provider operates the physical servers, storage and networking, while the customer retains substantial control over the operating system and software running above them. Amazon EC2 is an illustrative computing service. PaaS transfers more management to the supplier: a managed database or application platform lets customers develop and operate applications without constructing and maintaining the underlying platform. The French Autorité de la concurrence uses these distinctions in Opinion 23-A-08 of 29 June 2023. [2]

**Service layers are not interchangeable markets.** IaaS and PaaS are useful industry classifications, but they do not automatically define one antitrust market. In June 2024, the CMA’s emerging assessment was that PaaS generally did not substitute sufficiently for IaaS for most customers and workloads. By January 2025, it provisionally identified separate markets for standard-compute IaaS and PaaS, with a European geographic scope encompassing the UK and European Economic Area. It also distinguished accelerated-compute IaaS from standard compute. [7][1]

**Workloads explain the practical boundaries.** A workload is the collection of applications, data and services serving a particular business purpose. An online retail workload, for example, can combine computing, storage, authentication and database services. Providers may compete to supply the whole combination or a particular component. The Autorité therefore emphasizes customer requirements and substitution at workload or service level, rather than treating every cloud product as equivalent. [2]

**Three providers form the hyperscale core.** AWS, Azure and Google Cloud combine worldwide infrastructure networks with extensive IaaS and PaaS portfolios. They belong to large digital groups whose financial resources, internal computing needs and established technologies helped support their cloud expansion. The term “hyperscaler” describes this operating model and scale; it is not itself a legal finding of dominance. [2]

| Provider | Competitive position | Illustrative services | Important ecosystem advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amazon Web Services | Established leader; broad infrastructure presence | EC2, S3, RDS, Lambda | Extensive services, partners and trained developers |
| Microsoft Azure | AWS’s closest broad competitor | Azure compute, storage and platforms | Enterprise relationships and Microsoft software |
| Google Cloud | Principal hyperscale challenger | Compute Engine, BigQuery | Analytics, AI and cloud-native capabilities |

**The leading pair matters particularly.** The CMA’s May 2024 customer research described AWS as the perceived overall leader, followed by Microsoft, with Google reputable but serving a smaller user pool. Its January 2025 provisional assessment similarly placed AWS and Microsoft ahead of Google in the UK, especially in IaaS. The customer research comprised 60 interviews with individuals from 50 medium-to-large companies that had paid for IaaS or PaaS in 2022. [8]

**Other suppliers provide meaningful alternatives.** The competitive field also includes global technology companies with enterprise software, telecommunications or regional strengths. Their ability to constrain a hyperscaler depends on the customer, geography and workload. A provider offering a strong database platform or inexpensive storage is not necessarily an equivalent replacement for a multinational enterprise’s entire cloud portfolio. The CMA explicitly distinguished suitability for individual workloads from suitability as a customer’s main provider. [4]

The global and enterprise-oriented challengers include the following.

| Provider | Main competitive role | Relevant positioning |
|---|---|---|
| Alibaba Cloud | International infrastructure and platform supplier | Particularly relevant to Chinese-market connections |
| IBM | Enterprise IaaS and PaaS supplier | Hybrid cloud, Red Hat and OpenShift |
| Oracle | Enterprise infrastructure and platform supplier | Databases and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure |
| Huawei Cloud | Chinese infrastructure competitor | Regional IaaS competition |
| Tencent Cloud | Chinese and Asian cloud competitor | Regional infrastructure and platform services |

**Enterprise challengers have different starting points.** The Autorité describes Alibaba, IBM and Oracle as expanding from established businesses into cloud services. IBM’s offerings include infrastructure and platforms associated with Red Hat and OpenShift; Oracle brings an established database position. The 2024 ICRIER study, *A Competition Analysis of the Indian Cloud Computing Market*, also identifies Huawei and Tencent among relevant IaaS competitors. These are provider descriptions, not evidence that every company exerts the same competitive constraint in Europe. [2][9]

Regional and specialist providers further broaden the landscape.

|---|---|---|

**Local providers can alter national rankings.** Naver’s reported 7% South Korean share in 2021 placed it ahead of Google in the KFTC account discussed below. Japanese market evidence also identifies Fujitsu, NTT and SoftBank as relevant competitors. In Europe, the Autorité names OVHcloud, 3DS Outscale and Scaleway as cloud-focused “pure players,” while Biglaiser, Crémer and Mantovani’s March 2024 study, *The Economics of the Cloud*, identifies OVHcloud and IONOS as important European providers. [5][10][11]

**PaaS competition includes independent software vendors.** Independent software vendors, or ISVs, usually do not own the underlying infrastructure. They buy infrastructure inputs and sell platforms or applications, sometimes through the infrastructure provider’s marketplace. Consequently, an ISV can simultaneously be a hyperscaler’s customer, complement and competitor. The CMA’s May 2024 paper identifies MongoDB, Snowflake, VMware and Yugabyte among these participants. [4]

| ISV | Illustrative offering | Competitive relationship |
|---|---|---|
| MongoDB | Atlas managed databases | Competes in databases while using cloud infrastructure |
| Snowflake | Cloud data platform | Supplies data services across cloud environments |
| Yugabyte | YugabyteDB | Supplies distributed databases across providers |
| VMware | Virtualization technology | Supplies technology used across infrastructure environments |

**The resulting structure is layered.** There is a small group of broad, vertically integrated providers; a wider set of infrastructure challengers; and a more differentiated population of platform suppliers. This explains why the number of PaaS firms can be substantially larger than the number of credible full-portfolio infrastructure alternatives. It also explains why competition at the application or platform layer does not necessarily remove dependence on the infrastructure layer.

### What the five jurisdictional observations show

**Concentration is the common finding.** France shows a clear AWS lead; the detailed Dutch data place Azure first; the UK identifies AWS and Microsoft as the leading pair; South Korea combines a large AWS position with declining concentration over the reported period; and Japan shows substantial growth in concentration over a longer interval. The opening table preserves the requested years rather than substituting later observations.

| Observation | Measurement basis | Decisive concentration result | Main interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| France, 2021 | Public-cloud IaaS/PaaS revenue | Three hyperscalers: 71% | AWS leads clearly |
| Netherlands, 2020 | Public-cloud market revenue bands | AWS plus Azure: 70–80% | Strong leading pair |
| UK, 2022 | Combined IaaS/PaaS revenue shares | AWS plus Microsoft: [70–80]% | Two-provider lead |
| South Korea, 2021 | KFTC-reported cloud-market shares | AWS plus Microsoft: 74.1% | Large AWS lead; domestic challenger |
| Japan, FY2020 | Combined IaaS/PaaS market | Three named CSPs: 60–70% | Concentrated, with material regional competition |

**France provides a complete breakdown.** Figure 17 of the Autorité’s 2023 opinion reproduces Markess by Exaegis estimates of AWS at 46%, Microsoft at 17%, Google at 8% and other providers at 29% of French IaaS/PaaS revenue in 2021. The three hyperscalers therefore held 71%, calculated as 46 + 17 + 8. AWS and Microsoft together held 63%. [2]

**Growth capture is a different measure.** The Autorité also reports that the three hyperscalers captured 80% of French public-cloud infrastructure and application spending growth in 2021. That does not mean they held 80% of the market. The distinction matters because smaller suppliers can increase revenue while losing share: the same French evidence records other providers growing by 23%, compared with 35% growth for the market overall. [2]

**Dutch concentration survives the source discrepancy.** The detailed ACM-associated 2020 data give Azure 40–45%, AWS 30–35%, Google 5–10%, Oracle 5–10%, IBM 0–5% and a separate residual category of 5–10%. For the opening table, “Other” includes every supplier outside AWS, Azure and Google. Its calculated range is therefore 10–25%: Oracle’s 5–10%, plus IBM’s 0–5%, plus the source’s residual 5–10%. [3]

**The Dutch summary uses different bands.** ACM’s public summary says AWS and Azure each held 35–40%, whereas its detailed series places Azure ahead. Both presentations nevertheless imply a combined share of 70–80%. The detailed allocation supports Azure’s first position in that series; the common structural conclusion is a market dominated by the same two suppliers. The Autorité also quotes the detailed ACM figures and identifies their location as page 34 of the Dutch study. [12][2]

**The UK publishes individual 2022 bands.** The CMA’s May 2024 *Competitive landscape working paper*, paragraph 5.22, reports AWS and Microsoft separately at [30–40]% each of combined UK IaaS/PaaS revenue in 2022, with Google at [5–10]%. Paragraph 5.24 places the combined remaining smaller providers and ISVs at [10–20]%. These are the values used in the opening table. Paragraph 5.23 and the June 2024 updated issues statement put AWS and Microsoft’s combined share at [70–80]%. [4][7]

**Overlapping bands do not establish equality.** The UK working paper explicitly describes AWS as the largest combined IaaS/PaaS supplier and Microsoft as second. Their identical disclosure bands therefore should not be read as equal point shares. The CMA also found a sharper leading-pair position in IaaS than in PaaS, where independent platform suppliers account for a larger part of supply. [4]

**The UK figures have a publication vintage.** The January 2025 provisional decision revised the historical market-size methodology using provider revenues and underlying IDC and Synergy data. Its paragraph 3.141 explains the revision. Accordingly, changes between an earlier working-paper share and a later provisional-report share should not automatically be interpreted as changes in competitive position: the measurement denominator also changed. The requested 2022 row above uses the explicitly identified May 2024 combined-market series. [1]

**South Korea shows a large incumbent lead.** KED Global’s December 2022 account of the Korea Fair Trade Commission’s study reports AWS at 62.1%, Microsoft at 12% and Naver at 7% in 2021. It states that Naver had overtaken Google. AWS and Microsoft therefore jointly accounted for 74.1%, leaving 25.9% for Google and all other providers. [5]

**The Korean bounds follow that ranking.** Using Naver’s reported 7% as Google’s conservative ceiling gives Google a calculated outer bound of 0–7%. “Other,” including Naver, is then 18.9–25.9%, calculated as 25.9 minus Google’s 0–7%. The zero endpoint is a mathematical boundary, not a claim that Google had no business. This allocation retains both the reported provider ranking and the requirement that the four columns sum to 100%.

**Korean concentration was also declining.** The same account reports AWS losing share while Microsoft gained, providing a useful counterexample to an assumption that concentration increased everywhere.

| Year | AWS | Microsoft | Combined share | All remaining providers |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2019 | 77.9% | 6.7% | 84.6% | 15.4% |
| 2020 | 70.0% | 9.4% | 79.4% | 20.6% |
| 2021 | 62.1% | 12.0% | 74.1% | 25.9% |

**A declining leader can remain powerful.** AWS’s share fell by 15.8 percentage points between 2019 and 2021, while the leading pair’s combined share fell by 10.5 points. Those calculations show meaningful expansion by challengers, but the 2021 endpoint still leaves AWS considerably larger than any named competitor. Concentration and competitive movement are therefore not opposites: a market can remain concentrated while becoming less concentrated. [5]

**Japan supplies strong aggregate evidence.** The Japan Fair Trade Commission’s 2022 materials report AWS, Microsoft and Google collectively at 60–70% of the combined IaaS/PaaS market in FY2020. The remaining providers consequently account for a calculated 30–40%. The JFTC also reports a Herfindahl–Hirschman Index, or HHI, of 2,449. HHI is the sum of providers’ squared percentage shares. [6]

**Japan’s individual ceilings are calculated bounds.** Because each provider’s squared share is one non-negative component of HHI, no individual share can exceed the square root of the total. The opening table uses this conservative ceiling rather than dividing the three-provider aggregate equally.

\(HHI=\sum_i s_i^2=2{,}449\quad\Rightarrow\quad s_i\leq\sqrt{2{,}449}\approx49.5\%.\)

The three individual intervals remain subject to the additional condition that AWS, Microsoft and Google together hold 60–70%. They are bounds, not estimates that any provider was close to either endpoint.

**Japan’s concentration trend is clearer still.** The JFTC’s historical series shows both a rising top-three share and a rising HHI.

| Japanese fiscal year | Actual top-three providers’ combined share | HHI |
|---|---:|---:|
| FY2011 | 40–50% | 901 |
| FY2017 | 50–60% | 1,745 |
| FY2020 | 60–70% | 2,449 |

**The two Japanese groups differ.** The JFTC expressly cautions that the actual top three firms and the named group of AWS, Microsoft and Google were not the same group in FY2020, even though both totals appear in the 60–70% band. This is important evidence of regional competition: labeling the three global hyperscalers does not establish that all three occupied the first three national ranking positions. [13]

**Enterprise usage provides a separate comparison.** MM Research Institute’s June 2021 Japanese survey offers provider-level adoption evidence relating to FY2020. It covered 28,868 companies and examined 1,182 companies adopting or considering public-cloud IaaS/PaaS in detail. Among users of each service layer, the reported provider usage rates were as follows. [14]

| Provider | Share of IaaS users reporting use | Share of PaaS users reporting use |
|---|---:|---:|
| AWS | 40.3% | 37.4% |
| Microsoft Azure | 26.3% | 30.6% |
| Google Cloud | 13.7% | 15.9% |

**Usage is not expenditure share.** This proxy shows how frequently sampled enterprises reported using each provider, not how much revenue each provider earned. A company can use several providers and spend very different amounts with each. The survey therefore supports an adoption comparison but is not used to allocate the JFTC’s revenue-market aggregate.

**The cross-country judgment is bounded.** The evidence consistently identifies substantial concentration and a central role for AWS and Microsoft. It does not establish an identical national ranking, an identical Google position or a common concentration trajectory. The most relevant competitive comparison remains the one matching the customer’s jurisdiction, service layer and workload.

### Why scale and ecosystems reinforce concentration

**The concentration mechanisms reinforce one another.** Scale reduces infrastructure costs; service breadth attracts customers; customers attract software vendors and technical specialists; and the resulting ecosystem makes subsequent adoption easier and switching harder. Authorities describe these as mutually reinforcing advantages, while giving particular weight to infrastructure scale and portfolio breadth. The CMA’s June 2024 assessment described network effects as a contributing factor, but a less important one than scale and related entry barriers. [7]

| Mechanism | Source of advantage | Effect on customer choice | Competitive consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure scale | Procurement, energy and utilization efficiencies | Competitive performance and service capacity | Difficult small-scale entry |
| Portfolio breadth | Shared technology and development investment | More needs met within one supplier | Harder full-portfolio challenge |
| ISV and skills networks | More complementary software and expertise | Easier deployment and staffing | Reinforced ecosystem adoption |
| Adjacent-service integration | Existing software, relationships and technology | Convenient integrated purchasing and operation | Incumbent relationship advantage |

**IaaS requires substantial sunk investment.** Data centres, servers, components and networks require capital before a new supplier has secured sufficient demand. Much of that investment becomes sunk: it cannot be recovered fully if entry fails. Leasing space or using colocation can reduce building expenditure, but does not remove the need for servers, networking equipment and operating capability. The CMA provisionally identified these factors as significant barriers to entry and expansion in January 2025. [1]

**Scale lowers ongoing unit costs.** Large providers can obtain bulk discounts on hardware and network equipment, spread operating overheads, and run larger facilities efficiently. Aggregating customers across industries and geographies also helps smooth differences in demand and improve utilization. A smaller entrant must compete while purchasing less equipment and spreading its infrastructure costs over fewer workloads. [4]

**Physical scale has operational trade-offs.** Larger data centres can be energy-efficient, but location also affects latency—the time required for information to travel between systems. Proximity, resilience and customer requirements can justify smaller or more distributed facilities. Scale is therefore an advantage, not proof that the largest possible facility is always the best design. The CMA expressly recognizes this location-efficiency trade-off. [4]

**PaaS adds development economies.** Platform software requires substantial development expenditure, but those costs do not rise proportionately with each additional customer. The JFTC identifies developer labor as an important software cost and notes that existing technology from other businesses can be reused in PaaS development. A provider with many customers and related products can therefore spread development costs more widely. [6]

**Breadth raises the competitive threshold.** Customers often value a provider capable of meeting needs they already have and needs they expect to develop. The CMA records AWS offering more than 200 services and products, spanning computing, storage, databases, analytics, security and developer tools. An entrant can offer an excellent individual service without matching the breadth required to win a customer’s main cloud relationship. [4]

**ISV effects run in both directions.** An ISV benefits from developing for a cloud with many potential customers. Customers, in turn, benefit from access to a large selection of compatible third-party applications and platforms. Developing and maintaining compatibility with additional clouds takes time and money, so a smaller infrastructure provider faces a circular problem: attracting customers may require software availability, while attracting software vendors requires a sufficiently large customer base. ACM describes these technical and commercial dependencies in its September 2022 *Market Study Cloud Services*. [15]

**These are mainly indirect network effects.** Another customer’s use does not necessarily make a virtual machine directly more useful, as another participant might improve a communications network. Instead, more customers attract complementary services, support providers and expertise. These additions make the ecosystem more valuable, encouraging further adoption. The distinction helps explain why authorities investigate the strength of particular channels rather than assuming that all cloud services exhibit the same network effects.

**ISVs can also weaken dependence.** Products available across multiple clouds can give customers alternatives to provider-specific platforms. MongoDB Atlas, for example, operates across several cloud providers, while competing in database services with offerings such as Amazon DocumentDB and Azure Cosmos DB. Yet the infrastructure relationship remains: an independent PaaS sale commonly also generates demand for an underlying IaaS provider. The CMA’s January 2025 assessment therefore found that more numerous PaaS suppliers did not translate into an equally strong constraint on vertically integrated infrastructure providers. [4][1]

**Developer familiarity reinforces adoption.** Engineers acquire provider-specific knowledge about configuration, deployment, monitoring and security. A company employing those engineers can introduce additional workloads more cheaply on the familiar platform than on an unfamiliar one. Separately, a large external pool of trained engineers makes recruitment and implementation support easier. The first mechanism is learning within a customer; the second is an ecosystem advantage extending across customers.

**Customer evidence supports both mechanisms.** CERRE’s February 2024 review reports that retraining staff was identified by 33% of UK respondents as a switching challenge and ranked second among the challenges considered. At its July 2024 CMA hearing, Microsoft likewise said developers increasingly influence infrastructure decisions and tend to choose technologies with which they are comfortable. The latter is Microsoft’s characterization, while the survey evidence concerns reported customer experience. [16][17]

**Japanese responses illustrate ecosystem value.** The JFTC’s study links provider choice to complementary services and technical support, and records a substantial preference for the current provider when expanding cloud use. [13]

| Customer response | IaaS respondents | PaaS respondents |
|---|---:|---:|
| Chose provider for abundant related services | 22.9% | 30.8% |
| Chose provider for available engineers/support providers | 23.1% | 20.5% |
| Would prioritize current provider for expansion | 62.5% | 63.7% |

**Integration adds a separate advantage.** Existing productivity software, identity systems, databases, analytics and enterprise relationships can make a cloud ecosystem convenient to adopt. Microsoft’s relationship with an enterprise may therefore matter before any new infrastructure contract is negotiated. The CMA/Jigsaw research found that initial Azure adoption was often closely connected to pre-existing Microsoft products and enterprise agreements. Some customers found Azure so embedded in their IT approach that reviewing its use was difficult to justify. [8]

**Integration can create genuine efficiencies.** Customers can obtain coordinated services, consolidated purchasing and fewer interoperability problems within a provider’s environment. Providers can also reuse technology and infrastructure across products. The Autorité recognizes these benefits while noting that a tightly integrated stack can make an equivalent combination difficult for another supplier to reproduce. [2]

**The concern is weakened contestability.** Contestable demand is business that rivals can realistically compete to win. When expansion naturally follows existing infrastructure, software and skills, rivals may need to overcome much more than a price difference. Lower-cost infrastructure alone may not compensate for missing services, retraining, application changes or additional integration work. This connects scale, networks and integration to entry and expansion barriers without treating customer convenience itself as misconduct.

**The transition changes competitive pressure.** The CMA’s June 2024 statement observed that existing customers expanding or adding workloads were increasingly important to growth. If those customers face switching and multicloud barriers, winning the initial cloud relationship can influence later purchases. The Autorité similarly describes strong competition for a workload’s initial placement followed by potentially weaker competition once it is embedded in an ecosystem. [7][2]

**Part One’s conclusion is structural.** High concentration reflects mutually reinforcing cost, portfolio and ecosystem advantages; it is not explained by market shares alone, and it does not by itself establish unlawful conduct.

**Product and service structure**

A public-cloud explainer defines public cloud as computing resources owned and operated by a third-party provider, delivered over the internet to multiple customers using shared infrastructure. It includes servers, storage, networking and applications, generally on a pay-as-you-go basis, and identifies IaaS, PaaS and SaaS as the main service models [35]. A cloud-computing review similarly distinguishes public cloud, which serves the general public, from private cloud, used by one organisation, and hybrid cloud, which combines public and private environments [31].

The requested focus is narrower than the broad multi-cloud and hybrid-cloud scenario described in another source, which combines on-premises systems with IaaS, PaaS and SaaS but does not define an authority-specific market boundary or measurement method [30]. The OVHcloud explanation likewise says that IaaS can be deployed in public, private or hybrid models and does not specify a geographic, customer, workload or provider-population boundary [32]. These qualifications prevent the sources from being silently harmonised into one market denominator.

For measurement purposes, the Public First report defines “cloud services” as IaaS or PaaS and excludes SaaS unless otherwise stated. It states that analysts increasingly group IaaS and PaaS together; for example, Gartner’s 2020 IaaS framework was revised to include PaaS. The report therefore measures businesses using either IaaS or PaaS rather than SaaS alone [36]. It describes IaaS as replacing on-premises storage and computing with resources hired from a public-cloud provider, while PaaS allows developers to run databases and middleware without managing the underlying hardware [36]. Other materials describe PaaS as a development, testing and deployment environment using programming languages, tools, APIs and runtimes [31][32].

**Preliminary concentration mechanisms**

The strongest scale evidence in this group is a reported comparison from Public First: public-cloud servers typically have **more than 50% utilisation**, versus **less than 20%** for traditional on-premises servers; the report links the difference to shared centralised research and investment [36]. This supports a utilisation and fixed-investment mechanism, but it is a reported comparison rather than an authority finding that proves concentration. The same report says AWS users reported average **50% IT-cost savings** after migration and estimates that U.S. software developers reduced new-software development time by approximately **25%**; the cited extracts do not specify the observation period, and the latter is explicitly an estimate [36]. A separate article attributes AWS’s position to “vast infrastructure,” technology, service breadth, scalability, affordability and dependability, but expressly provides no authority-supported causal link to concentration [34].

The cards show possible complement and skills channels, but not a completed network-effects finding. The review describes PaaS APIs, programming languages and runtime environments; it gives AppEngine’s Python and Java support and says that a .NET requirement would lead a user to Aneka or Azure [31]. OVHcloud says PaaS is aimed especially at developers and generally requires trained teams, but it supplies no evidence that familiarity causes concentration or deters switching [32]. The available material likewise contains no direct authority evidence that ISV ecosystems caused market concentration. References to application platforms, containerisation, databases, middleware, and SaaS integration with analytics, storage and AI illustrate complement relationships but do not establish an ISV network effect [38].

Integration examples are similarly qualified. The commercial material says Azure fits organisations already using Windows Server, Office 365 or Active Directory, while Alibaba Cloud benefits from demand generated by Alibaba’s e-commerce activities [38]. These examples identify possible adjacent-service advantages, but their post-cutoff, non-authority status means they should not be treated as findings. The evidence therefore supports scale, service breadth, technical complements and developer capability as documented background mechanisms, while leaving their contribution to concentration for the eligible authority evidence.

**Scope and provider evidence**

Public-cloud scope is source-specific. The Japanese academic study distinguishes public cloud—externally supplied, pay-as-you-go computing—from private cloud built internally by users; it names IaaS, PaaS and SaaS as service models but does not define their operational boundaries separately [45]. Microsoft's Ofcom submission similarly places public-cloud providers across IaaS and PaaS, while treating SaaS as subscription-based, ready-to-use applications; it cautions that customers combine service categories within workloads, so the layers are not always separately switchable [40]. The Japanese Yano study treats “cloud-platform” services as IaaS/PaaS, reports separate IaaS/PaaS market-size and sales series for 2017–2023 (including forecasts), and separately reports a public-cloud aggregation excluding SaaS; its research also covered managed-cloud and HCI providers as adjacent services [43]. Synergy distinguishes public IaaS, public PaaS, managed private cloud and managed public cloud in APAC, but gives no operational definition of each segment [46]. These measures therefore should not be harmonised into one denominator.

In Microsoft's stakeholder submission—not an authority finding—AWS, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud Platform are identified as the largest suppliers; AWS is described as the worldwide leader with roughly 40% share, depending on the services counted, while the three providers are said to invest billions annually in data centres, networking and new services [40]. A commercial public-cloud roster also names IBM, Oracle, Alibaba, Fujitsu, Huawei, OVHcloud and Tencent alongside the three largest suppliers [44]. That roster establishes market presence, not an authority-based hyperscale classification. Fujitsu is profiled in the Japanese IaaS/PaaS study, but remains only a candidate other key player because the supplied material does not apply an authority materiality criterion [43].

Regional evidence adds local competitors without resolving the requested jurisdiction-year rows. Synergy's APAC comparison, based on quarterly revenue, identifies AWS and Alibaba as clear regional leaders; AWS leads four of five APAC sub-regions, Microsoft ranks second in three and third overall, Google and IBM appear as global players, Fujitsu and NTT are well placed in Japan, Naver and KT feature heavily in South Korea, and Tencent and Baidu feature among China's regional leaders [46]. The source does not establish equivalent status for Leaseweb, Naver Cloud, or any of these firms under a competition-authority hyperscale test.

**Concentration mechanisms and evidence limits**

The Japanese research article characterises cloud computing as oligopolistic because the Big Four's overall share was growing, HHI was increasing, and IaaS/PaaS services exhibited network externalities; it presents this as academic analysis rather than a competition-authority finding [45]. Scale evidence in this source group is likewise descriptive rather than causal: Microsoft's submission links the major suppliers' position to large investment in data centres, networks and services, while Synergy reports regional leadership by revenue [40][46]. These observations support concentration context but do not by themselves quantify the contribution of scale economies.

The developer evidence is mixed. Microsoft argues that sophisticated organisations with specialised developers can assess migration trade-offs, optimise workloads and use multiple suppliers [40]. It gives Walmart's use of Kubernetes across Azure, Google Cloud and OpenStack, Azure Arc's management of on-premises, multi-cloud and edge workloads, and Snowflake's cloud-agnostic data layer across AWS, Azure and Google Cloud as examples of portability tools [40]. Thus the source documents developer expertise and technical complements as potential counterweights to lock-in, rather than establishing a one-way developer-familiarity network effect. The cards contain no quantified ISV effect; Snowflake is evidence of a third-party complement, not a measured ISV-concentration result. A commercial report records Microsoft's **May 2024** plan to build cloud and AI infrastructure in Thailand and offer AI-skilling opportunities for the developer community, but it does not establish that this changed provider choice or concentration [44].


These sources qualify, rather than complete, the early-2025 authority record. The APAC commercial study defines cloud as internet-delivered compute, storage, databases, networking, software, analytics and intelligence; its public-cloud category includes IaaS, PaaS and SaaS, separately from private and hybrid cloud [47]. EY similarly lists compute, storage, platforms and applications but does not define IaaS and PaaS separately [49]. AWS, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud are described as global-scale providers; Alibaba Cloud, Tencent Cloud and Huawei Cloud as localized providers. Oracle, IBM, Fujitsu, OVH, NTT, Baidu, JD.com, Rackspace, VMware, NetApp, AMELA Technology and DigitalOcean are covered firms, not thereby confirmed key players. Alibaba Cloud and Tencent Cloud are therefore commercial candidates, and Fujitsu a covered Japanese candidate—not authority-confirmed classifications; the APAC report was updated on 13 January 2026 [47].

Amazon Web Services is within the report’s public-cloud/IaaS/PaaS scope, although no individual AWS products are enumerated [47]. The Supercloud paper identifies Microsoft Azure as an IaaS provider that matched some Amazon prices; Google Cloud appears in cross-cloud migration, with preemptable instances lower-priced but terminable [50]. Its cross-cloud IaaS model covers virtual machines, storage and networking across public and private clouds, but not PaaS [50].

The later APAC report says cloud computing fosters economies of scale, while fragmented localization rules require multiple compliance frameworks and erode them. It reports APAC public cloud at 46.85% in 2025, hybrid growth of 26.4% CAGR to 2031, large-enterprise share of 63.10%, and SME growth of 21.6% CAGR [47]. It links PaaS to proprietary developer-tool lock-in; marketplaces, SAP ERP on Alibaba Cloud, telco/Nvidia partnerships and localized compliance are reported ecosystem or access mechanisms, not ISV-specific authority findings. Hybrid architecture balances latency, sovereignty and continuity; orchestration unifies policy, cost and performance, while SMEs cite cost, complexity and security, eased by simple interfaces and pay-as-you-go pricing [47].

The Supercloud analysis says datacentre and hardware capital lasts years; whole-operation migration is difficult and expensive, giving providers pricing power, while basic IaaS prices are largely similar and live migration can improve geographic mobility and utilization [50]. EY’s February 2026 report later describes multi-MW hyperscalers, $6b+ availability-zone capital, $20–40m migration planning, 1–2 months of duplicated operations and SLA penalties [49]. It provides no shares or denominators for the Netherlands 2020, UK 2022 or Japan 2020 in this source group, so those cells remain unavailable here; the 2019–2020 Amsterdam moratorium is contextual, while the April 2025 ban is outside the cutoff [49].

Public-cloud structure is layered. The CRA response (2 August 2024) distinguishes IaaS+PaaS+SaaS from IaaS+PaaS, reports entry and expanding offerings, and says other providers out-earned segment leaders in H1 2017–H2 2023 in both measures [51]. The CMA working paper, as relayed by CRA, used revenue, data-centre capacity and new-business flow; it named AWS and Azure main players and AWS/Microsoft the largest capacity providers, but its multi-cloud data covered only AWS, Microsoft and Google [51]. IBM and Oracle appeared in capacity analysis; Huawei entered through telecom infrastructure without a hyperscaler classification; Aethir and Skytap reportedly secured about $9m and $18m, respectively, in the preceding year [51].

Working-paper concentration findings are contested: IaaS and combined IaaS+PaaS were concentrated and increasingly so, with AWS broadly stable in 2019–22; CRA’s newer data found falling HHIs and AWS shares in IaaS, PaaS and combined IaaS+PaaS, with 2023 omitted [51]. The working paper cites sunk infrastructure, bulk purchasing, operating efficiency and R&D as scale sources; CRA says entry and smaller-provider growth imply limited effects—a submission, not an independent CMA finding [51]. CRA also reports cloud at under 15% of IT spending versus over 70% for on-premises solutions, with 30–65% diversion to on-premises [51].

The public-IaaS study excludes private IaaS; IaaS is computing, storage and networking, while PaaS is a software-development environment, historically combined with IaaS but separately measured [52]. AWS expanded into PaaS/SaaS; Gartner worldwide AWS IaaS shares were 44.20% (2015), 49.40% (2016), 47.90% (2017), 45.00% (2018) and 39.80% (2019); Amazon and Microsoft held nearly two-thirds in 2019 and the top five 80% [52]. The study identifies Amazon, Microsoft, Alibaba and Google as a four-provider global public-IaaS oligopoly forming since 2018 and IBM among PaaS players [52]. An EU study reports top-five IaaS+PaaS share above 50% in 2016 and oligopoly risk [54].

Developer evidence supplies a technical channel: PaaS APIs often cause vendor lock-in [55]. Of 294 surveyed developers, 160 cited cloud characteristics: 31% scalability/elasticity, 11% automation/deployment and 10% easier setup; among 124 tool respondents, 57% used log/analytics, 39% performance and 27% provider SDKs [55]. Cloud IDEs were not mainstream, and tooling growth may reflect SaaS rather than platform choice [55]. For United Kingdom (2022), [52] is worldwide (2015–19) and [54] a projected 2025 EU27-excluding-UK study; for this source group, AWS, Azure, Google Cloud and Other shares, market definition and denominator are unavailable [52][54].

Evidence here supports a layered, non-harmonised market. IaaS supplies low-level VM, storage and network resources; PaaS supplies virtualised servers and associated services for running or developing applications; IaaS, PaaS and SaaS are distinct, with cloud generally on-demand and pay-per-use [64][68]. Microsoft’s later combined Windows Server/SQL Server “cloud infrastructure services” (IaaS/PaaS) description gives no separate-versus-combined measurement and is post-cutoff [56].

Public-cloud competition is framed around switching, multiple providers, service linking and migration. PaaS has provider-specific services and few interface standards; IaaS portability depends on VM/container formats, architectures and runtime/OS dependencies. Differing APIs make developers learn new interfaces and raise development costs; the OMG guide recommends open technologies and OVF/Docker packaging to widen alternatives [62][64]. Google App Engine—Datastore, Cloud SQL and Cloud Storage—is a PaaS example, not evidence of an ISV network effect [68].

## Part Two: Main Competition Issues and Anti-Competitive Practices

**Authorities distinguish barriers from infringements.** Market studies can identify features that weaken competition without adjudicating an abuse of dominance. The Autorité’s 2023 opinion is a sector assessment; the CMA’s 28 January 2025 decision was provisional; and the European Commission’s June 2024 Teams Statement of Objections expressed a preliminary antitrust view. These legal distinctions matter when interpreting the practices below. [2][18]

| Issue | Decisive evidence | Position at early-2025 cutoff | Competitive judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Switching barriers | APIs, formats, skills and integration costs | CMA provisional competition harm | Choice can weaken after adoption |
| Egress fees | Reported large price-cost and provider-price differences | CMA provisional competition harm | Switching and integrated multicloud discouraged |
| Startup credits | Several hundred thousand euros; offers up to 10× smaller rivals | Autorité conditional risk assessment | Benefits may coexist with loyalty effects |
| Microsoft licensing | 2019 restrictions; differentiated rival-cloud terms | CMA provisional partial foreclosure | Software power can disadvantage cloud rivals |
| Teams bundling | Tying alleged since at least April 2019 | Commission preliminary objections, June 2024 | Distribution and interoperability advantages |

### 1. Switching Barriers

**Technical dependence is a central obstacle.** Across the Dutch, French and UK studies, switching is more than transferring files. Customers may need to change application code, recreate connections between services, redesign identity controls, move databases and retrain staff. The CMA/Jigsaw research identified non-portable code environments, incompatible formats, database and storage migration, and networking problems as commonly mentioned technical barriers. [8]

**Non-standard APIs create application dependence.** An application programming interface, or API, specifies how software interacts with another service. If an application is written around one provider’s interface, an equivalent service elsewhere may require different calls, configuration and integration work. ACM reports that different or closed APIs and standards can prevent data being transferred properly, or at all. The Autorité similarly records customers identifying non-standard APIs as a reason application code must be rewritten. [15][2]

**Documented interfaces do not ensure equivalence.** An interface can be accessible without being interchangeable with another provider’s interface. Customers may still need to adapt software and test that the new system behaves as required. This distinction separates openness of access from portability in practice. The relevant competition question is whether customers can use competing services at proportionate cost, not simply whether an API exists.

**Proprietary formats complicate data portability.** Data may require conversion before another provider can use it, particularly when it resides in proprietary databases or depends on provider-specific processing. The Autorité identifies substantial transcription work as one possible migration burden. It also discusses deliberate use of a particular format to prevent portability as a possible restrictive practice, rather than treating every format difference as proven exclusionary conduct. [2]

**PaaS can deepen these dependencies.** Managed platforms reduce the customer’s operating burden, but applications may become dependent on their particular functionality. The Autorité’s concrete example is changing a PaaS database: the application code using that database may need rewriting. The same automation that makes a service attractive can therefore make replacement costly. This is a functionality-portability trade-off, not evidence that every differentiated platform is anticompetitive. [19]

**Ancillary services matter as well.** Identity and access management, or IAM, determines which users and systems can access resources. Ofcom records a customer describing the differences between AWS and Google IAM as technically challenging and potentially unsafe to manage together. Another customer said complexity in ancillary services, including IAM and billing, encouraged concentration on AWS. Thus, a portable application may still depend on surrounding controls that are difficult to reproduce across clouds. [20]

**Skills and organizational resources add costs.** Even where conversion is technically feasible, a customer must allocate engineers, management attention and testing capacity. The CMA’s January 2025 report records customers saying switching diverts IT staff from core work and can take more time and effort than expected. These opportunity costs help explain why a rival offering a somewhat better service may still struggle to win an established workload. [1]

**Japanese responses show several distinct barriers.** The JFTC’s questionnaire records contractual, financial, technical and learning obstacles separately. Its reported responses are particularly useful because they show that direct contractual prohibitions are only one component of dependence. [13]

| Reported switching barrier | IaaS, n=379 | PaaS, n=124 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Learning new technology | 146 respondents; 38.5% | 41 respondents; 33.1% |
| Incompatible data format or processing | 40; 10.6% | 26; 21.0% |
| Paying data-retrieval costs | 57; 15.0% | 22; 17.7% |
| Contract prohibits or restricts transfer | 12; 3.2% | 8; 6.5% |

**Contractual freedom is therefore insufficient.** A customer may possess a legal right to terminate yet face substantial practical costs in doing so. Conversely, a contractual restriction can worsen an already difficult technical migration. The evidence supports a cumulative account: learning, redesign, data handling, contract terms and charges can combine even when none individually makes switching impossible.

**Complex pricing obscures the business case.** ACM describes tariffs charging by action, stored gigabyte or compute-second. Customers must estimate future usage across many services to compare total costs, not simply compare a single posted price. Additional charges for networking and related services can make an apparently cheaper alternative less attractive after implementation. [15]

**Discount structures influence workload allocation.** The CMA/Jigsaw research found larger customers using layered committed-spend discounts, committed-use discounts and reserved instances. Participants reported that minimum spending targets or service-specific discount conditions could encourage purchases they otherwise would not make. These arrangements can complicate comparison because moving one workload may affect the economics of services remaining with the incumbent. [8]

| Pricing arrangement | Principal commitment | Customer benefit | Potential allocation effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Committed-spend agreement | Minimum spending across services | Discount on qualifying expenditure | Concentrates spend with one provider |
| Committed-use discount | Defined resource usage | Lower rate for committed usage | Favors use of committed resources |
| Reserved instance | Configuration- and term-linked arrangement | Reduced price for qualifying instances | Creates configuration-specific incentives |

**Egress adds a financial exit hurdle.** Charges for transferring data outside the incumbent’s infrastructure increase the expense of moving to a rival or integrating services across clouds. They operate alongside migration labor, redesign and parallel operation during transition. Their particular pricing and cost justification are examined in the next subsection.

**Korean evidence points in the same direction.** The KFTC study, as reported by KED Global, found that only 14% of customers had experienced changing providers. It also reported that 55.3% regarded switching as possible with time and money, while 39.7% described it as impossible. The authority connected these responses to technology incompatibility and the time and cost of transferring accumulated data. [5]

**Multicloud is not a single condition.** A customer may buy unrelated services from two providers while keeping each workload entirely within one ecosystem. That is different from integrating a database on one cloud with computing or analytics on another. The latter generally requires more cross-provider interfaces, security coordination and data movement. The CMA distinguishes these architectures because the ability to add a small secondary provider does not establish that the main workload is readily contestable. [4]

**Provider evidence demonstrates some mobility.** Microsoft’s November 2023 submission cited survey evidence that 60% of respondents had either switched IaaS/PaaS providers or added a provider. That is relevant counterevidence to an absolute claim of immobility. However, combining switching with provider addition does not establish the frequency of moving an established main workload or the feasibility of integrated multicloud. The CMA’s January 2025 assessment recognized successful multicloud use while finding that spending often remained concentrated with one main provider. [21][1]

**Differentiation also produces customer benefits.** Microsoft argued at its July 2024 hearing that customers deliberately choose between differentiated functionality and portability. The CMA acknowledged that cloud-agnostic software, open APIs and tools such as Kubernetes can mitigate dependence, while finding that these approaches do not fully overcome switching and integration challenges. The appropriate comparison is therefore not a frictionless identical-service market, but whether avoidable barriers prevent customers using attractive alternatives. [17][7]

**The competitive effect extends beyond exit.** If rivals can win only customers’ isolated workloads, they face a smaller accessible market than the headline cloud market suggests. Limited access to established demand can then impede the scale and ecosystem development needed to compete more broadly. Effective portability and interoperability would weaken that mechanism by allowing rivals to compete for components without requiring a complete replacement of the customer’s architecture.

**The switching-barrier takeaway is cumulative.** Authorities’ concern is that combined technical, organizational and commercial costs can prevent customers choosing the best evolving offer—not that every customer is unable to move or that every integration benefit should be removed.

### 2. Pricing Issues

**Egress pricing attracts the stronger finding.** By January 2025, the CMA provisionally concluded that egress fees reduced customers’ ability or incentive to switch or use multiple clouds and reduced suppliers’ incentives to compete for rivals’ customers. For startup credits, the French Autorité identified a conditional loyalty and exclusion risk while also recognizing substantial benefits. These are different evidential conclusions about different pricing practices. [1][22]

**Reported markups require distinct denominators.** The most striking numerical claim is Cloudflare’s estimate, cited by the Autorité, of almost 8,000% AWS transit-network “margins.” A separate Ofcom-attributed comparison places hyperscaler and IBM egress fees at five to ten times those of Oracle and OVHcloud. The first compares pricing with an estimated bandwidth-cost benchmark; the second compares suppliers’ fees.

| Evidence | Reported result | Comparison basis | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloudflare, cited by Autorité | Almost 8,000% | AWS transit-network cost comparison | External estimate |
| Ofcom, reported by ICRIER | 5–10× | Hyperscaler/IBM fees versus Oracle/OVHcloud | Cross-provider fee comparison |
| CMA provider-cost analysis | Some transfers have no incremental peering cost | Additional cost of individual transfers | Authority cost assessment |

**Egress fees charge outward movement.** They are charges for transferring data out of a provider’s cloud infrastructure. Transfers can go to another cloud, an on-premises system or an end user. Ingress—moving data into a cloud—is generally free. The competition concern focuses particularly on transfers needed for switching and multicloud, rather than assuming that every outbound transfer represents a competitive move. [7]

**The switching penalty is functional.** A fee need not be labeled an exit charge to discourage exit. If moving an existing dataset to a competitor triggers an additional bill, the rival’s offer must compensate the customer for that bill as well as other migration costs. Larger datasets increase this hurdle under volume-based pricing. The Autorité identifies this structure as a potential source of customer lock-in and notes the difficulty of predicting future traffic at initial adoption. [22]

**Integrated multicloud can face recurring charges.** Complete migration may involve a largely one-off transfer, but combining services across providers can require repeated transfers. This difference helps explain why fees can favor an integrated single-provider architecture or a multicloud architecture consisting of separate silos.

| Customer strategy | Relevant data movement | Egress exposure | Competitive implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full provider switch | Move stored data to replacement cloud | Primarily transitional | Raises the hurdle for rival acquisition |
| Siloed multicloud | Keep separate workloads on separate clouds | Limited cross-cloud movement | Preserves some workload-level choice |
| Integrated multicloud | Repeated exchanges across providers | Continuing charges | Discourages mixing best-fit services |

**Cloudflare’s estimate is narrowly based.** The Autorité cites Cloudflare’s 2021 estimate of AWS transit-network margins at almost 8,000% in Europe and the United States/Canada. Cloudflare used internal estimates of third-party bandwidth costs and suggested still greater differences could arise with settlement-free interconnection. The figure is an external cost-relative comparison, not an independently audited Autorité calculation of AWS’s total egress profitability. [22]

**“Margin” here needs careful interpretation.** A conventional gross margin divides profit by revenue and is not the measure represented by a figure of 8,000%. The quoted estimate concerns a narrow cost comparison. It should not be turned into a universal markup over the fully allocated cost of data centres, servers, network capacity, operations and capital. Nevertheless, it is relevant to the concern that outward-transfer prices can greatly exceed the incremental bandwidth expense associated with moving data.

**The five-to-ten multiple measures something else.** ICRIER’s 2024 review attributes to Ofcom an estimate that hyperscalers’ and IBM’s egress fees were five to ten times those of relatively smaller providers such as Oracle and OVHcloud. It also reports that fees exceeded incremental egress costs. The provider-price multiple is evidence of different commercial models, but it is not itself a price-to-cost markup: a smaller provider’s charge is not a direct measurement of another supplier’s cost. [9]

**The CMA examines actual cost categories.** Its May 2024 *Egress fees* working paper explains that broadly the same fixed assets can support ingress and egress. Egress-specific categories include internet transit, peering and edge networking. Transit generally involves purchasing connectivity from another network; peering involves direct exchange between networks. Depending on the arrangement, additional bandwidth or “burst” fees may be the only cost incremental to an individual transfer, and settlement-free peering can involve no incremental payment. [23]

**Zero incremental cost is not zero total cost.** Networks still require construction, operation and financing. Providers therefore argue that transfer fees contribute to investment and discourage inefficient traffic. Microsoft also argued that removing egress charges would redistribute the associated costs across other services and customers. These arguments address cost recovery and pricing design; they do not establish that the particular outward-transfer price transparently reflects the cost of the competitive transfer being charged. [17]

**The fee’s location matters competitively.** The Autorité observes that common infrastructure costs could also be recovered through storage or computing charges. Allocating more recovery to outward transfers can make the initial cloud offer appear cheaper while increasing later switching costs. Thus, two pricing structures can recover expenditure yet have different effects on customers’ freedom to change suppliers. [22]

**Observed bills provide important counterevidence.** The CMA’s June 2024 analysis found that a majority of relevant UK customers paid egress fees amounting to less than 1% of annual cloud spending, although a substantial minority paid higher proportions. Its qualitative research also found that egress fees were almost never the main switching barrier: technical challenges and an insufficient business case usually mattered more. [7][23]

**Small current payments can coexist with deterrence.** A customer avoiding cross-cloud transfers also avoids the associated charges. Its observed bill consequently does not reveal what it would pay under an integrated multicloud strategy or a full migration. The CMA’s hypothetical full-data-transfer exercise produced provider-estimate bands running from [0–5]% to [5–10]% of annual cloud spending, with materially higher proportions for some customers. These estimates concern data-transfer charges, not the entire cost of redesigning and migrating systems. [23]

**Customer circumstances determine materiality.** Fees are likely to matter more when a customer holds substantial data, needs repeated cross-cloud exchanges, or expects only a modest advantage from a competing service. They can be less decisive when technical redesign dominates the migration budget or the business benefits of moving are substantial. This explains the coexistence of successful switching and a competition concern: a barrier need not stop every transaction to reduce rivalry.

**Free-switching programs have a limited reach.** Providers introduced programs removing certain charges for customers fully switching away. However, the CMA’s January 2025 provisional report found their scope limited and uncertain, particularly for larger customers. It stated that these programs generally did not cover multicloud use, were limited to a 60-day switching period and did not cover all products. The CMA therefore did not consider them sufficient to change its conclusions materially. [1]

**The resulting egress judgment is specific.** The strongest authority conclusion is that the presence and magnitude of charges weaken switching and multicloud incentives, not that one universal percentage measures every provider’s excess pricing. Broad, practically usable transfer rights would weaken this mechanism; narrow full-exit waivers leave recurring cross-cloud exchanges exposed.

**Startup credits present a different problem.** Cloud credits are allowances applied against eligible service charges. They reduce early expenditure and can finance experimentation before a startup generates substantial revenue. The Autorité distinguishes ordinary short trials from much larger support programs aimed at innovative businesses. [22]

| Program category or example | Reported scale | Target or conditions | Main competitive significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary trial credits | Dozens of euros to about €1,000 | Usually no more than three months | Testing and product discovery |
| Large support programs | Up to several hundred thousand euros | Innovative users; potentially several years | Substantial acquisition and retention incentive |
| Google for Startups Cloud Program | USD 200,000–350,000 | Reported AI and Web3 startup offers | Illustrates hyperscaler support scale |

**The scale asymmetry is substantial.** The Autorité reports that hyperscaler credit offers can be up to ten times those offered by smaller providers. Some smaller firms can match a nominal amount for selected customers but cannot extend it to a comparable number of startups. ICRIER’s reported Google offer illustrates the scale of support that can accompany technical guidance and business assistance. [22][9]

**Eligibility can affect competitive demand.** Credits tied to one provider can make its initial effective price extremely low. A startup then develops applications, staff expertise and operating processes around that provider’s services. Once the allowance is exhausted, moving can require rebuilding precisely the capabilities created during the subsidized period. The concern therefore arises from the interaction of financial support with subsequent technical dependence, rather than from the nominal credit amount alone.

**Some credits involve purchase conditions.** The Autorité’s paragraph 418 discusses credits conditional on substantial purchase commitments or volumes and refers to AWS Activate and Migration Acceleration as examples in its examination. Providers justified such support partly as passing on savings from larger, more predictable demand, and partly as offsetting migration or training costs. The assessment must therefore consider the actual program terms rather than assume every startup credit has the same conditions. [22]

**The loyalty-rebate theory is conditional.** A loyalty rebate rewards keeping purchases with a supplier, potentially making rival offers less attractive even for business the rival could otherwise serve. The Autorité notes that credits can be reserved for the provider’s services for up to two years and can make consumption entirely free within the allowance. Where a problem of demand contestability exists, it says this could resemble an extreme loyalty rebate over total volumes and invoiced price, or potentially a predatory practice. [22]

**Free consumption alone does not prove predation.** The Autorité’s language identifies a possible competition-law characterization, not an infringement finding for all programs. A temporary zero effective price can support legitimate product discovery, customer acquisition or migration assistance. The important questions are how conditions influence customer choice, whether dependence persists, and whether exclusionary effects outweigh the relevant efficiencies.

**Smaller rivals can lose customer access.** A rival may need to compensate a startup for giving up unused credits and taking on migration costs before competing on ordinary service quality and price. If many promising startups are tied into the main ecosystems during their formative period, smaller providers may face a narrower route to growth and a longer path to profitability. These are the access-to-demand concerns identified by the Autorité, especially for customers with high innovation and development potential. [22]

**Startups also receive material benefits.** The UK Startup Coalition’s 2023 response strongly defended free credits and discounting as providing an important early boost and cheaper access to cloud infrastructure. Hyperscalers similarly described credits as discounts that support adoption and compensate switching costs. This is relevant stakeholder counterevidence: limiting support can itself increase startup costs. [24]

**Committed spending supplies a useful counterexample.** The CMA’s January 2025 provisional decision found that AWS and Microsoft committed-spend agreements were widespread and influenced workload allocation, but that rivals could profitably compete against them. In their then-current form and application, the CMA provisionally found that they did not harm competition. This prevents an inference that every discount encouraging concentrated purchases necessarily excludes competitors. [1]

**The pricing takeaway distinguishes the instruments.** Egress fees attracted a provisional finding of competitive harm, while startup credits remain a conditional concern requiring examination of eligibility, duration, provider specificity, migration dependence and efficiencies—not a blanket prohibition on subsidized cloud adoption.

### 3. Exclusive Licensing and Bundling

**Microsoft’s dual role creates the issue.** Microsoft supplies Azure infrastructure while also supplying software used on competing clouds, including Windows Server, SQL Server, Windows desktop products, Visual Studio and productivity suites. The competitive concern is whether its position in software gives it the ability and incentive to make rival infrastructure less attractive. By January 2025, the CMA provisionally found partial foreclosure of AWS and Google through relevant licensing practices. [1]

**BYOL concerns reuse of purchased licenses.** Bring Your Own License, or BYOL, allows a customer to deploy eligible licenses it has already acquired on cloud infrastructure rather than obtain a replacement license through the hosting provider. Rights differ by product, license terms, software version and hosting arrangement. Consequently, describing the issue as a single universal BYOL permission or prohibition would obscure the relevant restrictions.

| Licensing milestone | Documented position | Competitive relevance | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before October 2019 | Broader BYOL on dedicated hardware; shared-hosting restrictions | Hosting architecture affected portability | CMA historical account |
| 1 October 2019 | New restrictions for specified Listed Providers | Reduced eligible dedicated-host deployment | Official Microsoft announcement |
| 1 October 2022 | Expanded eligible hosting rights; Listed Provider exclusions persisted | Improved some alternatives, not equivalent access | Attributed industry account |
| January 2025 | Remaining price and non-price differences assessed | CMA found partial foreclosure provisionally | Provisional authority decision |

**The pre-2019 baseline was qualified.** The CMA explains that, with exceptions, perpetual on-premises licenses generally could not be deployed on non-Azure shared hardware before October 2019. Customers could, however, use licenses on dedicated hardware across providers. This matters because the 2019 change restricted an existing dedicated-host route; it did not replace a previously unrestricted right to deploy every Microsoft product on every public cloud. [1]

**The 2019 change is officially documented.** Microsoft announced on 1 August 2019 that, from 1 October, on-premises licenses purchased without Software Assurance and mobility rights could not be deployed on dedicated hosted cloud services offered by Microsoft, Alibaba, Amazon—including VMware Cloud on AWS—and Google. The notice protected existing software versions under licenses purchased before the effective date and identified License Mobility through Software Assurance and Azure Hybrid Benefit as routes available under their respective conditions. [25]

**The Listed Provider wording includes Microsoft.** The restriction was not formally expressed simply as “Azure allowed, every rival prohibited.” The competitive allegation concerns the interaction between restrictions, product-specific mobility rights and Azure-specific benefits. Formal inclusion in the same provider list does not establish equivalent effective licensing options for customers across the competing clouds.

**The 2022 changes improved some rights.** An industry account of the October 2022 changes describes expanded subscription-license portability to eligible hosts, Windows Server BYOL on shared hosting and per-virtual-machine licensing, while retaining exclusions for the four Listed Providers. CISPE’s November 2023 CMA filing also describes changes to the CSP-Hoster program but argues that these did not resolve its members’ central concerns. The expansion and the continuing restrictions are therefore both relevant to the chronology. [26][27]

**Restrictions differ across software products.** At the July 2024 hearing, Microsoft discussed the ability to BYOL SQL Server but not Windows Server to Listed Provider clouds. It defended treating server applications and operating systems differently, citing their relationship to hardware and concerns about validating licenses and preventing duplicate use. These are Microsoft’s explanations, rather than findings that the restrictions were necessary or proportionate. [17]

**The rival-cost mechanism is straightforward.** If a customer can reuse a license economically on Azure but must buy additional rights or a more expensive licensed service elsewhere, the rival’s total offering becomes relatively more costly. The rival can pass that additional input cost to customers, absorb it in its margin, or offer a less complete service. Each response can weaken its competitive position even if it continues operating.

**CISPE reports substantial cost differences.** Its November 2023 filing cites a CISPE-funded study by Professor Frédéric Jenny estimating an approximately 28% additional licensing burden for European companies using Microsoft software on competing infrastructure. The filing calls this an additional “tax,” but it is a stakeholder’s estimate of licensing differences, not a statutory tax or a finding that every customer’s total cloud bill rises by 28%. [27]

**A specific SQL comparison adds detail.** One CISPE member compared SQL Server Enterprise Edition licensing for a 32-core virtual machine and reported that the SPLA service-provider licensing route was about 20% more expensive than the Azure-direct end-customer price. It separately reported annual Extended Security Update charges equal to 100% of the software purchase-license price, with the same updates free to Microsoft’s direct Azure customers. These are attributed member comparisons, not market-wide average premiums. [27]

**Microsoft disputes foreclosure and materiality.** At its July 2024 hearing, Microsoft argued that license fees did not materially raise AWS’s or Google’s costs and that those companies had sufficient margins to compete. It also explained that its advertised “up to five times” AWS-versus-Azure Windows Server and SQL Server comparison concerned a niche case involving older software requiring security updates, rather than the typical customer’s entire workload. [17]

**The denominator determines economic significance.** A license charge can be small relative to a provider’s worldwide cloud revenues yet large relative to the particular workload being contested. The CMA’s January 2025 analysis emphasizes that customers do not always choose all their cloud services in one purchasing decision. Where competition concerns a narrower bundle, assessing the input against all cloud spending can dilute its apparent importance. [1]

**The CMA tested workload-level significance.** Appendix T uses 2022 Azure UK customer usage and asks what the licensing inputs would cost at AWS’s SPLA wholesale prices. For customers spending USD 10,000–1 million annually on Azure, its illustrative estimates were as follows. These are hypothetical input-cost calculations using observed usage, not measured rival-cloud customer price premiums. [28]

| Software input and scenario | Share of all Azure-service spending | Share of relevant workload spending |
|---|---:|---:|
| Windows Server | [10–20]% | [60–70]% |
| SQL Server, 50:50 Enterprise/Standard mix | [20–30]% | [60–70]% |
| SQL Server, 25:75 Enterprise/Standard mix | [10–20]% | [40–50]% |

**Those calculations use different workload denominators.** For Windows Server, the narrower denominator is Windows Server virtual-machine spending. For SQL Server, it is Windows Server virtual-machine spending plus SQL Server intellectual-property spending on Azure. The SQL scenarios vary the assumed edition mix; the analysis excludes SQL PaaS products and customers spending less than USD 10,000. The rows should therefore not be added together as if they were components of one measured price premium. [28]

**The January 2025 position went further.** Unlike its June 2024 initial licensing paper, which had made no provisional finding, the CMA’s January report provisionally found significant Microsoft market power in the relevant software products and that they were important cloud inputs. It found price or quality differences between Azure and AWS/Google, including some rival input prices exceeding Microsoft’s own customer-facing prices. [29][1]

**The authority identifies both price channels.** For Windows Server, the CMA’s detailed provisional analysis compares rival input costs with Azure customer-facing prices where customers have pre-existing licenses qualifying for Azure Hybrid Benefit. For SQL Server, it identifies rival input costs above Microsoft’s pay-as-you-go customer-facing price. It also recognizes that price comparisons differ for customers who do not qualify for Azure Hybrid Benefit. [1]

**Non-price restrictions can also matter.** The CMA records restrictions affecting deployment of Windows desktop and productivity software on AWS and Google, with an exception for certain Microsoft 365 licenses on Amazon WorkSpaces. Such products are important inputs to virtual desktop infrastructure, or VDI—remotely hosted desktop environments. Although VDI represented a relatively small proportion of overall public-cloud usage, the CMA considered the affected offerings material to its competitive assessment. [1]

**Partial foreclosure does not require exit.** The CMA’s concern is that AWS and Google become materially less effective competitors for affected demand. Their profitability elsewhere does not, by itself, disprove this mechanism. The January report found evidence consistent with higher customer prices, lower margins on relevant workloads and disproportionate Microsoft-software use on Azure, while recognizing that customers’ circumstances and price comparisons varied. [1]

**Licensing can reinforce broader concentration.** The CMA’s 2024 framework identifies a further channel: less contestable software-dependent demand can reduce rivals’ opportunities to achieve scale, learning and network advantages. When multicloud integration is difficult, a decision driven by Microsoft-software costs can also influence placement of associated workloads that do not themselves use that software. This connects the input-pricing issue to the structural mechanisms in Part One. [29]

**Teams bundling concerns a separate market.** On 25 June 2024, the European Commission issued a Statement of Objections expressing its preliminary view that Microsoft had tied Teams to Office 365 and Microsoft 365 business productivity applications since at least April 2019. It preliminarily found Microsoft dominant worldwide in professional-use SaaS productivity applications. The alleged harm concerned competition in communications and collaboration products, rather than a measured change in IaaS/PaaS shares. [18]

**Tying changes the distribution contest.** Bundling combines products in an offering; tying adds the concern that customers cannot obtain the desired product without acquiring the associated one. The Commission’s preliminary theory was that customers subscribing to the productivity applications were not given a choice whether to acquire Teams. This gave Teams access to customers through an established productivity-suite relationship instead of requiring a separate communications-product purchasing decision. [18]

**The advantage extends beyond convenience.** A competing collaboration supplier must persuade a customer to adopt another product when access to Teams already accompanies the suite. The Commission was concerned that this distribution advantage could prevent rivals from competing and innovating. It also considered the conduct capable of defending Microsoft’s productivity position and suite-centric business model against suppliers of individual applications. These are the stated preliminary mechanisms, not a quantified estimate of rival sales lost.

**Interoperability can amplify the bundle.** The Commission identified limitations between competing Teams products and Microsoft offerings as potentially exacerbating the distribution advantage. A rival may need access to the surrounding productivity environment to offer an equally useful service. If that integration is less effective, the rival faces both a customer-acquisition disadvantage and a functional disadvantage. [18]

**Integration itself can benefit customers.** Teams combines messaging, calls, meetings and file sharing, and connects workplace tools. Those functions can be valuable together. The competition concern is therefore not simply that Microsoft combines useful services. It concerns the combination of power in productivity applications, restricted customer choice over acquiring Teams, and interoperability conditions affecting competing collaboration tools.

**Unbundling changes were already under examination.** After proceedings opened in July 2023, Microsoft began offering some suites without Teams. The Commission’s June 2024 preliminary assessment found these changes insufficient to address its concerns. The relevant assessment therefore extends beyond the existence of a nominal standalone option to the practical conditions under which customers can choose competing products. [18]

**The complaints identify the competitive context.** The Commission opened its investigation on 27 July 2023 following a complaint by Slack Technologies, owned by Salesforce, and its Statement of Objections also covered a complaint received from alfaview on 20 July 2023. These named cases concern suppliers competing with Teams, helping locate the dispute in communications and collaboration rather than treating it as a general finding against all Microsoft cloud integration. [18]

**Licensing and bundling use different leverage.** Licensing affects the effective price or availability of software inputs on competing infrastructure. Teams tying affects distribution of a collaboration product through a powerful productivity suite. Both involve adjacent-market relationships, but neither substitutes for evidence about the other. A Teams distribution concern is not automatically proof of Azure foreclosure, just as a Windows Server licensing concern does not establish a Teams violation.

**The legal positions remain distinct.** The CMA’s provisional adverse-effect-on-competition finding arises from its market-investigation framework, whereas the Commission’s preliminary Teams case concerns potential abuse of dominance under Article 102 TFEU. For licensing, equivalent practical access to relevant software inputs would weaken the identified rival-cost mechanism. For Teams, meaningful customer choice and effective interoperability would address the stated distribution mechanism; the Commission’s preliminary view was that the changes assessed by June 2024 had not yet done so.

**The licensing-and-bundling takeaway is differentiated.** The early-2025 record supports a provisional authority finding that Microsoft licensing weakened AWS and Google, alongside a separate preliminary Commission case that Teams tying conferred an undue distribution advantage in collaboration software.

**Evidence basis and measurement.** The cutoff is 28 January 2025. Country rows retain their original service definitions, periods and measurement bases; † values are calculated bounds, not provider point estimates. Korean figures are attributed through KED Global’s account of the KFTC study. Survey percentages describe responses, not causal effects. Cloudflare and CISPE figures remain external or interested-party estimates, while the CMA and Commission positions identified as provisional or preliminary retain that status.

**The central conclusion is contestability.** The authorities’ recurring concern is not merely that a few providers are large, but that established customer demand can become difficult for rivals to win. Scale and integration deliver real benefits; competition weakens when technical dependence, transfer pricing or differentiated software access prevents customers from choosing alternative services on their merits.
