# Generative AI and the Future Labor Market: Research Evidence Before June 2023

**Productivity gains are the clearest finding.** Research circulated before 1 June 2023 supports the view that generative AI can accelerate particular tasks, assist workers, and reorganize professional work: one controlled GitHub Copilot experiment reported task completion **55.8% faster**, while a U.S. occupational analysis estimated that approximately 15% of worker tasks could be completed significantly faster using a large language model. These findings establish an important productivity opportunity, rather than a corresponding percentage increase or decrease in employment. [1][2]

**Employment consequences depend on implementation.** Negative perspectives emphasize task substitution, pressure on particular occupations, unequal access to complementary skills, and potential wage losses. Earlier AI and automation research provides substantial evidence for these distributional mechanisms. The principal challenges are reliability, skills, bias, privacy, intellectual-property governance, and worker control; the strongest opportunities involve complementary task design, wider access to productive tools, new implementation work, and training supported by worker participation. [3][4][5]

**The report follows four questions.** What benefits does the literature demonstrate or plausibly explain? Which workers face substitution and distributional risks? What implementation and transition challenges determine the outcome? Which opportunities could improve employment, access, and job quality? A brief scope discussion establishes the evidence categories, followed by separate sections on positive views, negative views, specific challenges, and future opportunities. The report ends with industry and occupational application tables.

## Scope and the pre-June-2023 evidence base

### What counts as generative AI and employment impact?

**Generative AI changes content-producing tasks.** This report concentrates on systems that generate text or software code, particularly large language models, ChatGPT, GPT-based tools, and GitHub Copilot. Its central distinction is between generating an output and successfully completing a work process. A model may produce a draft, answer, or code suggestion, while a worker still defines the problem, supplies context, checks the result, and assumes responsibility for its use. The programming experiments and the April 2023 marketing commentary make this distinction particularly concrete. [1][4]

**Employment includes more than headcount.** Relevant outcomes include hiring, redundancies, hours worked, wages, occupational mobility, access to work, and job quality. Job quality includes autonomy, meaningful task content, safety, intensity, and surveillance. A technology can improve one outcome while worsening another: work may become faster but more closely monitored, or employment may remain stable while recruitment into particular roles slows. The social-science literature therefore treats technological unemployment and algorithmic management as related but distinct issues. [6]

**Tasks are not entire occupations.** An occupation combines activities with different technical, interpersonal, and contextual requirements. Automating document preparation does not necessarily automate client negotiation, just as assisting programming does not automatically replace software design, debugging, or responsibility for a deployed system. Frank and colleagues’ 2019 *PNAS* article explains why skill bundles and changing task requirements are more informative than treating occupations as indivisible units. [7]

### Direct evidence and adjacent evidence

**Direct studies address generative systems.** Eloundou and colleagues’ *GPTs are GPTs*, first submitted on 17 March 2023, assesses the U.S. workforce using a task rubric informed by human judgments and GPT-4 classifications. Felten, Raj, and Seamans’ language-model exposure paper, submitted on 2 March and revised on 18 March 2023, identifies affected U.S. occupations, industries, and locations. Both examine potential exposure: the relationship between technological capabilities and work requirements. [2][8]

**Experiments answer narrower performance questions.** Peng and colleagues’ 13 February 2023 preprint compares developers assigned to complete a JavaScript HTTP-server task with or without Copilot. Vaithilingam, Zhang, and Glassman’s CHI 2022 extended abstract compares Copilot and IntelliSense within a 24-person programming study. These designs provide evidence about performance under specified task conditions; their contribution differs from estimating changes in employment across firms or national economies. [1][9]

**Surveys illuminate acceptance and practical use.** Jaworski’s 12 January 2023 preprint reports 42 programmer responses to an 18-question questionnaire about Copilot. Agossaha and colleagues’ April 2023 study reports 19 responses from a 41-person IT department concerning generative tools. Their findings help explain why an apparently useful capability may encounter security concerns, limited adoption, or uneven enthusiasm inside organizations. [10][11]

**Adjacent research explains labor-market mechanisms.** Earlier studies concern machine learning, natural-language processing, robotics, software, and algorithmic management more broadly. These technologies are not interchangeable with generative AI, but their research identifies mechanisms that remain relevant: substitution, complementary work, changes in demand, skill shortages, and distributional conflict. The report uses this literature to explain possible employment pathways, while identifying its technological scope. [12][13]

**Publication status remains visible throughout.** Peer-reviewed articles, conference research, preprints, and working papers all contribute, but they represent different stages and forms of evidence. OECD working papers provide supplementary scholarly policy evidence, particularly on workplace implementation and cross-country employment patterns. Conceptual articles establish arguments and identify risks; experiments measure specified interventions; exposure studies map potential effects; observational studies examine associations in labor-market data.

**The central interpretive rule is simple:** technical exposure identifies where work may change, while adoption, task organization, demand, and institutions determine how those changes reach workers.

**Additional documented evidence**

**GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models** is a 2023 preprint or working-paper study focused on U.S. occupation-specific tasks. It uses human LLM experts and GPT-4 to assess whether LLM capabilities could reduce the time required for occupational tasks [65][22]. Its main contribution to the synthesis is thus a capability-alignment and potential-exposure result. It supports the possibility that LLMs may affect knowledge-intensive tasks, but it does not by itself measure adoption, worker replacement, employment growth or decline, wages, productivity, or job quality [65][22].

Accordingly, the study can inform both the positive and negative sections, but only conditionally. If LLMs augment workers or generate complementary tasks, exposure could become a productivity or new-task opportunity; if firms use the capability to substitute for labor, the same exposure could create displacement or bargaining risks. The study does not determine which pathway will dominate. Its evidence status is modeled or rubric-based potential exposure, not an experiment, causal estimate, or observed labor-market change [65][22].

## Positive views on generative AI’s impact on employment

### Productivity gains can expand productive capacity

**Task acceleration has substantial potential.** Eloundou and colleagues estimate that approximately 15% of all U.S. worker tasks could be completed significantly faster at the same quality with access to an LLM. When software and tools built around LLMs are included, the estimated share increases to 47–56%. The important result is therefore not only that models can assist individual activities, but that complementary software could extend their usefulness across a much larger portion of work. [2]

**Software integration changes the scale.** The difference between the two estimates is a calculated **32–41 percentage points**: 47−15 to 56−15. This is an expansion in estimated task coverage, not an economy-wide productivity growth rate. It suggests that the economic importance of generative AI depends partly on the systems built around models—interfaces, task-specific applications, and connections to existing workflows—rather than on conversational access alone. [2]

**Broad exposure creates widespread augmentation possibilities.** The same study estimates that approximately 80% of U.S. workers could have at least 10% of their tasks affected, while approximately 19% could have at least half affected. These are nested worker groups, not separate shares to add together. Their positive interpretation is that assistance could reach many occupations, including workers whose jobs would remain predominantly human. [2]

**Productivity reaches employment through demand.** When assistance reduces the cost of a service, firms may supply more of it, improve its quality, or serve customers previously priced out of the market. Labor demand can consequently rise in complementary activities even as labor requirements fall for a particular task. Webb’s task-based model and the 2022 cross-country AI study explain this productivity channel alongside the opposing substitution channel. [12][13]

**Demand expansion is the decisive qualification.** Faster production is most likely to support employment when customers want substantially more output or when improved services generate additional needs. Where demand changes little, employers have stronger incentives to convert time savings into reduced staffing requirements. The positive literature is therefore strongest when it explains the connection between lower costs, expanded output, and remaining human tasks, rather than assuming that productivity automatically creates jobs. [12]

### Programming studies show both gains and constraints

**Copilot provides a concrete positive case.** Peng and colleagues report that developers assigned access to GitHub Copilot completed a JavaScript HTTP-server task 55.8% faster than the control group. The authors also identify heterogeneous effects and describe implications for transitions into software-development careers. Their result supports the proposition that generative assistance can lower the effort required to perform a specified programming task. [1]

| Research and date | Design and scope | Main result | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peng et al., 13 February 2023 | Controlled JavaScript task | Completion 55.8% faster | Strong task-specific acceleration |
| Vaithilingam et al., CHI 2022 | Within-subjects study; 24 participants | About one minute faster; p=.53 | Usefulness exceeded demonstrated speed gain |
| ICSE Companion study, May 2022 | Experiment; 21 participants | More lines added, then more deleted | Output quantity and quality diverged |

The three studies examine different tasks and performance measures. [1][9][14]

**Worker preference adds another dimension.** In Vaithilingam and colleagues’ study, 19 of 24 participants preferred Copilot, and 23 found it more helpful. Average helpfulness scores were 6.16 for Copilot and 4.45 for IntelliSense, with p<.001. Participants could therefore value assistance even when the mean completion-time advantage—approximately one minute—was not statistically significant, with p=.53. [9]

**Perceived help and successful completion differ.** Copilot users failed five tasks, compared with two failures under IntelliSense. Only 10 of the 24 participants felt more confident in Copilot-generated code; seven raised reliability concerns, five perceived a loss of control, and eight trusted it only for simple tasks. These findings make the positive conclusion more precise: assistance can improve the experience of programming without uniformly improving every measure of performance. [9]

**Quality-adjusted productivity matters most.** The 21-participant ICSE experiment similarly found increased productivity measured as lines of code added, alongside inferior quality indicated by more subsequent deletions. Lines generated are an intermediate output; usable software is the economically relevant outcome. Comparing these studies suggests that employment benefits depend on whether faster production survives testing, correction, integration, and maintenance. [14]

**Security concerns influence realized adoption.** Jaworski’s survey found predominantly positive attitudes but divided opinions and limited willingness to use Copilot, with security an important concern. This contrasts with a purely capability-centered account: a tool can be technically useful yet adopted cautiously because workers or organizations face consequences from insecure code or inappropriate use. The employment opportunity consequently includes developing trustworthy working practices, not merely distributing access. [10]

### Early workplace use suggests broad task augmentation

**Workers identified several useful applications.** In Agossaha and colleagues’ single-department study, 16 of 19 respondents had tried ChatGPT. Seven knew about Copilot, and two had tried it. Thirteen respondents regarded generative tools as useful for work, identifying development support, information retrieval, email writing, and advice concerning pre-sales, management, or proofs of concept. [11]

**Reported uses extend beyond coding.** Five respondents mentioned development support, four information retrieval, three email writing, and three the grouped advisory applications. These application counts overlap and should not be interpreted as mutually exclusive worker categories. The pattern indicates that generative assistance may affect surrounding office activities as well as a technical occupation’s central production task. [11]

**Complementarity involves a division of responsibility.** The April 2023 commentary *Marketing with ChatGPT* describes assistance with product descriptions, promotional messages, research, personalized emails, and customer-service responses. It also recommends validation against other information, expert interpretation, and human oversight. The complementary arrangement is therefore not simply “human plus machine”: the system produces candidate material, while the professional contributes contextual judgment, accountability, and selection. [4]

**Human feedback can improve both sides.** Deranty and Corbin’s review discusses earlier workplace research in which AI helped workers avoid errors, while workers’ feedback strengthened the AI applications. This reciprocal relationship offers a stronger augmentation mechanism than one-way automation. Workers supply knowledge about exceptions and context; systems provide assistance that can make human performance more effective. [6]

### Job quality can improve through task reorganization

**Reorganization can remove undesirable work.** Milanez’s April 2023 OECD research covers 96 manufacturing and finance workplace cases across Austria, Canada, France, Germany, Ireland, Japan, the United Kingdom, and the United States. It reports that job reorganization was more prevalent than displacement, with less tedium, greater engagement, and improved physical safety among the benefits described. These are broader-AI cases, providing workplace mechanisms rather than generative-AI-specific effects. [15]

**The benefit depends on remaining tasks.** The cases describe situations in which automating a task increased demand for human work surrounding it, especially where people retained a comparative advantage. This matters for job quality because removing repetitive activity can leave workers with more varied or engaging responsibilities. It also matters for staffing: savings in one activity can make another activity more valuable or necessary. [15]

**Survey respondents generally viewed AI positively.** Lane, Williams, and Broecke’s OECD working paper, published on 27 March 2023, surveys employers and workers in manufacturing and finance in seven countries: the same country group except Japan. Both groups were generally positive about performance and working conditions. Training and worker consultation were associated with better worker outcomes, suggesting that organizational practices help determine whether technical improvements become experienced benefits. [5]

**Physical and cognitive benefits differ.** Safety improvements in manufacturing should not be transferred mechanically to text-generation tools. For generative AI, the more immediate positive mechanisms concern reduced drafting effort, assistance with information processing, and potential movement toward judgment-intensive work. The broader workplace evidence is useful because it demonstrates that task redesign can improve work, while the direct generative studies identify which activities might be redesigned.

### AI development creates complementary labor demand

**Systems require continuing human work.** Milanez’s cases report growth in AI-related occupations because systems must be built, trained, updated, and maintained. This is a concrete job-creation pathway: adopting an automated application also generates implementation and support requirements. Its employment significance depends on the scale and skill composition of that work relative to the tasks displaced elsewhere. [15]

**Specialist employment was already expanding.** Green and Lamby’s 16 February 2023 OECD working paper estimates that workers with skills to develop and maintain AI represented 0.34% of employment across its country sample in 2019. Its detailed results report estimated employment growth of 63% for this workforce between 2017 and 2019, compared with 3% overall. These figures concern the broader AI workforce, not jobs newly created by ChatGPT or other generative systems. [16]

**The positive judgment is conditional augmentation:** generative AI can increase productive capacity and improve selected aspects of work, with employment benefits strongest where demand expands and organizations preserve valuable human responsibilities.

**Additional documented evidence**

Pre-cutoff evidence supports a qualified positive view: GenAI is better evidenced as task augmentation and productivity potential than as demonstrated net employment growth. Eloundou et al.’s *GPTs are GPTs* (U.S., 17 March 2023) estimated that 15% of all worker tasks could be completed significantly faster at the same quality with an LLM, rising to 47–56% with LLM software; about 80% of the U.S. workforce could have ≥10% of tasks affected and about 19% of workers ≥50% [33]. These potential-exposure estimates are not observed job creation or loss; adoption timing was not modeled [33].

Complementarity is the mechanism: AI handles some tasks while human judgment, creativity, and social skills remain. De Acypreste and Paraná’s and Deranty and Corbin’s 2022 reviews report productivity, new-task, and inclusive-growth arguments, but also heterogeneous definitions and deployment conditions; they are broad-AI context, not direct GenAI evidence [30][31]. Lane and Saint-Martin’s 2021 OECD review adds that skills, training, social dialogue, displacement support, and responsible use condition productivity gains and new opportunities [29].

An interview study of eight German organizations (41 interviews, 2022) reported gradual automation of manual or repetitive tasks, movement toward reasoning and empathy, new roles and skills, and upskilling of former call-centre employees rather than whole-role elimination; leadership support, participatory change management, and domain knowledge facilitated adoption [37]. In education, a 2022 AI review—not specifically GenAI—found pedagogical agents improved K–12 learning (g = 0.19, p < .05), but classroom implementation was rare and the outcome was learning, not teacher employment [32]. Positive evidence therefore supports task, productivity, skill, and possible new-role gains—not aggregate employment gains.

Contextual broader-AI evidence (not direct GenAI): across 23 OECD countries and 36 occupations (2012–19), exposure correlated with employment growth in high-computer-use occupations; productivity gains may offset displacement for digitally skilled workers, but the correlation is not causal [26].

## Negative views on generative AI’s impact on employment

### Task substitution can reduce hiring or staffing needs

**Automation can remove paid activities.** The April 2023 marketing commentary explicitly identifies potential job losses when ChatGPT performs activities previously undertaken by people. It describes copy editors and creative writers moving from writing toward editing, marketing researchers facing automated data analysis, and customer-service representatives encountering automated responses. These are proposed occupational transformations, supported by a conceptual analysis rather than a measured layoff total. [4]

**Editing is not automatically equivalent employment.** A shift from producing drafts to checking them may preserve professional involvement while reducing the labor required per item. Whether it sustains employment depends on the amount of additional content commissioned and the time needed for review. The negative interpretation is strongest when generation replaces a substantial volume of work and the remaining supervisory tasks require fewer paid hours.

**Hiring can change before redundancies appear.** The 2022 *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence* study summarizes U.S. research by Acemoglu and colleagues showing that AI-exposed firms restricted hiring into non-AI positions relative to other firms, despite no detected occupational-level employment effect. This identifies an important adjustment margin: opportunities for new entrants can contract even while incumbent workers remain employed. The finding concerns broader AI exposure, not a separately identified ChatGPT adoption effect. [13]

**Stable headcount can conceal adjustment.** Milanez’s cases similarly describe steady employment accompanied by slower job growth, limited AI-linked redundancies, reassignment, slower hiring, and attrition, including non-replacement of retiring workers. From a current employee’s perspective, reallocation may be relatively protective. From a job seeker’s perspective, the same process can mean fewer vacancies and a narrower entry route. [15]

**Output and labor requirements interact.** A simple accounting relationship clarifies the issue: total labor hours equal output multiplied by human labor hours per unit. If generative AI reduces hours per unit, total hours rise only when output expansion and additional human activities more than compensate. Headcount also depends on hours per worker. This captures the substitution-versus-demand mechanism developed in Webb’s model without treating technical task exposure as a staffing forecast. [12]

### Professional and higher-paid work is exposed

**Higher income does not ensure protection.** Eloundou and colleagues find exposure across wage levels, with higher-income jobs potentially more exposed to LLM capabilities and LLM-powered software. Felten, Raj, and Seamans likewise report a positive relationship between wages and language-model exposure. This contrasts with a simple account in which automation primarily threatens low-paid manual or clerical work. [2][8]

**Language-intensive professions are prominent cases.** Felten and colleagues identify telemarketers and post-secondary teachers in English, foreign languages, and history among highly exposed occupations. Legal services and securities, commodities, and investment activities are highly exposed industries. Their shared feature is substantial dependence on language and information processing, rather than a common level of education, status, or remuneration. [8]

**Ability portfolios explain occupational differences.** The earlier Felten, Raj, and Seamans exposure framework links 10 AI applications to 52 occupational abilities and aggregates them across 774 U.S. six-digit occupations. After adjustment for ability breadth, physicists rank 61st, at 1.35 standard deviations above mean exposure, while surgeons rank 387th, at 0.05 standard deviations below it. The contrast shows why exposure depends on the combination of abilities a job requires, not simply its educational level. [17]

**Lower-paid work can retain technical protection.** Deranty and Corbin discuss the difficulty of automating sensorimotor and context-dependent activities, including cleaning, gardening, and some other service work. Professional roles can contain codifiable information-processing tasks, while apparently basic occupations may require difficult physical adaptation. Generative AI therefore challenges the assumption that higher formal qualifications necessarily correspond to lower technological exposure. [6]

### Wage effects can be negative without mass unemployment

**Historical automation generated concentrated wage losses.** Acemoglu and Restrepo’s June 2021 NBER working paper examines U.S. labor-market changes between 1980 and 2016. It attributes 50–70% of changes in the wage structure to relative wage declines among groups specialized in routine tasks in rapidly automating industries. This is a share of observed wage-structure changes explained by their analysis, not a claim that wages fell by 50–70%. [3]

**Displacement weakens comparative advantage.** Their mechanism is that automation reallocates tasks from workers to capital, reducing demand for groups whose established advantage lay in those tasks. Workers can remain employed but move into less advantageous activities. Competition for remaining tasks can also transmit wage pressure to other groups, extending the effects beyond the workers directly exposed. [3]

**Employment participation can also deteriorate.** In one reported specification, a 10-percentage-point increase in task displacement is associated with a 4.6-percentage-point decline in the employment-to-population ratio and a 3.7-percentage-point rise in non-participation. The study thus identifies adjustment through both earnings and participation. Its historical technologies include machinery, robots, and software, providing a mechanism to consider rather than a numerical forecast for generative AI. [3]

**Other findings point in different directions.** Earlier AI-exposure research also reports wage growth for some complementary workers, and vacancy studies identify premiums for AI skills. These findings do not invalidate the displacement mechanism. They show that workers developing, applying, and competing with a technology occupy different positions in the labor market.

| Research | Population and period | Reported wage result | Main interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acemoglu–Restrepo, June 2021 | U.S. worker groups, 1980–2016 | Routine-task relative wage declines | Loss of task advantage |
| Webb, January 2020 | Historical occupation-industry analysis, 1980–2010 | Lower wages with robot/software exposure | Suggestive substitution pattern |
| Felten et al., 2019, summarized in 2022 | U.S. occupations | Positive wage growth in selected groups | Software-skill complementarity |
| AI-skills discussion paper, January 2020 | Online vacancies, 2010–2019 | Premium for AI-skill vacancies | Reward for complementary skills |
| Green–Lamby, February 2023 | OECD sample, 2017–2019 | AI wages +7%; overall +9% | High demand with expanding supply |

These studies use different wage measures and designs; the comparison concerns mechanisms rather than a pooled effect size. [3][12][13][18][16]

**Historical magnitudes illustrate uneven exposure.** Webb reports that moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of robot exposure was associated with 9–18% lower within-industry employment shares and 8–14% lower wages over 1980–2010. Corresponding software-exposure associations were 7–11% lower employment shares and 2–6% lower wages. He characterizes these results as suggestive rather than causal. [12]

**Complementary skills can attract wage premiums.** The January 2020 discussion paper *The Demand for AI Skills in the Labor Market* reports a substantial premium in vacancies requiring AI skills and a premium for non-AI vacancies at firms with many AI vacancies. Managerial occupations had the highest AI-skill premium. Its firm-level relationships are cross-sectional, making the evidence consistent with several explanations, including skill scarcity, productive firms selecting AI, and valuable organizational complementarities. [18]

**High wages do not imply exceptional growth.** Green and Lamby find that the broader AI workforce was comparatively well paid, but cumulative wage growth over 2017–2019 was 7%, against 9% overall. Employment and hours indicators suggested strong demand, while the wage pattern suggested that supply could be keeping pace. This distinction matters for future opportunities: a growing occupation need not experience continually increasing scarcity premiums. [16]

### Inequality can change at different points

**Average gains can conceal distributional losses.** Acemoglu and Restrepo’s general-equilibrium analysis explains approximately half of observed relative wage changes and about 80% of the increase in the college premium over 1980–2016. Their task-displacement technologies account for only approximately 3.8% cumulative total-factor-productivity growth over the period. The important argument is that substantial redistribution can accompany comparatively modest efficiency gains. [3]

**Different inequality measures can move oppositely.** Webb conditionally applies historical software and robot wage coefficients to AI exposure. Under those assumptions, the ratio of wages at the 90th and 10th percentiles falls by 4% using the software coefficient or 9% using the robot coefficient, while the 99th-to-90th-percentile ratio increases. These are alternative conditional projections, not a confidence interval or observed generative-AI effect. [12]

**The mechanism differs from traditional polarization.** If AI affects many upper-middle-income information-processing occupations, it could compress some wage differences while leaving gains concentrated among a smaller group at the top. Conversely, workers possessing complementary technical or organizational skills could gain relative to others. The relevant question is therefore which part of the distribution changes, rather than whether “inequality” rises or falls as a single undifferentiated outcome.

**Access to specialist opportunities is unequal.** Green and Lamby estimate that approximately two-thirds of the AI workforce held tertiary qualifications. Women represented 35% of that workforce, compared with 53% of employed tertiary-qualified workers in their comparison group. Almost half of AI workers in the European OECD earnings analysis were above the 80th earnings percentile. Growth in AI-related employment consequently begins from a relatively advantaged and demographically uneven base. [16]

### Countries and worker groups experience different adjustments

**National averages obscure different mechanisms.** The U.S. exposure studies, the 23-country OECD analysis, and the Chinese service-industry panel address different technologies and outcomes. Their results nevertheless provide a useful comparative judgment: technological effects depend on occupational composition, existing digital capabilities, industry structure, and workers’ opportunities to move between tasks.

| Setting and research | Evidence basis | Main employment finding | Distributional pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States; March 2023 studies | Direct LLM task exposure | Broad potential task changes | Higher-paid work also exposed |
| 23 OECD countries; 2022 study | AI exposure, 2012–2019 | No clear overall employment-growth relationship | Gains concentrated in high-computer-use work |
| China; 2022 service-industry study | Provincial panel, 2007–2020 | Positive overall service-employment association | High-skilled gains; low-skilled declines |

Sources: U.S. LLM exposure [2]; OECD-country study [13]; Chinese service-industry study [19]

**Digital capability changes the association.** The 2022 cross-country study finds greater AI exposure associated with stronger employment growth in occupations with high computer use. For occupations with low computer use, it reports suggestive evidence of slower growth in average hours. One interpretation is that existing digital capabilities help workers complement AI, while other workers encounter a different adjustment through hours. The study itself leaves the precise mechanisms open. [13]

**China combines expansion with skill displacement.** *Can Artificial Intelligence Boost Employment in Service Industries?*, published in 2022, analyzes 31 Chinese provinces and autonomous regions using province and year fixed effects. It reports a positive overall service-employment relationship, alongside higher high-skilled employment and lower low-skilled employment. AI development is proxied by service-industry robot-installation density, so the findings concern broader AI and robotics. [19]

**Skill upgrading is not necessarily polarization.** The Chinese study explicitly states that nationwide employment polarization had not yet appeared. Higher-skilled gains and lower-skilled losses indicate uneven adjustment, but do not by themselves demonstrate the classic pattern of expansion at both the top and bottom with a shrinking middle. The same study reports intensified competition for medium-skilled service jobs as manufacturing workers moved into services. [19]

**Regional structure changes the outcome.** In eastern China, a 1% increase in the study’s AI-development measure corresponds to a 0.439% increase in service employment. High-end services, including information transmission, software, and IT, were especially important there; the central region showed a different industry pattern. This supports regional differentiation rather than transferring one national or regional coefficient to every labor market. [19]

**International differences are substantively important.** Frank and colleagues’ 2019 review also contrasts retrospective evidence of declining manufacturing opportunities associated with robotics in the United States with a different German pattern. Their wider argument is that local skills, mobility, industry networks, and institutions influence adjustment. Generative AI’s global reach therefore does not imply a uniform global employment response. [7]

### Automation forecasts differ because their assumptions differ

**Large risk estimates are method-sensitive.** Earlier automation studies offer useful comparisons of forecasting methods. They should be read as assessments of technical susceptibility under particular definitions, rather than as percentages of jobs destined to disappear after generative AI adoption.

| Research | Geographic scope | Reported estimate | Measurement approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frey–Osborne, 2017 | United States | 47% of jobs at high risk | Occupation-level computerization |
| Arntz et al., 2016 | OECD countries | About 9%; Austria 12%, South Korea 6% | Task-sensitive occupational assessment |
| Nedelkoska–Quintini, 2018 | 32 countries | 14% highly automatable; 32% intermediate risk | Separate automation-risk bands |

These estimates are summarized in the pre-cutoff social-science review. [6]

**Within-occupation variation changes the estimate.** Occupation-level methods can assign a common risk to workers sharing a title, whereas task-sensitive methods account for differences in what those workers actually do. The 32-country study defines highly automatable jobs as having estimated automation probability above 70%, with the intermediate group at 50–70%. These classifications explain why seemingly similar discussions can produce substantially different headline figures. [6]

**The strongest negative judgment concerns uneven adjustment:** generative AI can reduce demand for particular paid tasks, constrain entry opportunities, and redistribute earnings even when total employment remains resilient.

**Additional documented evidence**

Direct pre-cutoff evidence supports a conditional negative view: LLM capability can expose tasks to substitution and inequality, but exposure is not job loss. Eloundou et al.’s U.S. assessment (17 March 2023) estimated that 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of tasks affected and 19% of workers could have at least 50% of tasks affected; higher-income jobs could be more exposed [38][41]. It estimated faster completion for 15% of all tasks with an LLM and 47–56% with LLM software [38]. These are potential rubric estimates, not adoption, unemployment, wage, or displacement observations, and do not distinguish augmentation from substitution [25].

Substitution is the risk when time savings meet no new demand. Technical feasibility is not full automation: social, economic, regulatory and organizational conditions, task definitions, annotator judgments, and GPT-4 prompting matter. Writing/coding and higher-wage occupations are exposed, but the unit is tasks, not jobs; the study flags potential labor disruption and disparity [25].

A single-firm, single-occupation customer-support study using September 2019–June 2021 data, including an August 2020 randomized trial, found AI recommendations associated with 15% higher productivity, larger gains for lower-skill and novice agents, and lower turnover [40]. Its conditional calculation—12% fewer worker-hours for the same issue volume if demand is inelastic—is not observed job loss; stronger demand, model-testing, or human review could offset it. Attrition combines voluntary and involuntary exits [40].

**Contextual evidence**

de Acypreste and Paraná’s review reports model-based risk for 47% of U.S. jobs (at least 70% automation probability in the following period) and 54.45% of Brazil’s formal jobs, plus high-skill complementarity, routine middle-skill replacement, and little low-skill effect [30]. These are not realized GenAI effects. It records service-sector wage pressure, gig/microwork insecurity, surveillance, privacy, and discrimination; it also finds more optimistic than skeptical accounts, dispersed metrics, and no common AI definition [30]. Frank et al. link technological shocks to uneven skill demand, geography, and income, but the supplied record reports no direct GenAI displacement or wage loss [39]. Deranty and Corbin’s broad AI/work record likewise supplies no direct GenAI negative outcome [34].

Negative effects therefore require adoption, substitution over augmentation, weak demand offsets, and limited worker or institutional adjustment—not exposure alone [38][40].

For the future labor market, broader-AI evidence finds job/wage polarization across the United States, Japan and nine European countries: technology complements high-skill work but replaces routine medium-skilled work; four macroeconomic models indicate growth with inequality, short-run wage reductions and only possible long-run wage growth [30]. It also warns that service automation, gig work, surveillance and opaque, biased ranking may worsen job quality, privacy and discrimination [30]; GPTs are GPTs treats LLM exposure as potential impact, not realized displacement, wage or employment loss, and cannot distinguish augmentation from displacement [25].

## Specific challenges identified by researchers

### Reliability affects the economics of deployment

**Errors create additional human work.** The April 2023 marketing article identifies incorrect, incomplete, misleading, biased, harmful, and fabricated outputs. It recommends validation, accountability, risk assessment, continuous monitoring, and human oversight. For employment analysis, these are not merely ethical additions: they determine how much checking and correction remains necessary after generation. [4]

**Review costs can absorb apparent savings.** The Copilot studies show why this matters. A suggested solution may reduce the time spent producing an initial answer while increasing the effort needed to understand or debug it. The economically relevant comparison is between complete, acceptable work processes, including rework—not between the speed of generating a suggestion and the speed of writing it manually. [9][14]

**Accountability can preserve expert demand.** When an organization remains responsible for an output, a competent person may need to assess whether it is correct and appropriate. This can support complementary employment, but it also creates an implementation challenge: the reviewer needs sufficient knowledge and time to identify errors. Human involvement improves the process only when it is substantive, rather than a nominal approval step.

**Reliability has occupation-specific consequences.** A weak promotional draft, defective software suggestion, and inaccurate clinical response impose different potential costs. The marketing commentary discusses applications in all these areas, but its recommendations emphasize contextual validation. The practical judgment is to assess the entire consequence of error in the occupation, rather than assume that similar fluency implies similar suitability for deployment. [4]

### Skills require more than learning prompts

**Domain expertise remains necessary.** The marketing article warns against treating ChatGPT as a black box and notes that professional application requires expertise. It also identifies shortages of personnel able to understand data, algorithms, and AI in marketing teams. The relevant skills therefore include interpreting results, evaluating evidence, understanding customers or clients, and recognizing when an automated suggestion conflicts with professional requirements. [4]

**Implementation demands organizational learning.** Milanez’s cases report higher skill requirements, shortages of specialized AI skills, stress associated with learning new systems, and increases in work intensity. These findings challenge the idea that adoption consists simply of giving existing workers a new interface. Organizations must provide learning time and redesign responsibilities while maintaining ordinary production. [15]

**Specialist training and user training differ.** Green and Lamby define the AI workforce as people with the statistics, computer-science, and machine-learning skills needed to develop and maintain systems. Most workers affected by generative AI need a different combination: practical tool use, professional evaluation, and knowledge of their organization’s constraints. Treating every exposed worker as a prospective machine-learning developer would confuse these two labor-market needs. [16]

**Adult-learning participation is not sufficient evidence.** In Green and Lamby’s European analysis around 2019, 16% of the AI workforce reported training in the preceding four weeks, compared with 18% of employed tertiary-qualified workers. Among AI workers who undertook training in countries reporting its subject, approximately 20% of training concerned information and communication technologies. These measures describe participation and subject matter, not successful conversion into new AI skills. [16]

**Informal learning also matters.** The same report recognizes that people working in research or development teams may acquire skills through participation rather than formal courses. Consequently, a sensible reskilling approach must distinguish formal instruction, learning through work, and demonstrable ability to perform new tasks. Training provision is valuable, but its labor-market purpose is successful transition rather than course attendance alone. [16]

### Bias can reproduce unequal access to work

**Historical discrimination can become model behavior.** Green and Lamby illustrate the mechanism with an AI promotion system trained on a firm’s history of promoting men. Unless developers recognize and address that history, the system can reproduce it. This is an example of how data and design choices affect employment opportunities, rather than evidence that automated decision-making is inherently impartial. [16]

**Generative applications inherit related concerns.** The ChatGPT marketing analysis warns that bias can be perpetuated or amplified without appropriate design and testing. Applied to employment, the mechanism suggests particular care when generated text contributes to recruitment materials, worker assessments, or representations of occupational competence. That employment application is an analytical implication of the bias mechanism, not an estimated incidence rate for discriminatory generative-AI decisions. [4]

**Workforce diversity is part of governance.** Green and Lamby argue that a more diverse AI workforce is more likely to recognize problematic data and model choices. The connection to employment is twofold: access to desirable AI-related roles matters directly, and the composition of system-design teams may influence how other workers are evaluated. Diversity therefore concerns both the distribution of opportunities and the quality of the resulting technology. [16]

### Privacy concerns differ across workplace uses

**Customer data create one exposure channel.** The April 2023 marketing commentary discusses systems collecting and analyzing substantial personal information for personalization and customer understanding. It recommends protecting data, explaining collection and use, and giving consumers meaningful control. For workers, this means that useful applications may also impose responsibilities for handling information appropriately. [4]

**Worker data create another channel.** Deranty and Corbin describe algorithmic management systems that collect information about workers’ activities, communications, schedules, and performance. Privacy issues can arise during collection, analysis, use, and retention. The employment consequence is not necessarily displacement: it may instead be diminished autonomy, increased pressure, or decisions made through processes workers cannot meaningfully challenge. [6]

**Confidentiality affects practical adoption choices.** The report’s implication is that organizations should distinguish public drafting tasks from tasks involving employee, customer, or commercially sensitive information. A useful system does not remove the need to decide which information may enter it and who remains responsible for the result. This follows from the literature’s privacy and accountability concerns, rather than a claim that all workplace generative tools handle data identically. [4]

### Copyright and intellectual property affect creative value

**Copyright is a distinct governance question.** The 2022 political-economy review includes Levendowski’s 2018 legal scholarship, *How Copyright Law Can Fix Artificial Intelligence’s Implicit Bias Problem*, within its discussion of inherited bias and data access. This establishes an important connection between copyright, the material used to construct AI, and the distribution of its consequences. It does not itself quantify employment effects from generated code or creative output. [20]

**Creative employment depends partly on rights.** For generative applications, the employment implications of copyright turn on the rights governing training material and outputs, licensing arrangements, provenance, and contractual allocation of responsibility or remuneration. This is a conditional economic assessment: compensated reuse could create income for rights holders, while arrangements that substitute for their paid work could place pressure on existing earnings. The direction depends on those arrangements, not on content-generation capability alone.

**Attribution and licensing require separate decisions.** A practical governance implication is to distinguish identifying a source, having permission to use material, protecting confidential information, and establishing responsibility for a final output. These questions should not be collapsed into a single instruction to “check the AI.” The connection to job quality is whether workers receive clear rules and appropriate authority, or instead bear poorly defined responsibility for material they did not fully control.

### Monitoring can undermine job quality

**Efficiency tools can become control tools.** Deranty and Corbin’s review describes algorithmic management in recruitment, task allocation, scheduling, evaluation, incentives, and termination. Such systems can improve coordination, but also increase information asymmetry between management and workers. The central risk is that improved managerial visibility becomes stronger worker control without a corresponding ability to understand or contest decisions. [6]

**Task complementarity does not ensure autonomy.** The review’s discussion of warehouses shows humans continuing to perform activities surrounding automated processes, sometimes because they are better or cheaper at those activities. Human labor can therefore remain technically necessary while becoming more tightly subordinated to automated pacing and coordination. This is an important counterexample to equating retained employment with beneficial augmentation. [6]

**Less tedium can coexist with greater intensity.** Milanez’s cases report both improved task experiences and concerns about stronger monitoring, increased work intensity, and learning-related stress. An organization can remove repetitive tasks while increasing the amount of work expected in the remaining time. The job-quality outcome depends on how productivity gains are distributed between output targets, discretion, workload, and learning opportunities. [15]

**Supporting labor also deserves attention.** The marketing commentary draws attention to mental stress among content curators involved in training ChatGPT. The wider social-science review describes often-obscured human work supporting AI, including microtasks and verification. New employment around automated systems should therefore be evaluated for conditions and remuneration, rather than counted as an unqualified benefit. [4][6]

### Labor-market transitions create matching problems

**New jobs need not suit displaced workers.** A growing demand for AI developers does not automatically provide a transition for a displaced administrative, service, or creative worker. The skills, location, qualifications, and experience required may differ substantially. Frank and colleagues emphasize that career mobility depends on specific skill relationships and local opportunities, rather than on the existence of vacancies somewhere in the economy. [7]

**Positive net forecasts can imply disruption.** Deranty and Corbin summarize a 2018 World Economic Forum automation projection of 133 million new roles and 75 million displaced workers, yielding 58 million net additional jobs: 133−75=58 million. This historical forecast illustrates why a positive net number can coexist with a large transition burden. The people losing work and those gaining new roles need not be the same people. [6]

**Regional mobility can amplify inequality.** The Chinese service-industry study identifies differences in labor quality, industry structure, and mobility barriers across regions. It also describes manufacturing workers moving into services and intensifying competition in some skill groups. For generative AI, the relevant lesson is that occupational change can produce spillovers into work that is not directly automated. [19]

### Measurement choices shape the conclusions

**Different indicators answer different questions.** Task exposure identifies potential technical alignment; vacancies identify advertised demand; surveys capture experiences or perceptions; employment records describe realized labor-market outcomes. An experiment’s treatment-control difference has a different interpretation from an association between exposure and employment growth. Researchers must connect the indicator to the outcome being discussed. [13]

**Vacancy data have selective coverage.** The 2022 cross-country study discusses evidence that roughly 70% of U.S. vacancies were advertised online in 2013, while Burning Glass data overrepresented computer and mathematical occupations by more than four times. Such data can be valuable for emerging skills but can give a distorted picture if their occupational composition is treated as representative without adjustment. [13]

**Occupational descriptions can lag change.** Frank and colleagues note that O*NET began partial twice-yearly updates in 2014, yet changing skills may outpace a static taxonomy. Their example of “installation” illustrates the problem: it means very different work for plumbers and programmers. Broad labels can consequently hide the task distinctions needed to understand generative AI’s effects. [7]

**The challenges point to one judgment:** productive deployment requires reliable outputs, appropriate skills, clear responsibility, and worker protections, because technical assistance alone does not determine good employment outcomes.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Technical and implementation barriers**

The pre-cutoff evidence identifies the main challenge as translating capability into safe adoption and employment—not treating exposure as job loss. Eloundou et al.’s U.S. analysis, published 17 March 2023, estimated potential effects for about 80% of the workforce (at least 10% of tasks) and 19% of workers (at least 50%), but made no prediction about model development or adoption [33]. A qualitative study of 16 international systematic-review experts, conducted in March–April 2023 and published 19 May 2023, found LLM hallucination or omission, weak specificity and provenance, fabricated references or statistics, unsupported conclusions, and unclear accountability; experts judged direct medical-review production not ready. Generated code also requires checking by domain experts; the study showed at most three outputs per participant and was not an LLM evaluation [46].

A broader-AI clinical-documentation prototype reported privacy constraints, poor general-domain performance, expensive in-domain data, engineering burden, mediocre performance, EHR-integration requirements, and the need to configure tools for clinical workflows [43]. A 2020 human-in-the-loop customer-service study found that fluent chatbots could create an “uncanny valley” and customer dissatisfaction; unusual cases required human escalation and billing-system integration, while time savings were not conclusively demonstrated [48]. In UK legal services, broader-AI interview evidence identifies resistance to change, partnership bottlenecks, skill gaps, mistrust, data-security, GDPR and cross-jurisdictional constraints, unstructured data, and negligence risk; this evidence is contextual rather than GenAI-specific [45].

**Worker, institutional and distributional challenges**

GPT exposure is uneven: programming, writing, and higher-wage occupations are more exposed, whereas physical and manual work was not exposed in the study’s assessment; exposure remains a proxy that does not distinguish augmentation from displacement [50]. The underlying U.S. data for 2020–2021 mainly cover documented, non-self-employed formal-economy workers; 3,504,000 workers lacking education or training data were excluded from those tables [50]. Frank et al.’s broader-AI account highlights sparse skills and work data, weak micro-process and labor-resilience models, and inadequate geographic-interdependence modeling, which can obscure rural–urban labor-force and income-distribution differences [39]. Eynon and Young’s 34 interviews found competing AI frames and disproportionate commercial influence in lifelong learning, requiring wider stakeholder participation and explicit educational aims [47]. A pre-cutoff ethical framework for AI and robots treats workplace introduction as social experimentation because effects are uncertain and unintended; it flags deskilling or displacement, autonomy loss, surveillance, privacy, and work-intensification risks, while noting possible new technical jobs. This is contextual, not a GenAI causal estimate [42].

**Evidence gaps**

For GenAI, the key gap is the bridge from exposure to adoption and employment: task decomposition may omit tacit work, labels are occupationally narrow and rubric-sensitive, and projections omit social, legal, and regulatory conditions; no adoption timetable or realized employment effect follows [50]. The supplied Deranty–Corbin record identifies a broad critical review but provides no substantive challenge finding and records conflicting working-paper/preprint versus April 2024 peer-reviewed metadata; no specific eligible GenAI inference is therefore made [44].

Generative AI/GPTs face hallucination, untraceable evidence, subjective labels, flawed task breakdowns and accountability risks; reconfiguration and human-oversight barriers slow adoption. Exposure cannot separate augmentation from displacement or establish job loss; it is higher in programming/writing and harder-entry roles, lower in science/critical-thinking roles [46][35][50].

The Generative AI report separates positive views, negative views, challenges and opportunities. Customer-service/contact-center chatbots may create uncanny-valley dissatisfaction; unusual cases need human escalation, while weak measures and self-reported, nonrepresentative evidence limit employment claims [48][49].

## Future opportunities for employment and the labor market

### New tasks can emerge around generated outputs

**Review and contextualization can expand.** The marketing literature identifies work in checking outputs, interpreting data, maintaining brand relevance, overseeing customer interactions, and evaluating ethical consequences. These responsibilities can grow when generation becomes cheaper and more widely used. Their value comes from ensuring that an output serves a real organizational purpose, not simply from adding a human presence to an automated process. [4]

**System development creates another task family.** Milanez’s workplace cases identify building, training, updating, and maintaining AI systems as sources of labor demand. These activities are distinguishable from using an application in an existing occupation. A future labor market could therefore contain both more AI-specialist work and widespread changes within ordinary professional roles. [15]

**Existing occupations may absorb new responsibilities.** Editing generated text may become part of marketing work, evaluating generated code part of programming, and overseeing automated advice part of customer support. Occupational change need not produce a new job title to matter economically. It can alter the required skill mix, relative wages, or number of workers needed within an established title. [4][9]

**New work needs a quality test.** The literature on hidden AI-support labor provides a counterweight to celebrating every new task. Verification, annotation, and moderation can be valuable, yet their employment contribution depends on pay, security, autonomy, and psychological demands. The opportunity is to create worthwhile complementary work, rather than merely relocate human effort into less visible parts of a production system. [6]

### Reskilling can support human–AI complementarity

**Chinese research anticipates wider skill relevance.** Zhu’s preliminary preprint, first submitted on 14 April 2023, analyzes job postings from BOSS Zhipin. It reports that approximately 28% of current occupations require ChatGPT-related skills and predicts that an additional 45% of future occupations will require them. The analysis combines job-posting information with an occupation-centered knowledge graph and a predictive method for occupation–skill relationships. [21]

**The two percentages describe different claims.** The 28% figure concerns the study’s assessment of current occupation–skill relationships; the additional 45% is a prediction concerning future relationships. They should remain separate rather than becoming a claim that 73% of workers will adopt ChatGPT, obtain new jobs, or face replacement. Their practical meaning is that generative-tool competence may become relevant within a broad set of existing occupations. [21]

**Predicted proficiency varies by industry.** Zhu identifies higher anticipated requirements in technology, products, and operations, with lower requirements in manufacturing, services, education, and health science. This suggests differentiated training rather than a single universal curriculum. Workers in technical integration roles may need deeper competence than workers using generated drafts as a small part of their jobs. [21]

**Exposure and skill demand can diverge.** Felten and colleagues identify U.S. post-secondary teaching among highly language-model-exposed occupations, while Zhu predicts comparatively lower ChatGPT-skill proficiency requirements for education in China. These findings concern different countries and different constructs. A system’s ability to affect tasks does not necessarily translate into employers advertising high proficiency in that system; comparing the measures avoids turning a difference in indicators into an apparent contradiction. [8][21]

**Training can improve the adoption pathway.** Lane, Williams, and Broecke associate training and worker consultation with better outcomes in their seven-country surveys. This supports an opportunity to combine technical instruction with workers’ knowledge of actual tasks, exceptions, and customer needs. The policy implication is to make workers participants in redesign, rather than treating adaptation as an individual obligation after decisions have already been made. [5]

### Productivity-enhancing services can broaden markets

**Marketing offers several complementary uses.** The April 2023 commentary describes drafting product descriptions, preparing promotional content, analyzing consumer language, personalizing communications, and helping call-center workers respond. These applications can reduce preparation time and support more individualized services. The employment opportunity is strongest when firms use the saved effort to improve relationships or expand service, instead of assuming every efficiency gain should reduce staffing. [4]

**Education offers task-level assistance.** The same article discusses individualized study plans, feedback, grading support, and virtual responses to student questions. These are conceptual applications, not measured teacher-employment effects. Their complementary pathway is to reduce preparation or routine response burdens while preserving educators’ roles in interpreting student needs, assessing learning, and deciding what instructional support is appropriate. [4]

**Healthcare raises the value of judgment.** The commentary also discusses symptom triage and initial consultations. Adjacent medical-AI examples in Deranty and Corbin’s review include VizAI, PathAI, Buoy Health, and Enlitic, described as complementing specialists with possible future substitution. The practical opportunity is assistance with defined information-processing activities; the employment effect depends on how professional judgment and patient interaction remain organized around them. [4][6]

**Legal work illustrates saved time clearly.** Deranty and Corbin report Wall’s 2018 JPMorgan Chase COIN example: interpreting commercial loan agreements was estimated to save 360,000 work hours annually. COIN is an adjacent AI application, not a generative-AI case. Its value for this report is demonstrating the distinction between removing labor from document-processing tasks and determining employment throughout a legal or financial organization. [6]

**Service expansion is a plausible opportunity.** If lower production costs make legal, educational, technical, or marketing support accessible to additional users, complementary labor demand can increase. Earlier AI literature explains this through lower prices and higher demand; the Chinese panel also discusses reduced transaction costs and expanded demand for services. The opportunity should therefore be assessed at the level of the service market, not only the automated activity. [12][19]

### Broader access could change who participates

**Programming assistance may lower entry barriers.** Peng and colleagues describe heterogeneous productivity effects as promising for transitions into software-development careers. This suggests an opportunity for people who can use assistance to perform tasks previously requiring more experience. A viable transition still requires the ability to understand, evaluate, and maintain the resulting work, as the smaller usability studies demonstrate. [1][9]

**Assistance can widen practical capability.** Agossaha and colleagues’ reported uses—development support, retrieval, and communication—show how tools can help workers beyond a narrowly defined production task. The opportunity is partly occupational mobility: existing workers may perform a broader range of activities with support. That opportunity differs from predicting an entirely new occupation or counting every user as an AI specialist. [11]

**Accessibility belongs within employment policy.** Deranty and Corbin’s review discusses earlier research identifying improved accessibility to some jobs among AI’s possible benefits. The useful employment principle is to reduce barriers that prevent people from applying their skills, while preserving control over the tools they use. Assistive capability becomes a labor-market benefit when it supports actual participation, appropriate workplace adjustment, and fair evaluation—not merely a demonstration of technical functionality. [6]

**Broader participation can also intensify competition.** Lower barriers can help entrants while reducing the scarcity value of some incumbents’ skills. This is an analytical implication of combining productivity assistance with the task-displacement and wage literature. It means that access and income protection are not automatically aligned: a tool can democratize participation while placing pressure on payment for a particular service. [1][3]

### Institutions can influence who receives the gains

**Worker consultation is a practical opportunity.** The OECD surveys associate consultation with better worker outcomes and identify room to strengthen trust. Consultation can help organizations distinguish genuinely burdensome tasks from activities workers value, and can reveal exceptions that a technology-centered implementation overlooks. Its employment significance lies in improving both task design and the legitimacy of organizational change. [5]

**Training needs organizational support.** A policy implication of the skills and workplace evidence is to provide time and resources for learning, rather than merely raising skill requirements. Training should address the tasks workers will actually perform, including reviewing outputs and handling failures. This recommendation follows from the combination of learning-related stress in workplace cases and the positive training associations in the surveys. [15][5]

**Education and regional policy should connect.** The Chinese service-industry study recommends improving training systems, university–industry cooperation, continuing education, and cross-regional movement of AI-related talent. Its reasoning is that labor demand, skills supply, and regional industry structure must adjust together. The wider literature similarly makes inclusive growth conditional on educational measures, especially for countries participating in global production networks. [19][6]

**Income support addresses a different problem.** The 2022 political-economy review reports proposals for basic income and training programs in response to technological job insecurity. These are proposed responses, rather than demonstrated generative-AI policy effects. Their functions differ: income support can protect living standards during disruption, while training seeks to improve access to subsequent work. One should not be treated as automatically replacing the other. [20]

**Governance can preserve valuable human responsibility.** The marketing literature recommends human oversight, privacy protection, bias testing, transparency, and accountability. In employment terms, these practices can support demand for competent professionals and reduce the risk that workers become responsible for outputs without sufficient control. This is a design opportunity: organize complementary work so that responsibility, authority, and adequate time remain aligned. [4]

### Three conditional labor-market scenarios

**The evidence supports scenarios, not inevitability.** The following outlook uses source-specific numerical anchors to identify contrasting adjustment paths. The numbers retain their original meaning—task coverage or worker exposure—and do not become estimates of jobs created or destroyed. The aggregate employment direction turns on adoption and its timing, demand expansion, human labor saved after review, new tasks, working hours, and the institutions governing transition. [2][12]

| Scenario | Numerical anchor | Likely adjustment | Conditions driving it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reorganization-led | 15% of U.S. tasks potentially faster with an LLM | Assistance within existing jobs | Selective adoption and effective review |
| Complementary expansion | 47–56% potential task coverage with LLM-based tools | Expanded services and surrounding human work | Integration, demand growth, and training |
| Uneven substitution | 19% of U.S. workers with at least half their tasks exposed | Hiring restraint and occupational pressure | Extensive substitution and weak adjustment support |

The numerical anchors come from the March 2023 U.S. exposure analysis; the scenarios are this report’s conditional synthesis. [2]

**Reorganization is the most grounded starting point.** Direct studies show useful assistance in specified tasks, while broader workplace cases frequently describe changes within jobs. This pathway fits situations where systems handle parts of a workflow but human responsibilities remain important. Its advantage is that productive capacity can increase without requiring an immediate replacement of whole occupations. [1][15]

**Expansion requires more than technical reach.** Wider task coverage becomes a favorable employment pathway when firms can serve additional demand and workers can move into useful complementary responsibilities. Specialist development work, professional review, and better services are possible sources of expansion. The positive conclusion strengthens when organizations convert saved effort into valuable output and improved work, rather than simply increasing work intensity.

**Substitution risks remain concentrated and real.** Extensive task exposure creates scope for reducing paid labor, particularly where output demand expands little and residual review requires fewer hours. The historical literature shows why wage pressure and reduced entry opportunities matter alongside redundancies. Training, consultation, and transition support influence whether affected workers can reach the complementary opportunities.

**The opportunity judgment is therefore institutional:** generative AI offers a substantial chance to improve productive work, but inclusive employment gains depend on how organizations redesign tasks and how labor-market institutions distribute adjustment costs.

**Additional documented evidence**

The best-supported opportunity is task augmentation, not proven net job creation [41]. Eloundou et al.’s U.S. LLM assessment, published 17 March 2023, estimates that at least 10% of tasks could be affected for about 80% of the workforce and at least 50% for 19% of workers; these are potential exposure estimates, not observed employment gains or losses [41]. A study of 12 journalists found an LLM brainstorming tool more helpful and less mentally demanding than its comparator and proposed extensions to case law and academia—an observed task benefit, not a jobs estimate [62].

Contextual, broad-AI literature identifies possible pathways. Acemoglu and Restrepo describe new tasks for trainers, explainers and sustainers, and possible employment in individualized education, health and design [55]. An OECD framework links AI development, maintenance, auditing and regulation to new work; its AI workforce was under 0.3% of OECD employment in 2019, while complementary work relies on creative and social intelligence, reasoning and critical thinking [52]. AI-skill demand and wage premiums were also associated in 2010–19 online postings, especially in professional services, although the relationship was cross-sectional and not GenAI-specific [61]. UK legal-services research anticipates faster research, enhanced services and redeployed expertise, conditional on business-model change, skills and organizational capacity [45].

Realization requires training infrastructure, retention and retraining incentives, social protection, worker consultation and co-design [52]. Eynon and Young’s 34-interview lifelong-learning study calls for stakeholder interaction and explicit educational goals to support democratic access [47]. A pre-cutoff healthcare review requires laboratory evaluation, specialized models, human fact-checking, prompt competence and patient-data protection before deployment [64]. A contextual summary of Frank et al. proposes granular skills data, skill interdependencies, worker mobility and spatial models to identify beneficiaries [39]. AI-review automation proposes planning, writing and screening support, including reported reductions of up to 60% in screening burden and savings exceeding 80 hours, but limited tool integration constrains adoption [60]. A customer-support summary reports 5,179 agents, 14% average and roughly 34% novice/lower-skilled productivity gains plus lower attrition, but cannot verify pre-1 June 2023 eligibility; it is not counted as eligible evidence [53]. Deranty–Corbin’s record supplies no substantive opportunity finding and lists its peer-reviewed review as 2024 [44]. Thus, augmentation and new tasks remain conditional on adoption, skills, governance and distribution.

Pre-cutoff broad-AI research makes opportunity conditional: automation may raise productivity and labour demand, create tasks and roles (trainers, explainers, sustainers), but transitions require new skills [27]. For employment, AI literacy, domain knowledge, critical judgment, reskilling, social protection and accountable governance can support inclusive gains [56][57][58]. These are recommendations, not observed GenAI job creation.

Generative AI may create trainers and explainers; the AI workforce was under 0.3% of employment in 2019. These are prospects, not observed GenAI effects; skills, training, adoption, feedback and governance condition realization [55][52][51].

Source recommendations—not planner inference—for Generative AI include AI literacy, reskilling, participation, social protection and pathways; separate positive, negative, challenge and opportunity sections [56][58][59][63].

Lane and Saint-Martin link contextual-AI task reorganisation and human–AI complementarity to skills, not direct GenAI effects; Deranty and Corbin propose basic income, social protection and ethical regulation, also contextually [54][27][31].

Customer service evidence is contextual: Alibaba’s chatbot handled more than 95% of inquiries in a 2017 sale, freeing representatives for personal issues; it is not a GenAI finding [54].

## Summary of application cases, impacts, and risks

**The overall conclusion favors conditional augmentation.** Before June 2023, the most concrete generative-AI findings concern task performance, usability, exposure, and early workplace use. The broader employment literature explains why these benefits can coexist with reduced hiring, wage pressure, uneven transitions, and deteriorating autonomy. A sound interpretation therefore evaluates productive output, employment opportunities, and job quality together.

**Evidence is read on consistent bases.** Dates refer to pre-1-June-2023 circulation or online publication; later revisions and year-only 2023 records do not establish cutoff-specific findings. Deranty and Corbin’s review is treated by its 2022 online publication rather than its later journal issue, and Milanez refers to the 24 April edition rather than the December replacement. Direct generative studies are separated from broader AI evidence; exposure percentages describe tasks or workers, time savings describe activities, and projections retain their stated assumptions. Country-unspecified entries preserve the studies’ participant or conceptual scope without inferring geography from affiliations. The 32–41-percentage-point task-coverage difference is this report’s calculation; other numerical estimates retain their source definitions.

## Appendix: Further Evidence

**Additional documented evidence**

The four perspectives in this report should consequently be read as different evidence claims rather than as four mutually exclusive predictions. A positive view concerns augmentation, productivity, complementary skills, or new tasks; a negative view concerns substitution, displacement, wage pressure, or reduced job quality; a challenge concerns the conditions or uncertainties that obstruct measurement or implementation; and an opportunity concerns a future possibility rather than a realized effect. The same LLM capability can support both augmentation and substitution, depending on whether firms use it to assist workers, reorganize tasks, or eliminate particular activities [65][22].

The apparent disagreement between positive and negative positions often reflects differences in the outcome unit. A capability estimate at the task level cannot be treated as an occupation-level employment forecast, and an occupation-level exposure estimate cannot be treated as observed job loss. Nor can a productivity increase in a particular task be assumed to raise wages or total employment: the result depends on adoption, demand, worker adaptation, market power, and institutional arrangements. These distinctions are especially important because the direct pre-cutoff LLM evidence is primarily theoretical or exposure-based, whereas much of the older labor literature concerns broader AI and automation.

**Additional documented evidence**

Frank et al.’s **“Toward understanding the impact of artificial intelligence on labor”** was published as a scholarly PNAS journal article in 2019, well before the cutoff. It is relevant contextual evidence rather than direct GenAI evidence. The article describes AI and automation as technologies that can augment the productivity of some workers, replace work performed by others, and transform occupations to some degree; it therefore supplies the central augmentation–substitution mechanism needed to interpret later LLM exposure estimates [67].

A nonacademic contextual source makes the same distinction between aggregate and worker-level outcomes. It reports that U.S. productivity averaged roughly 1.5% annually over the completed 2007–2019 business cycle and discusses optimistic projections of approximately 1 percentage point of additional annual productivity growth from AI; these are historical and projected broad-AI figures, not eligible pre-cutoff GenAI employment estimates [66]. The source argues that productivity gains can reduce labor requirements within a sector while increasing demand in other sectors through lower prices and higher income, yet it also recognizes sector-specific displacement and harm to individual workers [66]. It further emphasizes that monitoring technologies and unequal bargaining power can convert technological capability into lower wages or degraded job quality rather than shared productivity gains [66]. These claims are retained only as labeled contextual interpretation, not as core academic evidence about GenAI.

**Additional documented evidence**

The evidence therefore converges on four qualified propositions. First, LLM capability can affect economically important tasks, particularly where text or other content generation is technically feasible [65][22]. Second, augmentation and substitution are competing mechanisms, not sequentially guaranteed outcomes: exposure alone cannot determine whether employment, wages, or job quality will improve or deteriorate [65][22][67]. Third, adoption, worker adaptation, organizational redesign, demand effects, bargaining power, and institutional policy determine how task-level capability becomes a labor-market outcome [66][67]. Fourth, the evidence base before June 2023 contains substantial measurement and causal uncertainty, so positive opportunities and negative risks should be reported as conditional or potential unless a study observes the relevant employment outcome [65][22][67].

Fossen and Sorgner’s *New Digital Technologies and Heterogeneous Employment and Wage Dynamics in the United States: Evidence from Individual-Level Data* is an SSRN Electronic Journal preprint published on 1 January 2019; it therefore meets the date cutoff but remains broad-AI/digitalization context, not direct GenAI evidence [111]. Representative U.S. CPS/ASEC data cover 2011–18, including monthly transitions from January 2011 to October 2018 and linked annual wage observations, across workers, occupations, industries, regions, education, age, gender, and sectors. Computerization risk, AI advances, and machine-learning suitability are exposure measures; outcomes are nonemployment, occupational switching, entrepreneurship, and wage growth. RECIPE was posted 19 May 2023 and deployed ChatGPT with 213 university EFL students in South Korea during spring 2023; students reported positive experiences and questioned suggestions, but hallucinated sources remained a limitation [95]. These are observed work/learning findings, not employment or wage effects. A cutoff-unverified healthcare perspective [89] presents LLM documentation and communication assistance as clinician augmentation, but warns of misinformation, privacy, bias and weak oversight; treat it as contextual, not causal labor-market evidence. Human supervision and context determine whether productivity becomes opportunity; neither benefit nor risk alone establishes net job creation or realized job loss.

GenAI task evidence: May-2023 education tutoring/feedback, but hallucination/bias [96]; Mar-2023 healthcare: 71.8% accuracy/36 hypothetical vignettes, no clinical deployment [64]. Broader AI: conditional complementarity/substitution [52][190].

Context dominates; most evidence is not direct GenAI: Ojanperä (2018) reports 47% projected U.S. employment risk (702 occupations), versus 9% task risk and 14% highly automatable OECD jobs; methods/geography matter [58].
