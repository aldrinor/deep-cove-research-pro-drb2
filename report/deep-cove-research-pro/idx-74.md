# China’s Open Research Data Policies and Practices Before July 2020

**The central finding:** By 30 June 2020, China had established a layered research-data system combining national administrative rules, institutional policies—especially those of the Chinese Academy of Sciences—and discipline-specific sharing arrangements. Its principal national instrument, the 2018 *Measures for the Administration of Scientific Data*, required data from government-budget-funded science and technology projects to be deposited before project acceptance and established openness as the default, subject to security, privacy, intellectual-property and other restrictions. [2020]

**Implementation combined obligations and infrastructure:** The Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST), Ministry of Finance (MOF) and Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) connected these rules to project-management procedures, national data centers, repositories and data journals. The National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC) operated a documented article-manuscript open-access policy and supported data-sharing infrastructure; its project requirements also included targeted scientific-data-transfer agreements. These different arrangements should not be collapsed into one identical funder-wide dataset workflow. [2021][2022][2023]

**The report follows four questions:** What did each policy level require? How did project rules, repositories and journals implement those requirements? Which institutions supplied authority, funding and services? The final section consolidates the principal instruments into the requested three-column policy inventory.

| Researcher situation | Governing route | Deposit or service route | Access and use conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Government-funded research project | 2018 national Measures | Relevant scientific-data center | Deposit precedes acceptance |
| CAS research project | National rules plus CAS trial measures | Institutional and disciplinary services | Submission enters project assessment |
| NSFC-funded publication | 2014 NSFC publication policy | NSFC open-access repository | Manuscript opens within 12 months |
| Public-interest data use | National sharing provisions | Data-holding institution or center | Normally free; justified nonprofit charges |
| Commercial data use | National and service-specific terms | Contract with provider | Paid service and defined rights |
| Human-genomic or sensitive health data | Genetic-resource, ethics and privacy rules | Authorized institutional or biobank route | Consent, review and controlled access |
| Data-paper publication | Journal submission requirements | SCiDB or global-change repository | Paper and dataset published together |

**Research data means more than papers:** This report concerns the People’s Republic of China and developments through **30 June 2020**. Research data includes the records generated during research and the supporting material underlying published results. The national definition encompasses raw and derived data from basic research, applied research, experimental development, observation, monitoring, surveys and testing, principally in natural science and engineering. Open access to an article and access to its underlying dataset are therefore related but separate matters. [2020][2024]

## Policy System Review

### How the three policy levels interacted

**National rules supplied the baseline:** China’s system did not consist of three independent policy inventories. National instruments assigned responsibilities and established funding-related obligations; institutional instruments incorporated those obligations into research organizations; disciplinary arrangements specified the standards, permissions and protections appropriate to particular data. A CAS genomics project, for example, could simultaneously fall under the national deposit requirement, CAS project-management rules and human-genetic-resource controls. [2020][2021][2025]

| Policy level | Principal responsibility | Main implementation mechanism | Access implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| National | Coordinate publicly funded scientific data | Deposit, catalogs, centers and evaluation | Open by default, with exceptions |
| Institutional | Manage data within research organizations | Project assessment, preservation and paper-data review | Institutional classification and services |
| Disciplinary | Address domain-specific requirements | Technical standards, user rules and ethical review | Different permissions for different data |

**Deposit and public release were distinct:** A requirement to submit data to a center did not automatically mean that every submitted file became freely downloadable. Deposit secured institutional custody and preservation; classification and access review determined subsequent sharing. This distinction explains how mandatory submission could coexist with controlled access, commercial contracts or restrictions on sensitive information. [2020]

### National policies: from infrastructure construction to lifecycle governance

**The decisive national instrument:** The State Council General Office issued the *Measures for the Administration of Scientific Data*—《科学数据管理办法》—on **17 March 2018**, effective upon issuance and published on MOST’s website on 4 April. Its official document number was **Guobanfa [2018] No. 17**. Its purpose was to standardize scientific-data management, protect data security, improve open sharing and support innovation, economic and social development, and national security. [2020]

**Responsibility moved beyond individual researchers:** Articles 7–10 assigned MOST national coordination, policy and standards development, center construction and evaluation. Supervising departments and provincial authorities were responsible for their respective sectors and jurisdictions. Research institutes, universities and enterprises, as legal entities, became responsible for data quality, long-term preservation, security, updated open-data catalogs and sharing services. [2020]

This allocation mattered because research data often outlive a particular grant or researcher’s appointment. By placing responsibility on the institution, the Measures linked preservation and access to organizations capable of maintaining staff, systems and storage over time. The institution was not simply a place where researchers happened to keep their files; it was the accountable data manager.

**Acceptance became a compliance mechanism:** Article 13 required the lead unit of a government-budget-funded science and technology project—including projects under plans, special programs and funds—to deposit its scientific data with the relevant scientific-data center. The receiving center had to issue a deposit receipt, and project managers were instructed to establish a “deposit first, acceptance later” mechanism. Data generated after project acceptance also remained subject to submission. [2020]

This was stronger than a general appeal to share voluntarily. It attached data submission to an established administrative decision: whether a funded project could formally close. Its effectiveness nevertheless depended on the receiving center, submission standards and project-management arrangements being operational.

**Openness was qualified, not unconditional:** Article 19 established “open as the normal state, non-opening as the exception” for government-budget-funded scientific data. Article 20 required institutions to specify classification, secrecy periods, opening conditions, eligible users and review procedures, with access delivered through online downloads, offline sharing or customized services. [2020]

Article 24 required free provision for government decision-making, public security, national defense, environmental protection, disaster prevention and relief, and public-interest scientific research. Where charging was genuinely necessary, prices had to follow prescribed procedures and a nonprofit principle. Commercial use required a paid-service contract defining the parties’ rights and obligations. [2020]

**Protection remained part of openness:** Article 25 restricted external sharing of data involving state secrets, national security, societal or public interests, trade secrets or personal privacy. Necessary exceptions required scrutiny of the intended use, user qualifications and confidentiality arrangements, with tightly controlled access. Articles 26–29 added secrecy procedures, authentication, authorization, cybersecurity, emergency management and backup requirements. [2020]

These provisions produced a managed-access system rather than a universal public-domain regime. Intellectual-property protection and data citation also formed part of the framework: Articles 22–23 encouraged data publication and required users to acknowledge scientific data used in papers, patents and books. [2020]

**The foreign-journal provision was conditional:** Article 14 required an author to submit government-funded scientific data to the author’s institution before publication abroad when the foreign journal required the corresponding data to be supplied. It was an institutional archiving requirement tied to that publication situation, not a blanket prohibition on international scientific-data exchange. Ropes & Gray’s August 2018 analysis also recorded MOST’s clarification that the provision was not intended to stop outbound data flows. [2020][2026]

The distinction is important. Institutional submission, permission to publish, permission to transfer sensitive data and permission to redistribute a downloaded dataset are different decisions. Domain-specific rules could impose additional controls, particularly for human-genomic information.

**Private funding received different treatment:** Article 15 required submission of privately or socially funded data involving state secrets, national security or societal and public interests, while encouraging submission of other privately funded scientific data. The national rule thus combined a public-funding trigger with particular requirements reaching beyond publicly financed projects. [2020]

**Noncompliance had administrative consequences:** Article 31 covered data fabrication, intellectual-property infringement and failure to submit data as required. Depending on the circumstances, supervising departments could order correction, issue public criticism, impose disciplinary measures or apply administrative penalties under the relevant law. These were specified consequences, although they should not be mistaken for evidence that every project had already undergone equivalent enforcement. [2020]

**The earlier trajectory explains the transition:** The 2018 framework built on programs that had already created repositories, departmental rules and service networks. The principal changes were from infrastructure pilots to continuing services, and then from selected large scientific collections to broader project-level data stewardship.

| Period | Principal development | Policy emphasis | Transition |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1980s–1990s | CAS databases and international data cooperation | Preserve and exchange disciplinary data | Institution-led foundations |
| 2002–2005 | MOST Scientific Data Sharing Program pilots | Demonstrate sharing mechanisms | National coordination |
| 2006–2010 | Infrastructure construction and integration | Build centers, standards and catalogs | Consolidate resources |
| 2010–2017 | Operational platforms and assessments | Evaluate services and collect project resources | Shift toward continuing provision |
| 2018–June 2020 | National Measures, CAS rules and national centers | Manage data across the research lifecycle | Broaden project-level obligations |

The MOST Scientific Data Sharing Program began in **2002**. A 2014 account described a 2002–2005 pilot, a 2006–2010 infrastructure and integration phase, and a service-and-evaluation phase beginning in 2010. It reported more than 18 sharing projects across six broad fields and 24 departments. Its proposed 40-center or network structure was a development target, not a completed-center count. [2027]

**The 2004 Outline targeted fragmentation:** The *2004–2010 National Science and Technology Basic Conditions Platform Construction Outline*, prepared through the national ministerial platform initiative and forwarded by the State Council General Office, called for organizing departmental and national-project data, rescuing important data at risk of loss, digitizing historical resources and building a combination of centralized and distributed scientific-data centers. It explicitly envisaged graded and classified sharing rather than a single access arrangement for all resources. [2028]

Its practical importance was organizational: it treated accumulated scientific data as shared infrastructure rather than isolated project assets. By the 2011–2012 reporting period, the platform program had supported 10 scientific-data-center groups and three sharing networks, with six scientific-data platforms recognized within the national infrastructure system. [2029]

### Platform governance, evaluation and the 2019 center system

**Platform rules supplied the operating framework:** MOST and MOF jointly issued the *National Science and Technology Resource Sharing Service Platform Management Measures* on **13 February 2018**, as Guokefaji [2018] No. 48. These Measures covered public-service platforms centered on scientific data, biological germplasm and experimental materials. Publicly financed resources had to be shared with society except where confidentiality requirements or special provisions applied. [2030][2031]

The Measures required resources generated by publicly financed science and technology projects to enter designated platforms. Host institutions were responsible for integrating, updating, preserving and quality-controlling resources, maintaining online services, recording service activity and supplying stable staffing and facilities. This translated the general idea of openness into institutional operating duties. [2030]

**Evaluation linked sharing to financial support:** The 2018 platform Measures required annual self-evaluation and a classified national assessment every **two years**, combining user evaluation, online portal testing and expert review. Results informed subsequent subsidies and dynamic adjustment; poorly performing platforms could be required to rectify problems and ultimately leave the national platform system. [2030]

An earlier MOST–MOF assessment notice, issued on **25 January 2017** and published on 23 February as Guokefaji [2017] No. 24, demonstrates that evaluation already had practical consequences. It assessed 23 existing platforms and evaluated 15 platform-construction projects. Twenty existing platforms received positive operational judgments, six construction projects were admitted as basically meeting platform requirements, and three existing platforms received yellow-card warnings. [2032]

The positive judgment covered **87.0% of the existing platforms assessed**, calculated as 20 ÷ 23 × 100. That percentage describes an administrative assessment outcome—not the proportion of Chinese research data open to the public. The notice’s final list contained 28 platforms after admissions and consolidation, so “28 listed platforms” should not be read as 28 uniformly successful assessments.

**The 2019 reorganization created designated centers:** MOST and MOF issued the *Notice on Publishing the Optimized and Adjusted List of National Science and Technology Resource Sharing Service Platforms* on **5 June 2019**, published on 10 June as **Guokefaji [2019] No. 194**. It designated **20 National Science Data Centers** and **30 national biological germplasm and experimental-material resource banks**. [2033]

The notice required five-year construction and operation plans, clearer institutional functions, resource aggregation, improved storage and security, stronger analysis and application services, evaluation and international cooperation. It was therefore both a designation decision and a program of further institutional development. The 20 data centers and 30 resource banks were separate categories; the earlier 28-platform list represented a different organizational stage and resource mix.

**Field observations gained a specific pathway:** MOST’s General Office issued the *National Field Scientific Observation and Research Station Construction and Development Plan, 2019–2025* on **20 June 2019**, as Guokebanji [2019] No. 55. It required observation standards, quality control, long-term data accumulation, data-aggregation systems supported by relevant national scientific-data centers, and explicit plans for orderly opening and sharing. [2034]

The plan reported an existing system of **106 national field stations**, developed since 1999 across ecosystems, special environments and atmospheric background, geophysics, and material corrosion. This made it a particularly relevant national instrument for Earth-system and environmental data: it connected continuing observation to standards, preservation infrastructure and sharing services rather than treating data release as an isolated website function.

**National policy takeaway:** By the cutoff, China had moved beyond general encouragement toward an administrative system linking public funding, institutional responsibility, deposit, service provision and evaluation; the access granted to users remained conditional on the data and intended use.

### Institutional policies: CAS and biobank governance

**CAS embedded data in research management:** CAS issued its *Measures for Scientific Data Management and Open Sharing (Trial)*—《中国科学院科学数据管理与开放共享办法（试行）》—on **11 February 2019**. The instrument contained **eight chapters and 32 articles**, covering principles, responsibilities, management requirements, safeguards, confidentiality and security. [2021]

Its most consequential provision brought scientific data from research projects supported by national fiscal funds into the project-management process. Submission became a necessary indicator for project establishment and assessment. CAS also addressed paper-associated data by defining responsibilities for researchers, CAS journals and affiliated legal entities and establishing submission-and-review arrangements. [2021]

**The institutional contribution was organizational:** CAS could connect its requirements to institutes, major scientific facilities, field stations, journals and information infrastructure within one organization. Its trial Measures specified responsibilities for these different actors and planned a center system comprising a general center, disciplinary centers and institute-level centers. The three center types were an institutional design, not a claim that the entire planned network had already been deployed. [2021]

Compared with the national framework, this policy brought the obligation closer to the people and units conducting research. Its strength lay in connecting project evaluation, paper-associated data and institutional services. Its stated trial status also indicated that institutional implementation was still developing.

**CAS publication openness had a separate lineage:** On **15 May 2014**, CAS required researchers and graduate students to deposit final peer-reviewed manuscripts arising from public funding in their institute repositories and make them openly accessible within **12 months** of official publication. That institutional article policy preceded the broader 2019 scientific-data instrument. [2022]

The associated repository infrastructure was substantial. A 2016 account described a CAS institutional-repository grid drawing content from 102 repositories, alongside an open-access journal portal. These were publication-access services; their scale should not be interpreted as a count of research-data repositories. [2035]

**Biobanks supplied additional institutional examples:** The China National Genebank’s **2017 Security Rules**, applying to the Genebank itself, combined privacy protection and institutional review board oversight with identity verification, tiered access, data-use logging and confidentiality agreements for overseas users. An institutional review board, or IRB, assesses whether proposed research meets ethical requirements. [2025]

The **China Kadoorie Biobank’s 2014 Data Access and Sample Preservation Policy** provided a second model. The Biobank, jointly operated by the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Oxford University, required international applicants to have secure-storage and responsible-use procedures approved by an independent Access Committee. Substantial changes in intended use required further approval. [2025]

These policies illustrate a different meaning of successful sharing from unrestricted public download. Their purpose was to enable scientifically useful access while maintaining participant protections, approved research purposes and continuing oversight.

**Institutional policy takeaway:** CAS demonstrated how a national obligation could enter project and publication management, while biobank policies showed how institutions could facilitate sharing through accountable, controlled-access arrangements.

### Disciplinary policies: meteorology, Earth systems, marine data and genomics

**Meteorology offered an early operational rule:** The China Meteorological Administration (CMA) issued the *Measures for Sharing Meteorological Data*, **Order No. 4**, on **27 November 2001**. The rule covered archived meteorological observations, processed datasets, climate statistics and numerical-analysis material. Its purpose was to strengthen sharing for economic development, national defense, social development and public welfare. [2036]

The rule required specified basic observations to be published online for free public download: four scheduled surface-station reports and two scheduled upper-air reports exchanged through the World Meteorological Organization’s Global Telecommunication System, together with current-year monthly and annual statistics for participating climate stations. Noncommercial research and educational uses also received favorable access treatment. [2036]

**Free access did not confer redistribution rights:** Users received limited, non-exclusive use rights. They could not transfer the supplied data, incorporate them into externally distributed databases or products, or convert nonprofit access into commercial use. Internal network distribution was restricted, and conditions attached to data received from other providers had to be respected. [2036]

The sanctions were specific: depending on the violation, fines could reach **RMB 10,000**, **RMB 30,000** or **RMB 50,000**, with suspension of data provision in serious cases. Thus meteorology supplied both an early sharing obligation and a clear counterexample to equating “free download” with unrestricted reuse. [2036]

**Earth-system sharing depended on standards:** The Data Sharing Network of Earth System Science, or **DSNESS**, supplied a technical and organizational disciplinary model. A September 2013 study described it as one of nine Scientific Data Sharing Project pilots that had become a long-term operational platform. It comprised one general center and 15 distributed sub-centers, organized around the principle that data originate in research and should be reused in research. [2037]

Its reference standard system had three levels—directive, general and application—and **21 implemented high-level standards and specifications**, including extensible metadata and data-quality-control specifications. Metadata means the descriptive information needed to understand and locate a dataset: its content, provenance, coverage, format and conditions of use. [2037]

The contrast with meteorology is instructive. Meteorology’s regulation specified provision and use rights; DSNESS’s standards addressed the practical problem of making heterogeneous research data intelligible and interoperable. The 2019 field-station plan added a nationally issued policy connection between observation standards, long-term accumulation and relevant data centers.

**Marine governance included sector-specific instruments:** The State Oceanic Administration issued its *Opinion on Standardizing Marine Ecological-Environment Monitoring Data Management* in **February 2015**. Its subject was stewardship of marine monitoring data, placing this resource within a sectoral management framework. The Ministry of Transport’s *Implementation Opinion on Opening and Sharing Transport-Sector Data Resources*, issued in **September 2016**, similarly established a sector-level direction for opening and sharing information resources. Both instruments were identified in contemporary scientific-data policy accounts. [2038]

Their significance is that scientific-data governance extended beyond funding agencies and CAS. Monitoring and transport data could enter research through organizations whose primary responsibilities were operational or administrative. A sectoral commitment to sharing, however, remained distinct from an access license for a particular dataset.

**Ocean-satellite practice demonstrated differentiated access:** The HaiYang satellite-data system allowed international researchers and noncommercial organizations to request archived data online. Large-volume near-real-time access required a memorandum of understanding or contract; releases excluded China’s exclusive economic zone and sensitive regions, and supplied data could not be used for military or commercial purposes. These were operational distribution conditions, not consequences that should automatically be attributed to the 2015 marine monitoring Opinion. [2039]

**Genomics combined several regulatory purposes:** Human-genomic data could simultaneously be treated as a national genetic resource, health information and sensitive personal information. Chen and Song’s 2018 study traced these overlapping frameworks, showing why a general scientific-data openness policy did not displace genetic-resource licensing, participant consent, health-data safeguards or cybersecurity requirements. [2025]

The earlier licensing framework included the *Interim Measures on Human Genetic Resources*, issued by MOST and the Ministry of Health on **10 June 1998**, and MOST’s **2 July 2015** administrative licensing service guide, effective from 1 October 2015. These instruments governed the collection, preservation and international provision of human genetic resources and associated information. They explain the historical development of controlled genomic-data exchange. [2025]

By the 2020 application cycle, NSFC’s health-science instructions expressly required compliance with the **Regulations on the Administration of Human Genetic Resources**, the 2019 State Council regulation. The 2018 study’s discussion of earlier drafts should therefore be read as historical development rather than as the final description of the legal position at the cutoff. [2023][2040]

**Privacy standards addressed individual protection:** The **2017 version** of the *Personal Information Security Specification*, issued on **29 December 2017** and effective **1 May 2018**, treated genetic information and family history as sensitive information. Developed through the National Information Security Standardization Technical Committee under the Standardization Administration of China, it recommended de-identification, anonymization, privacy policies and rules for sharing or transferring personal information. It was a non-binding national standard, not a genomics-specific statute. [2025]

Related health-sector instruments included the *Measures on Population Health Information (Trial Implementation)*, effective **5 May 2014**, and the *Measures on the Ethical Review of Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects*, issued **30 September 2016** and effective **1 December 2016**. The former addressed population-health information management and overseas storage; the latter established consent, privacy and ethical-review requirements for biomedical research involving people. [2025]

**Self-regulation complemented binding requirements:** The China National Genebank and Huazhong University of Science and Technology prepared consultation guidelines on sharing biobank samples and associated data during **2017–2018**. The draft addressed encrypted or anonymized handling, IRB review, research-purpose restrictions and international sharing subject to the relevant authorization. Its consultation status distinguished it from an enacted national requirement. [2025]

A streamlined licensing procedure was operating from **December 2017** for specified multicenter international clinical trials seeking drug or medical-equipment approval. Recognition of the principal institution’s ethical review reduced repeated examination, but did not remove the wider genetic-resource and cross-border controls. [2025]

**Disciplinary policy takeaway:** Different data required different kinds of openness: meteorology emphasized prescribed provision and restricted reuse, Earth-system science emphasized standards and federation, and human genomics emphasized ethically and legally controlled access.

## Practice Cases Listing: Research-Project Management

### MOST projects: submission tied to completion and service provision

**MOST had explicit submission procedures:** The clearest project-management examples connected funded research to resource submission, metadata, documentation, quality review and formal completion. They developed before the 2018 national Measures and later supplied practical models for the broader deposit requirement. [2041]

| MOST implementation case | Required material | Administrative trigger | Sharing route |
|---|---|---|---|
| June 2010 national-plan notice | Data and other project resources | Project summary and acceptance | National resource platforms |
| 2014 Basic Scientific Work cycle | Data, metadata, plan, documentation and tools | Completion of 48 projects | Online submission system |
| 2018 national Measures | Project-generated scientific data | Deposit before acceptance | Relevant data center |
| Specified 2019 Key R&D guidance | Models, algorithms, data and related outputs | Project-call obligations | Public publication or free online sharing |

**The 2010 notice broadened the deliverable:** MOST’s *Notice on Strengthening Management of Eleventh Five-Year Science and Technology Plan Project Summary and Acceptance*, issued in **June 2010**, required project-generated scientific literature, data, experimental reports, instruments, biological materials and resource information to enter national platforms. Initial implementation concentrated on population health and agriculture. [2041]

By **April 2013**, **1,923 projects or topics** in those two fields had deposited **74,628 resource items across 10 resource types**. The collection included scientific databases and datasets, but also papers, monographs, patents, standards, reports and physical resources. It was therefore evidence of project-resource collection at scale, not a count of 74,628 open research datasets. [2041]

**Quality review accompanied collection:** The Platform Center developed field-specific submission standards, manuals and an online submission system. Project institutions submitted resource plans and descriptions and uploaded data; expert committees reviewed quality and screened material for sharing. Training extended to more than 500 project-hosting units through 12 mobilization and training meetings. [2041]

This example shows why the implementation problem was larger than obtaining files. Project information, resource descriptions and quality review were necessary to turn project outputs into materials another researcher could discover and use.

**The 2014 cycle made deposit concrete:** A MOST acceptance notice covered **48 Basic Scientific Work Special projects**: 11 key projects approved in 2007, 27 projects approved in 2008 and 10 general projects approved in 2009. Acceptance comprised financial audit, financial acceptance, scientific-data submission and project completion acceptance. The first three activities had to be completed before final acceptance. [2042]

Submission required a project-information form, metadata form, scientific-data submission plan, actual data, explanatory documentation and software tools. The online system was scheduled to open on 15 June, data submission was due by the end of July, and acceptance by the end of November 2014. Classified data were excluded from this submission route and retained under secrecy rules; classification could not be used to withhold unrelated nonclassified data. [2042]

**Later reform changed administrative packaging:** The State Council’s **18 July 2018** *Measures for Optimizing Research Management and Improving Research Performance*, Guofa [2018] No. 25, called for a single comprehensive performance evaluation rather than separate financial and technical acceptance exercises. It also promoted one-time material submission and sharing of information already held in the national research-management system. [2043]

The 2014 four-part procedure is consequently best understood as a dated implementation case. The durable principle was that data stewardship formed part of project accountability, not that every later MOST project followed precisely the same sequence of acceptance meetings.

**Specified 2019 calls emphasized free sharing:** A January 2019 MOST notice issuing annual National Key R&D Plan guidelines, including the Stem Cell and Translational Research special program, required research-generated models, algorithms, data, data products, evaluation methods, results and parameters to be publicly published or shared online free of charge. This was an explicit project-call requirement extending beyond conventional final reports. [2024]

**MOST project-management judgment:** The strongest mechanism was the combination of an identifiable receiving service, a defined submission package and a project-management decision tied to submission; a general sharing statement alone supplied less operational direction.

### NSFC projects: publication access, targeted data duties and research safeguards

**NSFC’s documented obligations were differentiated:** NSFC combined publication openness, research-integrity requirements, discipline-specific conditions and participation in data-sharing infrastructure. These elements should be considered together without assigning the publication policy’s deadline to research datasets.

**Manuscripts had a 12-month deadline:** Under NSFC’s **15 May 2014** policy, funded researchers had to deposit final peer-reviewed manuscripts of covered research articles in the NSFC open-access repository and make them accessible within **12 months** of official journal publication. The object of this requirement was the manuscript. [2022]

The repository launched in **May 2015**. Within its first year, a 2016 account reported approximately **135,000 articles**, published during 1998–2015, associated with **274,634 authors** and **1,305 institutions**, with more than **669,001 downloads**. This was substantial implementation of publication access, while measuring a different activity from dataset deposit. [2035]

**Some calls expressly required data transfer:** The **2020 NSFC Guide to Programs** included seismology topics associated with the China Seismic Experimental Site for which funded projects had to fulfill the site’s **scientific-data and results-transfer agreement**. This is a concrete NSFC project-level data obligation, narrower than an award-wide procedure applying identically to every NSFC grant. [2023]

The same guide’s marine-science discussion described the NSFC Open Research Cruise, or ship-time sharing project. Researchers seeking participation had to explain their observation plan and fieldwork content in relation to the technical approach of their funded project. Here, sharing concerned research infrastructure and coordinated observation, with a project-specific planning requirement rather than a general dataset-release timetable. [2023]

**Integrity and confidentiality shaped applications:** The 2020 program guide required authentic, lawful applications and scientific-integrity commitments signed by applicants, main participants, host institutions and cooperative institutions. Proposals could not contain unlawful or confidential material. [2023]

NSFC and MOF’s **28 March 2019** *Notice on Further Improving Science Fund Project and Funding Management*, Guokegincai [2019] No. 31, strengthened host-institution responsibility for ethics and scientific and technological security. It required ethical-review and security-management systems while simplifying routine project administration. [2044]

For health-science proposals, the 2020 guide required institutional certification or approval for ethical and informed-consent requirements involving human participants. Human-genetic-resource research had to comply with the relevant regulation, and research involving highly pathogenic microorganisms required biosafety commitments. Applications lacking required approvals were not eligible for funding. [2023]

**Actual data services supported NSFC projects:** The Genome Sequence Archive at CAS’s Beijing Institute of Genomics reported serving more than 200 projects spanning NSFC, the National Key R&D Program and CAS Strategic Priority programs by June 2019. This demonstrates a functioning route for data storage, management and sharing by NSFC-supported researchers. It does not turn service participation into proof that every NSFC award used the same repository or deadline. [2045]

**The national framework still mattered:** The 2018 Scientific Data Measures expressly covered government-budget-funded plans, special programs and funds. For NSFC-supported work, this national layer interacted with host-institution rules, applicable disciplinary requirements and specific award conditions. The central distinction is between that general baseline and an NSFC-specific operational instruction naming a dataset, agreement, receiving service or deadline. [2020]

| Operational question | MOST examples | NSFC examples |
|---|---|---|
| What is submitted? | Scientific data and broader project resources | Manuscripts; data under specified agreements |
| Where does it go? | Designated platform or relevant data center | NSFC article repository; designated disciplinary route |
| What timing is explicit? | Before acceptance; call-specific deadlines | Manuscript openness within 12 months |
| What data planning appears? | Submission plans, metadata and documentation | Observation plans and targeted program conditions |
| What constrains access? | Classification, security and permitted use | Confidentiality, ethics, biosafety and domain rules |

**Funder comparison takeaway:** MOST’s examples specified a relatively complete data-submission chain, while NSFC’s documented arrangements combined a clear publication-access mandate with targeted data obligations and research safeguards; the difference was one of operational specificity, not exemption from national scientific-data governance.

## Practice Cases Listing: Repositories and Data Centers

### National infrastructure and representative disciplinary centers

**China operated a networked architecture:** The June 2019 designation of 20 National Science Data Centers organized services by discipline and resource type rather than replacing them with one national warehouse. The list included genomics, microbiology, space science, astronomy, Earth observation, polar science, ecology, materials corrosion, metrology, Earth systems, population health, agriculture, forestry, meteorology, earthquakes and ocean science. [2033]

**The national portal provided discovery:** The China Science and Technology Resource Sharing Network, promoted by MOST and MOF and operated through the national Platform Center, supplied cross-sector search, navigation, resource-management and assessment services. Its July 2014 profile reported nearly **six million records** and more than **1,000 TB** across six resource domains, including scientific data but also instruments, experimental bases, literature, natural resources and science-popularization material. [2046]

The portal’s role was therefore broader than dataset storage. It helped users identify services and resources across departments, while the underlying platforms retained responsibility for delivery and access conditions.

| Center or platform | Operating institution | Data coverage | Sharing model | Dated development status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| National Genomics Science Data Center | CAS Beijing Institute of Genomics | Human, animal, plant and microbial genomes | Archive and open-sharing services | National designation, June 2019 |
| National Space Science Data Center | CAS National Space Science Center | Space observations and project data | Long-term archiving and regulated sharing | National designation, June 2019 |
| National Population Health Data Center | Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences | Medical and population-health data | Public-service and reviewed access | Operational lineage from 2003–2004 |
| National Earth System Science Data Center | CAS IGSNRR | Earth-system and environmental data | Distributed services and data review | National designation, June 2019 |
| National Meteorological Science Data Center | CMA National Meteorological Information Center | Meteorological observations and products | Public downloads and classified services | Established sharing lineage from 2001 |
| National Astronomical Science Data Center | CAS National Astronomical Observatories | Astronomical observations and surveys | Archiving, releases and international services | National designation, June 2019 |

The host institutions and national designations are specified in the official annex; service characteristics draw on the respective institutional and disciplinary accounts. [2033][2047]

**Genomics combined capacity with project services:** The National Genomics Science Data Center was established at the Beijing Institute of Genomics with CAS co-building institutions. Its June 2019 announcement reported **12 PB of storage capacity**, a Genome Sequence Archive serving **more than 200 research projects**, and data-management services for **more than 130 domestic and foreign institutions**. [2045]

These figures describe infrastructure capacity and service reach. The center’s broad genomic remit was valuable because sequencing produces large, heterogeneous collections requiring specialist submission formats, processing and integration. Human-genomic access also operated within the privacy and genetic-resource framework discussed above.

The institutional sequence was already established: the BIG Data Center was approved in December 2015, national-center designation followed in June 2019, and the China National Center for Bioinformation was founded on the same institutional base in November 2019. [2048]

**Space science emphasized continuity and preservation:** The National Space Science Data Center was based at CAS’s National Space Science Center, with participation by other CAS institutions and the University of Science and Technology of China. Its remit included data from national programs, major projects and space-science observatories, together with paper-associated data. Its stated service direction combined timely sharing, long-term availability, security and management of international data flows. [2049]

This specialization mattered because scientific missions can produce data over many years, while later reanalysis may depend on calibration, instrument descriptions and consistent processing. A center organized around those needs performed a different function from a general-purpose file repository.

**Population health had a long operational lineage:** The National Population Health Data Center originated as a MOST scientific-data-sharing project in **2003**, launched in **April 2004**, and was recognized as an operational service open to society in **2010**. Its responsibilities included collection, review, processing, storage, certification, mining and sharing of population-health scientific data. Its institutional profile described **16 topical services** spanning basic medicine, clinical medicine, pharmacy, public health, traditional Chinese medicine and population and reproductive health. [2050]

An earlier workshop account described the medical sharing system’s intended free, nonprofit operation and its integration of basic, clinical and public-health resources, including a SARS and respiratory-disease database. That history shows that the 2019 national designation consolidated a pre-existing service tradition rather than creating medical-data sharing from nothing. [2047]

**Earth-system services federated distributed data:** The CAS Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research—**IGSNRR**—led the Earth-system sharing platform and hosted the 2019 National Earth System Science Data Center. Earlier services combined online downloads, offline applications, commissioned services and support integrated with research projects. [2029][2033]

For **2012**, the platform reported more than **2.3 million website visits**, **6,342 newly registered real-name users**, and support for **312 national, provincial or ministerial research projects or topics**. DSNESS’s 2013 description separately documented its one-general-center, 15-sub-center architecture and 21 standards. These records show both user-facing activity and the organizational work needed to connect distributed data. [2029][2037]

**Earlier national services already had substantial use:** Across six recognized scientific-data platforms, the national development report recorded **65 TB of integrated resources by the end of 2012**, more than **12.5 million website visits during 2012**, more than **9,000 user organizations**, and approximately **165,000 service-user person-times**. The platforms had collectively developed **40 management rules and 215 standards or specifications**, totaling 255 instruments. [2029]

The practical lesson is that national policy rested on existing service organizations with rules, users and technical staff. Its challenge was to extend and coordinate those capabilities across a wider range of research data.

### CAS databases, clouds and publication-linked repositories

**CAS supplied several complementary models:** Its infrastructure included long-running databases, geospatial download and processing services, a multidisciplinary Earth-data platform, and repositories linked to data journals. They solved different problems and should be selected by scientific purpose rather than ranked solely by storage volume.

These profiles are documented in CAS’s infrastructure presentation, the CASEarth launch report and the journal and repository accounts. [2051][2052][2053][2054]

**Scientific Databases represented sustained accumulation:** CAS’s Scientific Databases mission began in **1986** as a long-term, multi-institute effort. A 2014 presentation reported more than 350 research databases, approximately 500 datasets from 61 institutes and over 200 TB available for open access and download. The 2017 presentation reported more than 350 databases, **1,350 datasets** and over **600 TB** from the same number of institutes. [2027][2051]

The development direction was not simply to accumulate more files. CAS described a progression from individual research databases toward integrated resource databases and reference databases. That progression aimed to make data useful beyond the project or laboratory that originally produced them.

**Geospatial Data Cloud added usable delivery:** In 2017, the Geospatial Data Cloud, or **GSCloud**, reported **438.7 TB of resources**, more than **150,000 registered users**, and **1,783 supported publications**. It offered geographic, temporal, dataset and metadata filters, ordinary HTTP downloads, bulk FTP downloads and on-demand processing. [2051]

Its resources included Landsat, MODIS and digital-elevation data, with a focus on China and surrounding areas. The operational advance was to combine access with processing: a user could specify a region and output parameters rather than necessarily downloading and preparing an entire source collection.

**CASEarth linked data and computation:** CAS launched the Big Earth Data Science Engineering program, commonly called **CASEarth**, in 2018 and released its Earth Big Data Sharing Service Platform on **15 January 2019**. The launch announcement described approximately **5 PB** of resources. [2052]

| Earth-observation data | 1.8 PB | 36% |

**The release was phased rather than instantaneous:** A January 2019 account stated that users could then search approximately **40%** of the platform’s data online, with additional data to follow. The distinction between the announced resource pool, searchable holdings and permissions for obtaining individual datasets is central to understanding its development status. [2055]

CASEarth Databank supplied a concrete product-oriented example: **200,000 scenes**, each with 12 products, yielding **2.4 million products**. The Digital Silk Road component contained **94 thematic datasets** covering resources, environment, climate, disasters and heritage. These were components of the platform’s service architecture, not additional volumes to add indiscriminately to the 5 PB headline total. [2052]

**SCiDB joined deposit to publication:** The scientific-data repository at scidb.cn and the *China Scientific Data* online work platform went online at the end of **August 2015**. They supported dataset and paper submission, peer review and publication. The journal required authors to submit the dataset to the repository when submitting the accompanying data paper. [2053][2056]

This route was especially relevant to individual researchers’ datasets. It connected curation and persistent identification to an academic publication rather than depending exclusively on a large disciplinary program.

**Global-change publishing integrated three products:** The Global Change Research Data Publishing & Repository platform—**GCdataPR**—combined metadata, data products and data papers, supported by IGSNRR/CAS and the Chinese Geographical Society. Its repository lineage began in **June 2014**, while its journal added a formal linked-publication route in 2017. [2057]

A 2017 access notice allowed free downloading of digital articles and associated datasets, with free individual use conditional on data citation. It separately charged for printed issues and institutional server-network dissemination and interoperability rights. This makes GCdataPR a useful example of accessible data publication with differentiated reuse terms. [2054]

### International networks, trust and actual access

**WDS-China connected domestic and international services:** China established nine World Data Centers in **1988**, spanning disciplines including astronomy, space science, meteorology, oceanography and renewable resources and environment. The subsequent WDS-China network linked disciplinary services to international exchange, preservation and data-management cooperation. [2047]

The China Astronomical Data Center, supported by CAS’s National Astronomical Observatories, archived and released Guoshoujing Telescope data. The World Data Center for Renewable Resources and Environment, supported by IGSNRR, combined geographic, environmental, land-use, population and socioeconomic resources. These cases illustrate how a domestic disciplinary repository could also function as an international exchange node. [2058]

**Trust involved more than file access:** The WDS-China account identified eight mainland centers and recorded CoreTrustSeal certification for the astronomical center and the renewable-resources-and-environment center in late 2018 and early 2019. CoreTrustSeal evaluates organizational infrastructure, data management and technical capability, so certification addressed the reliability of stewardship rather than merely the existence of a download page. [2058]

The same account reported GCdataPR’s repository activity: **634 published datasets**, **257 GB online**, approximately **3.06 million visits**, **45,107 IP-based data users**, **208,726 downloaded files** and **4,001 GB downloaded**. These were repository-service measures, distinct from journal readership or the impact of individual data papers. [2058]

**Access varied even within Earth observation:** By February 2020, Fengyun current and archived meteorological satellite data were freely accessible online. CBERS data had progressed from free provision to Brazilian users in 2004, to African users in 2007, and worldwide free access in 2010. Ziyuan services differentiated free nonprofit and domestic educational access from paid commercial or private access. [2039]

In **November 2019**, China announced worldwide open availability of **16-meter imagery from GF-1 and GF-6**. This represented a specific expansion of access; it did not imply identical permissions for every Gaofen product or every Chinese satellite system. [2039]

| Access model | Representative case | What users received | Practical distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free public download | Specified CMA basic data | Prescribed observations and statistics | Redistribution remained restricted |
| Open online scientific resources | CAS databases and GSCloud | Searchable, downloadable resources | Resource-specific terms still mattered |
| Free individual use with citation | GCdataPR, 2017 terms | Articles and associated datasets | Institutional redistribution differed |

**Repository comparison takeaway:** China’s strongest infrastructure cases combined preservation, domain expertise, standards and services; users needed to distinguish whether a dataset was discoverable, obtainable, reusable and redistributable, rather than treating all four as equivalent.

### Principal titles and publication models

**Three titles illustrate the emerging system:** *China Scientific Data* provided a multidisciplinary data-paper route; the *Journal of Global Change Data & Discovery* specialized in linked global-change data publication; and the *Digital Journal of Global Change Data Repository* represented the repository-centered digital-publication route. Their principal characteristics were as follows.

| Journal | Sponsor or publisher | Launch timing | Scope and publication model | Development by the cutoff |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *China Scientific Data* | CAS; CAS Computer Network Information Center | 2015 | Multidisciplinary data papers with repository deposit | Bilingual quarterly; over 70 papers reported in 2017 |
| *Journal of Global Change Data & Discovery* | IGSNRR/CAS and Chinese Geographical Society; GCdataPR | March 2017 | Global-change papers linked to datasets | Bilingual quarterly; 73 linked papers in 2017 |
| *Digital Journal of Global Change Data Repository* | IGSNRR/CAS and Chinese Geographical Society; GCdataPR | June 2014 preprint lineage | Repository-centered global-change data publication | Monthly publication from June 2020 |

The launch and publishing characteristics are documented in the contemporary journal announcements and the global-change publication metadata. [2053][2057][2059]

**Data papers addressed a credit problem:** A data paper describes a dataset’s origin, collection, processing, structure, quality and potential use. Unlike a conventional research article, its principal contribution is the documented data resource rather than a new scientific finding derived from it. A January 2020 CAS Bulletin discussion identified data journals as a means of recognizing the work of data producers who might otherwise receive limited authorship credit in conventional publications. [2024]

This incentive mechanism complemented administrative submission. A mandate could require a researcher to deposit data; a data paper could make the deposited resource visible, citable and academically recognizable.

### China Scientific Data: a multidisciplinary repository-linked journal

**The journal made deposit part of submission:** *China Scientific Data*—《中国科学数据》—was founded in **2015**, supervised by CAS and hosted by the CAS Computer Network Information Center, or **CNIC**. It was among the first pilot national online serial publications and received the domestic serial identifier **CN11-6035/N**. Its initial submission instructions required simultaneous dataset submission to SCiDB. [2053]

Its scope included life science and medicine, Earth systems, space science and astronomy, physics, chemistry, materials, information science and social science. It also accepted selected data-science reviews and viewpoints. Its central product remained the data paper linked to the underlying resource.

**The workflow combined reviewing and curation:** CAS’s 2017 presentation described submission of both paper and dataset, paper review, data curation, expert review and identifier and metadata processing. The journal was an open-access bilingual quarterly and had published **more than 70 data papers** by the presentation date. [2051]

The online work platform and repository entered trial operation in August 2015, with formal operation and promotion scheduled from 2016. A **14 January 2018** meeting established the Chinese-English edition’s editorial committee, demonstrating continuing organizational development beyond the initial launch. [2056]

**Multidisciplinary scope exceeded the early author base:** A 2019 association-rule study comparing *China Scientific Data* with the international journal *Scientific Data* found that the Chinese journal’s authors were concentrated in some CAS institutes, with relatively few institutions outside CAS. Earth science and biological science were prominent, while social science received less attention. [2060]

The study also found that receipt-to-publication intervals for both journals were mostly **100–200 days**. That observation described publication patterns in the analyzed articles, not a service guarantee. More importantly, it showed that a formally broad journal scope did not immediately produce equally broad disciplinary or institutional participation.

**Selection should follow the publication task:** *China Scientific Data* was particularly relevant when a researcher needed a multidisciplinary venue combining dataset deposit, description and review. Its early CAS-centered author base reflected the infrastructure and community supporting it, while expansion beyond that base was an important dimension of its development.

### Global-change journals: integrated publication and differentiated rights

**The 2017 journal specialized in linkage:** The *Journal of Global Change Data & Discovery*—《全球变化数据学报》—launched in **March 2017** as a bilingual quarterly sponsored by IGSNRR/CAS and the Chinese Geographical Society. Its stated purpose was to publish global-change data papers linked to scientific data, promoting sharing, intellectual-property protection and scientific discovery. [2057]

Its scope combined geography, natural resources, ecology, environment, atmosphere, ocean, inland waters, socioeconomic subjects and human history. It also covered data policy, technology and capacity building. This was disciplinary specialization around global change, but with substantial interdisciplinarity within that field.

**Dataset linkage was measurable:** In **2017**, **73 papers** were linked to datasets published in the Global Change Research Data Publishing System, representing **79.35%** of that year’s papers. The founding-year account identified integrated data-and-paper publication as the journal’s principal innovation. [2057]

The inaugural issue provides concrete examples: the **Global Land Surface Water Dataset at 30-meter Resolution, 2010**, and the **Photosynthetically Active Radiation Dataset at 5-kilometer Resolution over China–ASEAN, 2013**, appeared with accompanying article records and dataset links. These examples show the mechanism more clearly than a general statement of openness: the reader could move from a description of a defined scientific product to the associated data. [2061]

**Bilingual publication was an operating commitment:** The Chinese and English versions were required to match in authorship, affiliations, funding, scientific content, references, DOI and pagination. Neither language version had academic precedence. This arrangement aimed to serve domestic and international communities without creating separate substantive records. [2057]

In its first year, the journal reported **289 authors including coauthors**, representing **74 organizations**, comprising 61 domestic and 13 foreign organizations. Its sponsors also organized nine outreach presentations in 2017 to explain data publication and sharing. [2057]

**Free reading coexisted with paid services:** The 2017 terms permitted free downloading of digital articles and associated datasets and free individual use with citation. Printed issues cost **RMB 200 per issue**, or **RMB 800 for four issues**. The institutional digital, network-distribution and interoperability package cost **RMB 2,200 per issue**, or **RMB 8,800 annually**. [2054]

The distinction matters for both readers and institutions. An individual researcher’s permission to download and cite data was different from a library’s or service provider’s permission to redistribute collections or integrate them into an institutional network.

**The digital repository journal followed another route:** The *Digital Journal of Global Change Data Repository* traced its first preprint issue to **June 2014** and began monthly publication in **June 2020**. It shared the IGSNRR/CAS and Chinese Geographical Society sponsorship and GCdataPR publishing infrastructure, but was a distinct title from the quarterly *Journal of Global Change Data & Discovery*. [2059]

The global-change journal’s listing identified Crossref, the Chinese Science Citation Database and CNKI as discovery or indexing channels. These served different functions: Crossref supplied DOI registration and linking, while the Chinese systems supported literature discovery and citation-related access. [2059]

### What the journal comparison shows

**The main difference was publication design:** *China Scientific Data* offered a multidisciplinary CAS-centered journal and repository workflow. The global-change journals concentrated on a domain community and integrated dataset, metadata and paper publication within a common platform. Both approaches sought to improve credit and reuse, but they organized the relationship between journal and repository differently.

**Paper counts were not impact rankings:** The more-than-70-paper figure for *China Scientific Data* was a cumulative presentation-era count; the 73 linked papers for the global-change journal described one calendar year and a particular linkage measure. Likewise, GCdataPR’s dataset downloads measured repository activity, not readership or citation impact of its journal. The appropriate comparison was therefore scope, workflow, documented linkage and access conditions.

**Journal practice takeaway:** Data journals supplied a practical bridge between sharing obligations and researcher incentives, but the scientific value of publication still depended on the underlying dataset’s documentation, quality, identifiers and usable access terms.

**Project-management implementation**

National Key R&D Plan (MOST): evaluation rule issued 2018-12-14; data-submission plan issued 2019-12-26. At establishment, submitters set data, quality control, opening time and sharing; the data manager reviews and the project manager approves. Seven stages run plan → preparation → submission → review → aggregation → publication/sharing → maintenance. Data require an approved-center certificate plus disposal, ownership, preservation and open-sharing plans; the default was MOST’s National Glacier–Permafrost–Desert Scientific Data Center, among 20 announced centers [2192]. Evaluation materials are due within 3 months; approved deposit unlocks acceptance, archiving follows within 1 month, and missing reports/materials may mean “not passed.” Scores ≤90 are qualified; >90 may be excellent, capped at 15% per special program [2191][2192]. Secret or unprocessed privacy data are excluded; approved internal preferential use is generally ≤12 months. Otherwise access is open by default subject to users, review, security, intellectual-property and citation rules. Designated-center storage and sharing are free and support metadata, CSTR/DOI, search, downloads, APIs and visualization [2192].

**Centers, repositories, and journal implementation**

Science-data centers collect, classify, process, catalogue, preserve, secure, issue receipts and share; fiscal-funded data are open by default except secrecy or special rules, with public-interest services free and commercial services contracted. MOST and the Ministry of Finance govern; departments supervise; the National Science and Technology Infrastructure Center runs the shared network and evaluation, including self-review, biennial assessment, funding, correction/removal and sanctions [2190][2200]. The 2004–2010 outline built 10 center groups and 3 networks; six platforms operated by 2011, and their 2012 totals were 65 TB, 12.5 million visits, 9,000 organizations, 165,000 users and 1,025 projects [2078][2094]. NADC lists Earth-system, Tibetan Plateau, space, genomics and meteorological centers, but their full profiles remain unresolved; its astronomy center provides archiving, backup, publication, sharing, analysis and data fusion [2164]. These were not interchangeable national policy authorities. Their importance lay in converting general obligations into domain-appropriate data products, reviews and access arrangements.

**Professional support addressed researcher workload:** The CAS National Science Library trained **56 special-library librarians** at a repository and data-management workshop in **March 2015**. This support role mattered because researchers could not meet long-term stewardship requirements through storage space alone. Metadata preparation, rights management, documentation and repository submission required practical skills and assistance.

**International cooperation supplied additional mechanisms:** China joined CODATA in **1984**, with CAS representing the country, and developed international data-center connections thereafter. In **September 2019**, CODATA, with CAS support, organized an international workshop in Beijing whose participants released the *Beijing Declaration on Research Data*, calling for cooperation in making publicly funded research data as open as possible globally. This was an international declaration and community initiative, distinct from a binding Chinese administrative rule. [2024][2039]

**Institutional leadership takeaway:** China’s system advanced through a division of labor: national authority established obligations, finance and assessment sustained services, research organizations supplied infrastructure, and disciplinary communities made data meaningful and usable.

**Institutional architecture**

**National coordination, funding, and implementation**

The **General Office of the State Council** was the issuing channel for the 2018 *Measures for the Management of Scientific Data*; the available card evidence supports its promulgation of the national measure and its objective of strengthening scientific-data management and open sharing, but does not establish additional office-specific implementation beyond issuance. [2255] The measure’s wider mechanism was clearer: government-budget-funded science programs had to submit their scientific data, and a 2019 MOST key-R&D-program guideline required models, algorithms, data, data products, evaluation methods, research results, and parameters generated during research to be openly published or shared online for free. This marked an explicit extension from large-science data toward “small-science” research data. [2256]

**CAS and CNIC as institutional infrastructure drivers**

The **Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS)** had both an international coordination role and a domestic infrastructure role. In **June 1984**, CAS represented China in joining the international data committee of the International Council of Scientific Unions. [2256] CAS’s scientific-database work began in the 1970s; national approval for a CAS scientific-database information system came in **1986**, construction began in **1987**, and the system later received CAS and national science-and-technology awards. During the 10th Five-Year Plan it had **503 professional sub-databases**, and during the 11th it reported **148 TB** of shareable integrated data. [2271]

CAS CNIC was the overall responsible unit for CAS’s 12th-Five-Year **Scientific Data Resource Integration and Sharing Project**, one of five major CAS informatization projects. By the project account’s 2016 reporting point, the infrastructure included **52 PB** of distributed storage, **8 PB** of cloud storage, about **300 physical servers**, more than **5,000 virtual machines**, and **38 PB** of archive capacity, with “one primary, one backup plus 12 sub-centers” disaster recovery. **Fifty-eight units** jointly built **40 databases**, integrating **655 TB** of shareable resources and supporting **131 research projects**; the project also reported **751 papers, 55 software copyrights, and 30 patents**. [2271] Its infrastructure, research-data cloud, and application cloud supplied storage, archiving, computing, metadata discovery, registration, review, publication, monitoring, evaluation, access, and sharing services. The project’s planned integration target was **500 TB** during the 12th Five-Year period. [2271]

CAS CNIC also connected infrastructure to publication. In **August 2015**, it obtained one of China’s first pilot network-serial-publication approvals and founded *China Scientific Data* (CN11-6035/N), promoting scientific-data publication, citation, intellectual-property protection, openness, and sharing. Its stated data-publication model covered raw data and processed data products and aimed to help users discover, obtain, understand, reanalyse, and cite data. [2271] The evidence identifies CNIC as an implementation and service actor, not as the issuer of a separate policy; it does not document a CNIC-specific NSFC or MOST project-data rule. [2271]

**Supporting institutional and publication channels**

The **2000** joint MOST–NSFC proposal called for public-interest sharing of state-owned scientific data and construction of a national sharing-service system within **10 years**, initially emphasizing large-science data. [2256] Data journals also functioned as incentive infrastructure: Wiley launched *Geoscience Data Journal* and *Biodiversity Data Journal* in **2012**, while *Scientific Data* launched as an open-access journal in **2014**, using data descriptors, repository links, and FAIR-oriented reuse practices. [2256]

The Renmin University China Survey and Data Center illustrates a social-science implementation channel. In **2016**, its deputy director presented the China National Survey Database’s strategy and implementation at China Open Access Week and discussed opening and sharing basic social-science and research data. [2111] The center also lists collaborations with the National Development and Reform Commission, China Association for Science and Technology, Ministry of Education, and China Development Research Foundation. [2257] Its archive rationale was practical: individually held survey data often became closed, producing repeated research, weak quality evaluation, incompatible formats, and longer project cycles; a fully open survey database was proposed to integrate and reuse those resources. [2081]

Finally, a **2024** NSFC notice describes an Open Access Repository and university–NSFC interoperability, agreements, joint working groups, unified standards, and paper-data sharing; because it is post-cutoff, it cannot establish an NSFC initiative before 2020. [2273] Likewise, a **2025** retrospective account of MOST–CAS collaboration and a 2025 policy concerning data-engineering teams, national centers, journal columns, and open datasets are excluded from the historical findings. [2261][2272]

**Infrastructure and coordination in practice**

The infrastructure layer is the clearest documented policy-to-practice channel, though it does not prove universal openness. The National Basic Science Data Center/CNIC profile lists discovery, submission, preprints, technical services, project archiving, metadata and quality tools, portal security, and citation standards [2112]. It reports **6.5 PB**, **62,828 resources**, **4,325 projects**, **42 units**, and **46.4333 million visits**, but gives no observation date; its 2020–2025 footer leaves cutoff status unresolved [2112]. CNIC, established in **March 1995**, is described as CAS’s network, computing, and data-infrastructure operator, including the National Basic Science Data Center, CAS data-center system, and China Science and Technology Cloud [2276]. A CAS account describes unified one-stop search/access and storage, processing, and data-intensive-discovery tools [2260]. ScienceDB is described as CNIC’s long-term sharing and publication repository with FAIR and dataset-citation functions; publisher recommendations are reported, but operation before 2020-06-30 remains unresolved [2065][2123][2112].

Centers operationalized lifecycle management and conditional access. CAS’s National Ecological Science Data Center aggregates ecosystem observations, surveys, experiments, remote sensing, models, paper data, and citizen-science data; applies collection-to-quality-control lifecycle management and standards; and offers online/offline access through public, protocol-sharing, and restricted modes [2114]. The China Science and Technology Resources Sharing Network identifies NSTIC as copyright holder and technical-support organization [2114]. A later CNIC account reports a platform built by **42 research institutions** and **20 national scientific-data centers**, with standards, software, and portals; its later or undated figures include **>2.7 PB**, **2 sub-centers**, **22 subject databases**, **34 regulations**, **57 specifications**, **23 national standards**, and **116 professionals**. Its cutoff-compatible finding is that China launched National Key R&D scientific-data archiving in **June 2020**, with the center undertaking basic-discipline archiving [2275]. The space-science center’s registration/login pathway and GB/T 33990/91/96/97/98-2017 standards for quality, dictionaries, product grading, formats, and metadata show disciplinary standardization rather than automatic open download [2113].

NSFC was a core funding and project-governance driver: established **14 February 1986**, initially under the State Council and managed by MOST from **2018** while relatively independent, it directed and coordinated the national fund, formulated basic-research plans, accepted applications, organized peer review, and administered projects [2267]. The cited profile does not establish a project data plan, deposit destination, access rule, or acceptance consequence, so NSFC’s data-specific role remains unverified. A later synthesis reports eligibility, correspondence, and conference review with voting; criteria included scientific value, originality, social impact, feasibility, experience, budget, other funding, past implementation, and continued funding. It also reports the 2003 joint evaluation decision involving MOST, CAS, and NSFC and 2018 State Council/CPC principles of differentiated, objective, and fair evaluation [2263]. These are priority-transmission mechanisms, not data rules.

A later policy-system study reports no policy explicitly named “open science” in its corpus; openness, sharing, and collaboration were scattered across policies, with uneven implementation. It identifies multi-agency coordination, public engagement, investment, and evaluation/incentive mechanisms as stronger channels than collaborative knowledge production or the science-and-technology business environment, and attributes to CAS promotion of data management, journals, infrastructure, and basic-research collaboration [2264]. CAS and NSFC’s **2014** policies required final peer-reviewed publicly funded papers to enter repositories and become open within **12 months**—CAS through institute repositories and publication support, NSFC through its own repository—not research-data deposit [2266]. A later study reports that *China Industrial Economics* required original data, code, processed data, and supplements in **November 2016**; citation effects were **+0.249**, **+1.071**, and **+0.497** in years 1–3 and **−1.237** in year 5, an economics-journal result not generalizable nationally; it also reports an NSFC open-science-attitudes survey in **April 2020** without findings or a data rule [2265]. A later analysis using NSFC data through 2019 reports Key Programs averaging about **RMB 290,000/project**, roughly seven times individual grants, with about **40** elite universities receiving approximately half of funding; its 2026 publication makes this only a qualified concentration hypothesis [2274]. A later review reports the 2018 national measure, a May 2019 human-genetic-resources sharing restriction, June 2019 MOST listing of genome repositories, and population-health, BIGD, and NMDC milestones in 2004, 2015, and 2019; these indicate conditional, security-sensitive openness but require primary-record verification [2268].

**National coordination and infrastructure**

**MOST** launched the Scientific Data Sharing Program (SDSP) in **2002**; **24 government agencies** participated by 2007. SDSP combined policy and legal work, standards, pilot projects, national centers, and international cooperation. By the end of **2006**, it reported **4** national laws or regulations in draft, **39** departmental or agency rules drafted or released, **23 of 32** principal standards completed, training, and **more than 120** sectoral standards. Its projected **2010** architecture comprised **40** centers or networks, **300** master databases, and **1** portal across six fields; by December **2005**, pilots had integrated **864 databases** containing nearly **50 TB**, while end-2005 reports recorded over **50,000** registered users, about **15 TB** downloaded, and more than **1,225** national key projects benefited. The same passage says “14 million” accessing users, inconsistent with the registered-user figure; that number is therefore not treated as reliable [2259]. The 2006 national S&T strategy treated infrastructure as an innovation prerequisite and projected R&D expenditure at **2% of GDP by 2010** and **at least 2.5% by 2020**—targets, not observed outcomes [2262].

The **General Office of the State Council** converted this orientation into the **2018 Measures for the Management of Scientific Data (科学数据管理办法)**: raw and derived data were to be submitted to authorized centers for inspection; government-funded data were to reach national repositories before overseas publication; and timely upload plus public and departmental sharing were expected. MOST was assigned the leading center-building and data-management role, while research institutes, universities, and enterprises were expected to comply. Openness remained conditional: state secrets, national security, public interest, trade secrets, and personal privacy excluded data from public sharing, and sensitive exchange with foreign parties required approval [2258].

**NSTIC**, identified as the platform center and copyright holder of the China Science and Technology Resources Sharing Network, operationalized catalogs, national scientific-data centers, identifiers, data deposit, classification, and regional resource sharing; MOST and the Ministry of Transport appear among connected departments [2115]. The national scientific-data-center system thus functioned as an aggregation and service structure. Its Earth-observation example coordinated sectoral centers, research institutions, and enterprises and described whole-life-cycle collection, management, analysis, mining, and sharing; its registry identified MOST as funder, the Earth Observation Data Center as technical institution, and the National Remote Sensing Center as general institution [2082]. The evidence establishes a network connection for Transport, but not a separate Ministry of Transport open-data rule.

CAS’s principal implementation channel was **CNIC**: it operated the National Basic Science Data Center and the General Center of the CAS Data Center System and initiated China Science and Technology Cloud in **2017**, providing retrieval, access, use, transaction, delivery, and sharing services through infrastructure described as secure and reliable [2116]. CAS also maintained a U.S.–China Roundtable on Scientific Data Cooperation from **2006–2011** [2270].

**NSFC** is evidenced here chiefly as a funder: an NIH–NSFC MOU in **October 2010** led to a January **2011** solicitation receiving **220 proposals** and supporting **34 projects**, but these cards do not establish an NSFC-specific open-data rule [2270]. **SOA** had a marine data-exchange role: its NOAA panel involved scientist visits in **2012–2013**, produced more than **20 papers** and a data-assimilation/reanalysis MOU, and hosted the APEC Marine Sustainable Development Center in **2011**; no SOA project-data rule, domestic repository, journal link, or policy-table instrument is established here [2270]. Thus the chain was national mandate → MOST/NSTIC infrastructure → departmental and legal-person implementation → catalogued, deposited, and accessible data, moderated by top-down funding guidance, investigator interests, secrecy, and proprietary constraints [2258][2270].

**CAS/CNIC.** CNIC, established in March 1995, operated CAS research-data infrastructure: CSTNET/HPC services, the CAS scientific database and storage backup, and the Chinese CODATA committee secretariat; it also promoted data-sharing and identifier standards [2269]. It ran the National Basic Science Data Center, the CAS Data Center System’s General Center, and China Science and Technology Cloud, a data-and-computation infrastructure for discovery [2276]. A 2021 retrospective attributes weak sharing to missing channels, standards, ownership and incentives; CNIC’s publication/storage platform added identifiers and citation [2065].

## Overall Assessment at 30 June 2020

**The system was established but transitional:** A grounded qualitative assessment places its maturity between **operational disciplinary services** and **still-developing researcher-level sharing**. Long-running meteorological, Earth-system and medical infrastructures, functioning repositories, data journals and the 2019 national-center network demonstrated substantial capacity. Contemporary assessments nevertheless described broad sharing as exploratory, concentrated in natural and engineering sciences, with immature incentives and persistent data held outside organized services by individual researchers. [2038][2064]

**The principal transition concerned coverage:** Earlier efforts concentrated on large scientific facilities, observation networks and accumulated disciplinary collections. The 2018 national Measures, CAS’s 2019 trial policy and specified MOST project calls brought individual projects and paper-associated data more explicitly into governance. Data journals and repository services addressed the accompanying credit and usability problems. [2024]

A stronger judgment of uniform implementation would depend on demonstrated project-deposit compliance across disciplines, consistent access and reuse terms, and systematic coverage beyond established centers; a comparative judgment about repository or journal leadership would additionally depend on comparable use and quality measures. At the cutoff, the supported conclusion was that China had built a national policy-and-infrastructure system, not that every research dataset was publicly reusable.

**Historical figures retain different meanings:** The statistics above are dated administrative or operator snapshots rather than one synchronized national census. Storage capacity, hosted resources and downloaded volumes measure different things; databases, datasets, articles, visits, registered accounts, IP-based users and person-times are also distinct. Calculated shares are identified, and the maturity assessment is qualitative. English policy names are descriptive translations; trial instruments, consultation drafts and institutional missions retain their stated status, while policy dates refer to the instruments rather than later website updates.
