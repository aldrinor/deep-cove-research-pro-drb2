# Retirement Savings Protection Gaps in Seven Asian Countries: Pension Rules in Force in Early 2025

As of early 2025, the principal retirement-protection gap in Indonesia, Malaysia, Pakistan, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Vietnam is incomplete and interrupted participation rather than the absence of pension institutions. Mandatory schemes are usually tied to formal employment or public service; informal and self-employed workers often depend on voluntary accounts. Long vesting periods—typically 10, 15, 20, or 25 years—frequently convert interrupted careers into lump sums instead of lifetime income. Defined-contribution schemes broaden access but commonly pay lump sums, permit early withdrawals, or leave longevity and investment risks with the worker. These mechanisms are consistent with the World Bank’s broader finding that informality, fragmented systems, and limited fiscal capacity constrain pension coverage in middle- and low-income countries [1].

The strongest early-2025 comparison is therefore rule-based, not a single country ranking. Thailand and Vietnam show particularly clear contribution-density and vesting problems; Indonesia combines low participation with low mandatory contribution rates; Pakistan and Sri Lanka have sharp public-private divisions; Malaysia has a substantial funded provident system but significant lump-sum and access risks; and the Philippines combines broad social insurance with a relatively high vesting threshold and a separate public-sector regime.

## Section 1: Comprehensive Overview of Pension Systems

Pension designations below distinguish pay-as-you-go defined benefit (PAYG DB), asset-backed defined contribution (asset-backed DC), occupational and personal voluntary arrangements, and severance obligations. A DB scheme promises a formula linked to service and earnings. A DC scheme accumulates contributions and investment or credited interest in an account. A severance DB is an employer-liability benefit triggered by termination or retirement rather than a funded lifetime pension.

The profiles cover the national and principal named arrangements in the supplied policy record. Where a provider or ancillary fund is identified but its current product-level formula is not specified, that field is marked as scheme-specific rather than filled with an unsupported number.

### Indonesia

Indonesia has three named mandatory pension providers—PT Taspen, PT Asabri, and BPJS Ketenagakerjaan—and two voluntary provider types, Employer Pension Funds (DPPK) and Financial Institution Pension Funds (DPLK) [2]. The central structural divide is between BPJS programs attached to employment and voluntary funds attached to employers or financial institutions.

**BPJS Ketenagakerjaan Pension Security (Jaminan Pensiun, JP).** Type: mandatory earnings-related PAYG DB. Coverage: public-sector employer categories are recognized in the statute, although their membership rules are regulated separately; private-sector employees of non-state-administrator employers are covered; self-employed workers are described in the OECD model as using the same system while covering both sides of the contribution. Participation: mandatory for covered workers. The statutory formula is 1% multiplied by contribution months and the weighted average annual wage during the contribution period, with the wage divided by 12 for the monthly pension base [3].

A periodical pension requires 15 years of contributions; below that threshold, the outcome is a lump sum. The OECD’s 6 December 2024 model reports a minimum of IDR363,300 per month and a maximum of IDR4.36 million per month after 15 years, while the original statutory amounts were Rp300,000 and Rp3.6 million and are subject to annual adjustment [4]. Indonesia’s Ministry of Manpower stated on 9 January 2025 that the normal pension age was 59 in 2025, rising to 65 in 2043 [5].

**BPJS Old-Age Security (Jaminan Hari Tua, JHT).** Type: mandatory asset-backed DC provident account. Coverage: employees are the core covered group; the OECD model also describes self-employed workers as covered through the same system; public-sector coverage follows the relevant employment category rather than a separate JHT design. Participation: mandatory for covered employees. Employees contribute 2% of earnings and employers 3.7% of payroll. At retirement, the balance can be taken as a lump sum or, when above IDR3 million, as a monthly payment for up to five years; it is therefore not automatically a lifetime annuity [4]. Payments can begin at age 56 under the cited legal overview [6].

**PT Taspen.** Type: mandatory public-service pension provider, placed in the public-sector DB category in the comparative pension inventory; the provider-level component mix is scheme-specific. Coverage: public-sector civil servants and related government personnel are the relevant target group; private-sector coverage is outside the stated target; self-employed coverage is not part of the identified public-service mandate. Participation: mandatory for covered public servants. A current provider-level benefit formula, minimum service rule, and separate retirement age are plan-specific.

**PT Asabri.** Type: mandatory public-service DB-category provider for defense and security personnel; the supplied architecture identifies the provider but does not state the component-level funding structure. Coverage: military, police, and related uniformed personnel; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the identified target. Participation: mandatory for covered personnel. The normal age and formula depend on the applicable service regime.

**Dana Pensiun Pemberi Kerja (DPPK).** Type: voluntary occupational pension fund, offering either DB or DC. Coverage: employees of sponsoring employers; a public- or private-sector employer may sponsor a fund, but the supplied legal overview does not impose one sectoral design; direct self-employed membership is not described. Participation: voluntary enrollment or employer participation. Contribution levels have no single statutory minimum in the cited overview, so outcomes depend on the sponsoring fund’s rules.

**Dana Pensiun Lembaga Keuangan (DPLK).** Type: voluntary personal asset-backed DC fund operated through a financial institution. Coverage: individuals, including self-employed workers; employees may participate personally or through an employer-related arrangement; the supplied source does not assign a separate public/private employment boundary. Participation: voluntary. The fund is DC-only, with age and payout conditions determined by the particular financial-institution product [6].

**Statutory termination package.** Type: employer-liability severance DB or DB-like termination obligation rather than a standalone pension account. Coverage: covered private-sector employees; public-sector and self-employed treatment is not defined in the cited supplementary-pension overview. Participation: mandatory employer obligation. Payments from a DPPK or DPLK can reduce the employer’s remaining termination-package liability, so supplementary pensions interact with severance protection rather than simply sitting above it.

**Indonesia’s protection signal.** The main gap is participation density. AMRO reported about 30.7 million active participants, equal to 16.5% of the working-age population, and described BPJS contribution rates as 8.7% of monthly income across old-age savings and pension funds [7]. The combination of a 15-year JP threshold, a JHT payout that can be limited to five years, and pre-retirement access means that legal coverage does not necessarily become lifetime income. The formal/informal divide is reinforced by the absence of childcare and unemployment credits in the OECD model.

### Malaysia

Malaysia’s core retirement arrangement is the Employees Provident Fund (EPF or KWSP), complemented by voluntary Private Retirement Schemes (PRS) and targeted EPF matching programs. The comparative pension inventory identifies an asset-backed DC private-sector arrangement and a separate public-sector DB category [1].

**Employees Provident Fund (EPF/KWSP).** Type: mandatory asset-backed DC provident fund. Coverage: covered employees in the private and other participating employment sectors; public-service employees may fall under the separate public-service pension or EPF arrangements depending on employment status; self-employed workers use voluntary routes such as i-Saraan. Participation: mandatory for covered employees. The OECD’s 6 December 2024 profile reports employee contributions of 11% up to age 60, employer contributions of 13% for monthly earnings below RM5,000 and 12% above that amount, and 4% employer contributions for workers aged 60 to 75. The profile reports no contribution ceiling [8].

Before age 55, 70% of contributions go to Account 1 for retirement and 30% to Account 2 for housing, education, medical and other approved purposes. Account 2 can be accessed through a one-time withdrawal at age 50. At 55, the accounts consolidate into Akaun 55, with post-55 contributions placed in Akaun Emas. The balance may be taken as a lump sum, installments, or a combination; the OECD reports a guaranteed minimum interest rate of 2.5% annually. These rules make EPF a substantial savings institution but do not ensure that the balance is converted into income for life.

**Public Service Pension Scheme.** Type: mandatory public-sector DB category. Coverage: public servants and government employees within the JPA-administered service framework; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the identified public-service target. Participation: mandatory for covered public servants. The supplied comparative inventory confirms a public DB category, but the scheme-level formula, minimum service, monetary floor, ceiling, and separate age are fund-specific in the evidence set. The country-level statutory comparator is age 55, but it should not be treated as a complete public-service formula.

**Private Retirement Schemes (PRS).** Type: voluntary personal or occupational DC. Coverage: employed and self-employed Malaysians; both private-sector and public-sector workers can participate as individuals, but PRS is not a substitute for mandatory public-service or EPF participation. Participation: voluntary. The Private Pension Administrator describes PRS as a voluntary long-term savings and investment scheme for employed or self-employed Malaysians, while the Securities Commission’s guidelines revised on 1 October 2024 identify retirement age as 55 or another age specified by the Commission [9][10]. PRS divides contributions between sub-account A and sub-account B in a 70:30 structure and allows listed pre-retirement withdrawals for specified events.

**i-Saraan.** Type: voluntary targeted EPF asset-backed DC matching program. Coverage: individual contributors outside ordinary payroll participation, especially self-employed or non-salaried workers; the supplied program announcement does not define a separate public/private employment boundary. Participation: voluntary. From 1 January 2025, the government match rose to 20%, capped at RM500 per year and RM5,000 over the individual’s lifetime. The program had 330,196 members and RM1.61 billion in contributions in the first half of 2024 [11].

**i-Suri.** Type: voluntary targeted EPF asset-backed DC matching program. Coverage: housewives registered in the e-Kasih database; public-sector and private-sector employment labels are not applicable to the household-targeted design; self-employed status is not required. Participation: voluntary. For 2025, the government match was 50% of contributions, capped at RM300 annually and RM3,000 over the lifetime or until age 55, whichever came first [11].

**Non-Malaysian worker route.** In October 2024, EPF stated that non-Malaysian workers could still contribute voluntarily while Budget 2025 announced an expansion of mandatory coverage expected to reach more than two million workers. Contribution rates, target groups, and implementation details were to be specified separately. Thus, the early-2025 position includes a transition from voluntary to intended broader mandatory coverage rather than a settled uniform rule.

**Malaysia’s protection signal.** EPF’s large funded base is a strength: AMRO reported EPF assets equal to 65.5% of GDP in 2022, compared with BPJS assets equal to 3.3% of Indonesian GDP. The comparison is not a benefit ranking because the funds have different coverage and payout structures. Malaysia’s central risk is that substantial balances remain exposed to early access and lump-sum depletion. Age 55 is an access point, not necessarily a retirement date, and the RM350 means-tested assistance at age 60 is a poverty backstop rather than an earnings-related pension.

### Pakistan

Pakistan has a private-sector earnings-related scheme, separate civil-service pensions, a public-sector provident fund, and voluntary fully funded DC arrangements. The separation between EOBI and civil-service coverage means that employment status determines both participation and benefit design.

**Employees’ Old-Age Benefit Scheme (EOBI).** Type: mandatory private-sector earnings-related DB or PAYG DB. Coverage: workers in covered private establishments; the OECD’s 6 December 2024 profile uses a threshold of five or more employees, while the retrieved EOBI Act text applies the mandatory rule to establishments with ten or more employees and permits smaller establishments to apply voluntarily [12][13]. Public servants are covered by the civil-service regime rather than EOBI. Self-employed coverage is not part of the stated EOBI target.

Participation: mandatory for workers in the statutory establishment category. The pension formula is average monthly wages multiplied by years of insurable employment and divided by 50, implying an estimated 2% of the average wage per contribution year. The minimum pension was PKR10,000 per month from July 2023. Fifteen years of contributions are required for the regular pension; two to fewer than 15 years produces a lump-sum old-age grant equal to one month of average monthly earnings per contribution year. Normal pension age is 60 for men and 55 for women, with miners able to use age 55 after 10 preceding years in mining. Early claiming can begin at 55 for men and 50 for women, with a 0.5% reduction for each completed month before the normal age.

**Civil-service pension.** Type: mandatory public-sector DB. Coverage: federal and other government servants within the pensionable civil service; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the stated population. Participation: mandatory for covered pensionable government service. The retrieved Pension Rules state that superannuation is at age 60. Ordinary pension requires at least 10 qualifying years; five to fewer than 10 years may produce gratuity. Twenty-five years permits retiring pension before superannuation under the stated conditions [14].

The rules state 70% of average emoluments after 30 years of qualifying service, with proportionate reduction for 10 to fewer than 30 years. The same excerpt displays a last-pay-drawn formula using `7 × qualifying service ÷ 300`, so the salary reference is best described as average emoluments with a last-pay formula presentation. A simple annual-equivalent estimate is 70 percentage points divided by 30 years, or approximately 2.33 percentage points per year. The supplied rule text gives no separate current monetary minimum or maximum.

**General Provident Fund (Federal Services).** Type: asset-backed DC provident account. Coverage: permanent pensionable and non-pensionable government servants, plus temporary or officiating servants after two years of continuous service; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the identified target. Participation: compulsory for the listed public-service categories and optional for other eligible government servants. Accounts record subscriptions and credited interest; the 2017 rules provide a 4% interest floor for qualifying existing subscribers if the rate set for a year falls below 4% [15]. The supplied rules do not establish a common normal retirement age or a lifetime-income conversion.

**Voluntary Pension System (VPS).** Type: voluntary fully funded DC. Coverage: adult Pakistanis with computerized identity documents, including self-employed and informal workers; public- and private-sector employees may also participate. Participation: voluntary. The World Bank describes VPS as a tax-advantaged self-contributory DC plan open to employed and self-employed people, while SECP’s expanded framework allows employers in both sectors to offer fully funded DC pensions [1][16]. The account is intended to provide retirement income, but the supplied rule summary does not state a single normal age or uniform benefit formula.

**Employer Pension Funds.** Type: voluntary occupational fully funded DC. Coverage: employees of group companies, holding companies, federal governments, and provincial governments; both public- and private-sector employees can be included; self-employed coverage is not stated. Participation: employer offering and worker enrollment are not established as universally mandatory. Fund-level contribution, retirement-age, and payout rules govern.

**BISP savings scheme pilot.** Type: voluntary targeted matched DC-like savings arrangement. Coverage: low-income workers reached through the Benazir Income Support Programme’s social registry, including informal workers; public/private and self-employed status depends on the pilot’s target population. Participation: voluntary pilot. The World Bank describes a 40% government match of gross savings up to a preset level at the end of the two-year pilot. It is a supplemental savings intervention, not a replacement for EOBI or civil-service pension entitlement.

**Pakistan’s protection signal.** EOBI’s 15-year pension threshold and civil service’s 10-year threshold are less severe than Thailand’s civil-service 25-year requirement but still expose mobile or late-entering workers to grants or gratuities. The gender difference in EOBI’s normal age is paired with an early-claim penalty. If a claimant used a full five-year early window, 60 months multiplied by 0.5% would imply a 30% reduction, subject to the scheme’s eligibility conditions. The absence of a universal non-contributory old-age pension makes formal employment and voluntary saving especially important.

### The Philippines

The Philippines combines SSS social insurance, the mandatory WISP or Mandatory MySSS Pension Booster, voluntary MySSS Pension Booster saving, and GSIS public-sector pensions. The result is broader design diversification than in a pure provident system, but the schemes retain separate worker boundaries and vesting rules.

**Social Security System (SSS) regular retirement pension.** Type: mandatory earnings-related PAYG DB. Coverage: private-sector employees, self-employed workers, overseas Filipino workers, household helpers, and specified voluntary SSS categories; public-sector employees covered by GSIS are not normally part of the same public-service pension. Participation: mandatory for covered employment categories, with voluntary SSS membership routes for others [17][18].

A lifetime monthly pension requires 120 monthly contributions before the semester of retirement. A claimant below 120 contributions receives a one-time lump sum equal to contributions plus interest. The SSS formula is the highest of: PHP300 plus 20% of AMSC plus 2% of AMSC for each credited year above 10; 40% of AMSC; or the minimum pension of PHP1,200 with at least 10 credited service years or PHP2,400 with at least 20 credited service years. The SSS page separately states a PHP1,000 additional benefit, while the OECD model embeds PHP1,000 in its displayed formula.

The salary base is the Average Monthly Salary Credit, with the OECD using the higher of average earnings over the five years ending six months before claim or average earnings over the contribution period. Normal retirement is age 60 when separated from employment or self-employment; technical retirement is age 65. Mineworker and racehorse-jockey rules are occupational exceptions rather than a general age rule.

**WISP or Mandatory MySSS Pension Booster.** Type: mandatory asset-backed DC provident account. Coverage: qualifying SSS members with covered earnings above the applicable threshold and no final claim for retirement, total disability, or death; the principal target is employed members, with an employer share for covered employees. Participation: mandatory for qualifying SSS members. The OECD’s 2024 profile uses a PHP20,250 monthly covered-earnings threshold; the SSS 2024 Pension Booster FAQ uses ₱20,000. WISP investments are intended to place at least 75% in low-risk government securities, with the remainder in blue-chip corporations, and accumulated assets are converted into an annuity paid over at least 15 years [18][19].

**MySSS Pension Booster, formerly WISP Plus.** Type: voluntary personal asset-backed DC. Coverage: all SSS members with one posted contribution and no final benefit claim, including self-employed workers, maritime professionals, OFWs, corporate executives, and other members with savings capacity. Participation: voluntary. Contributions can be as low as ₱500 per payment. Withdrawals are restricted in the first year, permitted with reduced income during years two to four, and more broadly permitted from the fifth year onward [20].

**Government Service Insurance System (GSIS).** Type: mandatory public-sector DB. Coverage: compensated national and local government employees, covered government corporations, constitutional bodies, and other statutory government employers. AFP, PNP, and contractual workers without an employer-employee relationship are excluded under Republic Act No. 8291. Private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the GSIS population. Participation: mandatory for covered public employees [21].

The GSIS formula is 37.5% of revalued average monthly compensation plus 2.5% for every service year above 15, subject to a 90% ceiling of average monthly compensation. The salary base is average monthly compensation over the final 36 months. The statutory minimum is PHP1,300 per month, with at least PHP2,400 per month for members with at least 20 years of service after the Act’s effectivity. Retirement-benefit entitlement begins at age 60 with 15 years of service; compulsory retirement is age 65 with 15 years. Three to fewer than 15 years produces a separation benefit rather than the regular pension.

**Personal Equity and Retirement Account (PERA).** Type: voluntary personal retirement account, mapped to the VP category. Coverage: intended as an individual retirement arrangement for eligible Filipino savers, including employed and self-employed persons; detailed early-2025 coverage and investment rules are product-specific in the supplied record. Participation: voluntary. The BSP page currently links to a 13 July 2026 FAQ, so that later document is not used to define early-2025 rules [22].

**The Philippines’ protection signal.** The SSS 120-month requirement is shorter than the 15-year threshold in several DB schemes but still turns a decade of interrupted contributions into a lump sum. GSIS offers a stronger public-sector formula and a 90% ceiling, but its 15-year requirement and separate public-service membership leave workers who move between sectors exposed to fragmented entitlements. The January 2025 SSS contribution change from 14% to 15%, with the maximum MSC rising from PHP30,000 to PHP35,000, improves financing and the contribution base, but it does not by itself solve contribution-density gaps [23].

### Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka has a strong public-service DB pension but relies primarily on provident funds for private-sector and semi-public workers. The architecture is therefore sharply divided between lifetime public pensions and private-sector account balances.

**Public Servants’ Pension Scheme (PSPS).** Type: mandatory government-financed public-sector DB. Coverage: permanent civil servants, armed forces, provincial and local-government employees, government teachers, and judicial officers; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the scheme. Participation: mandatory for eligible permanent public servants. The ILO description states that 10 years in a permanent post is required, with retirement at age 55 for men and 50 for women. After 30 years, the reported pension outcome is 85% to 90% of last salary, plus a lump sum equal to two years of last drawn salary [24].

The 85%–90% outcome implies an average-equivalent estimate of approximately 2.83%–3.00% of final salary per year over 30 years, but the source describes ad hoc formula adjustments rather than a linear statutory accrual. A separate current minimum or maximum monthly amount is not stated in the supplied rule set.

**Public Servants’ Provident Fund (PSPF).** Type: mandatory public-sector contributory DC provident fund. Coverage: government employees who are not eligible for PSPS; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the identified target. Participation: mandatory for eligible public employees. The ILO historical description reports combined government and employee contributions of 20% of salary—12% from government and 8% from the employee—and transfer of the government contribution to PSPS when a worker subsequently becomes eligible for PSPS. The supplied description does not establish a common normal retirement age.

**Employees’ Provident Fund (EPF).** Type: mandatory asset-backed DC. Coverage: private-sector and semi-public-sector employees who do not receive a pension benefit; self-employed participation is not identified as ordinary EPF membership. Participation: mandatory for covered employees. The Central Bank and Department of Labour state a minimum contribution of 12% from employers and 8% from employees, or 20% of gross monthly earnings in total. The account is invested and credited with interest, and the normal withdrawal ages are 55 for men and 50 for women [25][26].

The EPF is paid as a lump sum rather than a lifetime annuity. Members with more than 10 years of service and an account balance above Rs300,000 can withdraw up to 30% of the balance, capped at Rs2 million, for housing or medical treatment under the cited statutory route. Migration, permanent disability, marriage-related departure from employment for female members, and entry into pensionable employment are additional benefit events.

**Employees’ Trust Fund (ETF).** Type: mandatory employer-funded DC for covered formal employment, with a voluntary contribution route for self-employed and migrant workers. Coverage: specified public- and private-sector enterprises; self-employed persons and migrant workers are eligible to join voluntarily. Participation: mandatory for covered enterprises and voluntary for self-employed or migrant contributors. The ILO description identifies a 3% employer contribution; the ETF Board identifies public- and private-sector enterprise coverage and voluntary self-employed and migrant membership [27].

**Approved Private Provident Funds (APPFs).** Type: voluntary or sponsor-based occupational DC, capable of substituting for EPF when statutory minimum contribution conditions are met. Coverage: employees of sponsoring companies; public/private status depends on the sponsor; self-employed coverage is not described. Participation: fund-specific, because the source establishes employer sponsorship and EPF substitution but does not state one universal enrollment rule. Contribution rates must not be lower than EPF requirements, and benefits follow the approved fund rules.

**Widows, Widowers and Orphans Pension Scheme.** Type: mandatory public-sector survivor DB, not an old-age retirement plan. Coverage: public servants’ spouses and children under the statutory categories; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the identified scheme. Participation: mandatory for covered public servants. The ILO description reports contributions of 4%–7% of salary and survivor benefits of 100% for spouses and 50% for children under the relevant conditions. It has no normal old-age retirement age.

**Sri Lanka’s protection signal.** The PSPS provides a comparatively strong formula for a securely attached public servant, but the private-sector EPF and ETF primarily produce balances at exit. The gender-specific retirement ages of 55 for men and 50 for women shorten women’s accumulation period even though women generally live longer. The older ILO evidence reports approximately 3.1 million workers covered, or 22% of the population over age 15, against an informal-employment share of 62% in 2007. These figures are historical indicators, but they illustrate why a mandatory formal-sector account does not equal broad national protection.

### Thailand

Thailand has the most visibly multi-layered architecture in the group: a near-universal old-age allowance, mandatory and voluntary Social Security Office (SSO) articles, public-service DB and DC schemes, the National Savings Fund, employer provident funds, and several ancillary products. The ILO’s 2022 diagnostic review describes the system as fragmented across employment categories, with high informal and vulnerable employment.

**Old-Age Allowance (OAA).** Type: non-contributory, tax-financed old-age benefit; MDB is the closest requested code, although the scheme is not contributory DB. Coverage: Thai citizens aged 60 or older who do not receive a civil-service pension; this includes private-sector, self-employed, informal, and non-employed citizens subject to the eligibility test. Participation: statutory entitlement rather than mandatory contribution or voluntary saving. The benefit is THB600 per month at ages 60–69, THB700 at 70–79, THB800 at 80–89, and THB1,000 at 90 or older. The OECD’s 2024 profile reports a means-tested element for new claimants from 12 August 2023 [28].

The ILO review found that approximately 78% of people aged 60 and over received the OAA administratively in 2019, while survey data suggested about 85%. Its value was below Thailand’s 2019 national poverty line of THB2,763 per month, so broad coverage does not remove adequacy risk [29].

**SSO Article 33 old-age pension.** Type: mandatory partially funded DB. Coverage: employees of covered private enterprises, with exceptions including some seasonal, domestic, agricultural, and one-time contract workers; certain temporary public employees may be routed through SSO rather than civil-service pensions. Self-employed workers are not covered by Article 33 unless they enter through another SSO category. Participation: mandatory for covered employees. The combined SSO contribution rate is 7%, of which 3% is paid by the employee, 3% by the employer, and 1% by government; 0.65 percentage points are allocated to family benefits.

A monthly pension requires 180 months, or 15 years, of contributions and age 55, with employment cessation. The formula pays 20% of the average salary over the final 60 working months for the first 15 years and adds 1.5 percentage points for each additional year. The pensionable earnings ceiling is THB15,000 per month. Under the formula, 30 years produces 42.5% of the pensionable salary base and 40 years produces 57.5%. Fewer than 180 months produces a lump sum [28][30].

**SSO Article 39.** Type: voluntary DB continuation of Article 33. Coverage: individuals who were previously insured under Article 33, including people who move from formal employment into self-employment; it is not an open first-entry scheme for all self-employed workers. Public/private status follows the former Article 33 employment history. Participation: voluntary. The contribution is a flat THB432 per month, calculated as 9% of a THB4,800 base salary, and the Article 33 formula applies. Age 55 remains the minimum retirement age.

**SSO Article 40.** Type: voluntary specified-benefit or lump-sum arrangement, mapped to VDB for the requested table because the benefit options are legally specified even though the accumulation mechanics are DC-like. Coverage: workers outside another mandatory scheme, especially informal and self-employed workers; public/private status depends on whether the worker is outside other mandatory coverage. Participation: voluntary. Options include member contributions of THB100 or THB300 per month with government additions of THB50 or THB150; members can contribute up to THB1,000 per month while the government contribution remains capped at THB150. Benefits are generally a lump sum based on contributions, government support, months, and interest. Retirement age is 60.

**National Savings Fund (NSF).** Type: voluntary funded DC. Coverage: workers outside other mandatory pension schemes, especially farmers, self-employed workers, informal workers, and the unemployed; public or private employees covered by another mandatory scheme are outside the intended target. Participation: voluntary. Annual contributions range from THB50 to THB13,200. Government matching is up to THB600 for ages 15–30, THB960 for ages 31–50, and THB1,200 for ages 51–60. At age 60, savings and interest are converted into a pension calculated as total savings divided by 240, with a minimum of THB600 per month until the account is depleted. Leaving before age 60 returns member contributions and interest but not the government match.

**Employer Provident Funds (PVD).** Type: voluntary occupational DC. Coverage: employees whose employers establish and register a fund; employers may be in the private or public enterprise sphere; self-employed workers are outside employer-sponsored participation. Participation: voluntary and dependent on employer establishment. Employees and employers each contribute between 2% and 15% of salary. Benefits are accumulated contributions plus investment returns less charges, ordinarily paid as a lump sum. The minimum retirement age for tax-privileged withdrawal is 55 [31].

**Civil Service Pensions.** Type: mandatory tax-financed public-sector DB. Coverage: Thai civil servants; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the scheme. Participation: mandatory. Twenty-five years of service produces a pension based on years of service and final salary, or the average final 60 months for entrants since 1997. Ten to fewer than 25 years produces a lump sum. The minimum retirement age is 60.

**Government Pension Fund (GPF).** Type: mandatory public-sector DC with voluntary additional contributions. Coverage: civil servants; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the target. Participation: membership is mandatory for covered civil servants, while employee contributions above the mandatory 3% are voluntary. Government contributions total 5%, and employees can contribute between 3% and 15%. Retirement age is 60, with lump-sum or gradual withdrawal options.

**State enterprise pension arrangements.** Type: named sector-specific occupational retirement arrangements, with DB/DC design and participation linked to employment rules. Coverage: employees of state enterprises; private-sector workers outside the relevant enterprise and self-employed workers are not the stated target. Participation: employment-linked. The ILO review identifies these schemes but does not provide a single formula, minimum service period, or common retirement age.

**Local Government Officers Pension Fund.** Type: named public-sector occupational retirement arrangement, with fund-specific DB/DC parameters. Coverage: local-government officers; private-sector and self-employed workers are outside the target. Participation: employment-linked. The supplied review identifies the fund as an ancillary scheme but does not state a common age or formula.

**Private Teachers Aid Fund.** Type: named occupational arrangement for private-school teachers and related personnel. Coverage: private teachers and eligible education employees; public servants and self-employed workers are outside the stated target. Participation: employment-linked. The supplied evidence does not state a common formula or retirement age.

**Retirement Mutual Funds (RMFs) and Super Savings Funds (SSFs).** Type: voluntary personal investment and retirement-saving products, mapped to VP. Coverage: employed and self-employed individual investors; public/private status depends on the investor’s employment. Participation: voluntary. The ILO review identifies these as tax-deductible investment instruments rather than a single national pension fund, so product-level withdrawal ages and benefit forms vary.

**Proposed National Pension Fund (NPF).** Type: proposed mandatory DC, not operative as an early-2025 current scheme. Coverage in the draft would have included employees of enterprises, with exceptions for groups already in other pension systems. Participation: proposed mandatory. The draft design used a proposed retirement age of 60 and combined employee-employer contributions ultimately reaching 20% of salary. It is kept separate from current tables because a proposal is not an enacted early-2025 entitlement.

**Thailand’s protection signal.** The vesting cliff is unusually consequential. Administrative analysis found that in 2020 only 21% of Article 33 beneficiaries aged 63–77 and 27% aged 56–62 had contributed for more than 180 months. Among older beneficiaries with at least 180 months, estimated benefits ranged from THB1,308 per month in the lowest salary quartile to THB3,143 in the highest, with replacement rates of about 24%–25% [30].

The 15-year threshold interacts with labour mobility, the THB15,000 salary ceiling, and the absence of routine indexation. A worker who switches to Article 39 near retirement can have the final five-year salary base reset to THB4,800. Voluntary Article 40 and NSF participation reaches informal workers but usually produces a small lump sum or a time-limited pension. The ILO’s earlier actuarial scenario projected SSO reserve depletion around 2054 without adjustment, while gradual contribution and retirement-age changes improved the projected balance; that is a modelled scenario based on earlier parameters, not an early-2025 outcome.

### Vietnam

Vietnam’s pension architecture consists of compulsory social insurance, voluntary social insurance, a market-based supplementary retirement tier, and non-contributory old-age assistance. The decisive early-2025 legal fact is that Law No. 41/2024/QH15 had been enacted but was scheduled to take effect on 1 July 2025; the 20-year rule therefore remained the relevant compulsory-insurance threshold during early 2025 [32].

**Compulsory social insurance.** Type: mandatory PAYG DB administered by Viet Nam Social Security. Coverage: public-sector employees, private-sector employees on qualifying contracts, civil servants, public employees, defense and security personnel, guest workers, business-household owners, and specified enterprise or cooperative managers. Participation: mandatory for listed categories. The OECD classifies the current scheme as PAYG DB for public and private workers [33].

Before the July 2025 transition, 20 years of contributions were required for a regular pension. The OECD formula was 45% of insured average monthly covered earnings for the first 20 years for men or 15 years for women, plus 2 percentage points for each additional year, capped at 75%. A February 2025 VSS explainer stated that 20 years produced 45% for men and 55% for women. The salary base is insured average monthly covered earnings, with the OECD model using career earnings rather than only final wages. Fewer than 20 years produced an old-age grant rather than a regular lifetime pension.

Normal retirement in 2025 was 61 years and three months for men and 56 years and eight months for women under normal working conditions. The statutory roadmap raises men’s age by three months per year toward 62 in 2028 and women’s age by four months per year toward 60 in 2035 [34]. Hazardous occupations can retire up to five years earlier and highly skilled workers can work up to five years later under the stated conditions.

**Voluntary social insurance.** Type: voluntary DB, based on the World Bank description of the Voluntary Social Insurance Fund and its DB retirement and survivor structure. Coverage: Vietnamese citizens aged 15 or older outside compulsory insurance, especially self-employed, informal, rural, and household workers. Public/private status: participation is available to citizens outside the compulsory employment categories rather than tied to one sector. Self-employed coverage: yes. Participation: voluntary. Contributions are paid by the individual at 22% of selected flexible income, with monthly, quarterly, semiannual, annual, or one-time payment options under the cited comparison. The World Bank describes voluntary-insurance retirement eligibility as aligned with the current private-sector rules; the age row therefore uses the compulsory 2025 schedule as a clearly identified proxy.

**Supplementary retirement scheme.** Type: voluntary market-based supplementary retirement arrangement, mapped to VO because employer-funded or jointly employer-employee funding is expressly contemplated. Coverage: employees and employers in public or private employment may contribute; self-employed coverage is product-specific rather than established by the statutory definition. Participation: voluntary. Products may be supplied by life insurers, investment funds, or other financial institutions. The law does not impose one DB/DC design or a common retirement age, so the product contract controls.

**Old-age social assistance.** Type: non-contributory public old-age assistance, mapped to MDB only as the closest requested code. Coverage: older Vietnamese citizens meeting age, poverty, household-support, and pension-status conditions; it is not limited to public or private workers and can reach people with no contribution history. Participation: statutory entitlement, not a mandatory contribution. The OECD 2024 profile reports a minimum social pension of VND360,000 and age- and household-condition multipliers. It is a poverty floor rather than an earnings-related pension.

**Vietnam’s protection signal.** VSS expected approximately 20.11 million social-insurance participants by 31 December 2024, equal to 42.71% of the working-age population, including 2.29 million voluntary participants. This is participation, not pension entitlement or replacement-rate coverage. The 20-year pre-July threshold remains the central protection cliff: workers outside continuous formal employment may receive a grant rather than a pension. The scheduled 15-year threshold from 1 July 2025 materially changes that cliff, but it is a post-cutoff reform and is not used to rewrite the early-2025 table.

### Contribution periods create the first protection gap

**The vesting cliff matters more than nominal coverage.** A worker can be legally enrolled yet fail to qualify for a lifetime pension. Indonesia JP, Pakistan EOBI, and Thailand Article 33 each require 15 years. The Philippines SSS requires 120 monthly contributions, equivalent to 10 years, while GSIS requires 15 years. Sri Lanka’s PSPS requires 10 years, but its EPF is a DC account whose balance is paid at exit rather than converted through a DB vesting formula. Vietnam required 20 years before 1 July 2025.

**Lump sums replace insurance.** Below the threshold, Indonesia JP pays a lump sum, EOBI pays an old-age grant, SSS pays contributions plus interest, GSIS pays a separation benefit, and Thailand’s Article 33 and civil-service systems also use lump sums. A lump sum preserves some accumulated value but does not insure against living longer than the account. The ILO’s Thailand review emphasizes that lump sums are commonly depleted early and are particularly problematic for women because women generally live longer [29].

**Interrupted careers are the common counterexample.** A continuously formal worker can pass a 15- or 20-year threshold, but a worker moving between informal work, self-employment, migration, public employment, and private employment may accumulate fragmented credits. Thailand’s administrative evidence is unusually direct: only 21% of older Article 33 beneficiaries aged 63–77 and 27% aged 56–62 exceeded 180 months. The mechanism is not simply low contribution rates; it is the interaction of a 15-year rule with a labour market in which workers move between schemes.

**Public-service thresholds can be even higher.** Thailand’s civil-service pension requires 25 years for a pension and 10–24 years produces a lump sum. A worker with nine years in the civil service may receive no retirement income from that public-service pension, while a worker with 20 years in government service and 14 years in the private sector can fall below the pension threshold in both schemes if credits are not portable. Pakistan’s civil-service threshold of 10 years is less severe, but five to fewer than 10 years still produces gratuity rather than ordinary pension.

**The strongest reform effect would come from smoothing the threshold.** A pro-rata pension or portable credit system would reduce the discontinuity between 14 and 15 years in Indonesia or Thailand and between 19 and 20 years in Vietnam. The conclusion would be weaker if administrative records showed that almost all participants regularly contribute beyond the threshold; the available Thailand evidence points in the opposite direction, while the supplied evidence does not establish equivalent early-2025 contribution distributions for all seven countries.

### Retirement age is an accumulation and payout variable

**Age works twice.** A lower normal age shortens the period for contributions and lengthens the period over which a balance or pension must last. It can improve access for workers in physically demanding jobs, but without a compensating floor or contribution credit it can reduce adequacy.

The earliest general normal ages in the comparison are Sri Lanka’s EPF and PSPS ages of 50 for women and 55 for men, Malaysia’s EPF age of 55, Thailand’s Article 33 age of 55, Indonesia’s JHT age of 56, and Pakistan’s EOBI ages of 55 for women and 60 for men. The Philippines’ general SSS age is 60, while GSIS has age-60 eligibility and age-65 compulsory retirement. Vietnam’s 2025 normal-condition ages are 61 years and three months for men and 56 years and eight months for women.

**Gender differences compound labour-market differences.** Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Vietnam have lower female normal ages. A lower age can be protective when it reflects demanding work or unequal longevity, but it also reduces women’s contribution horizon. The World Bank identifies career interruptions for unpaid care, lower wages, and longer life expectancy as recurring reasons why women receive lower contributory pensions. Earlier female retirement therefore needs to be assessed alongside contribution credits, minimum service, salary bases, and survivor benefits rather than treated as an isolated advantage [1].

**Early retirement can reduce the formula directly.** Pakistan reduces EOBI pensions by 0.5% for each completed month before normal age. A full five-year early window would imply 60 months × 0.5% = a 30% reduction, subject to the scheme’s conditions. Vietnam’s pre-cutoff OECD description applies a 2% reduction for each year before normal age under reduced-working-capacity conditions. Thailand’s Article 33 pension cannot generally be claimed before 55, while Malaysia’s EPF permits Account 2 access at age 50, which is an early withdrawal route rather than necessarily a retirement claim.

**Delayed work does not always produce delayed-income protection.** Indonesia permits deferral without a maximum age in the OECD model, and Malaysia allows continued contributions after the normal EPF age. The Philippines allows later SSS claiming without a stated increment, while Vietnam does not permit starting a pension after normal age even though work and pension receipt can be combined. These distinctions matter: a retirement age is not simply an age at which work must stop, but a point at which benefit eligibility, contribution accumulation, and actuarial adjustment interact.

### Benefit formulas shape the quality of protection

**Salary reference periods redistribute risk.** Indonesia and Vietnam use career-based or career-weighted earnings, which reduce the effect of a late-career salary spike but expose the pension to low early-career wages and long periods outside formal employment. Thailand and the Philippines use the final five years or a five-year window, which can raise benefits for workers with rising wages but can also reward late-career salary concentration. Sri Lanka’s PSPS uses final salary, while Pakistan’s civil-service rules present both average-emoluments and last-pay descriptions.

**Accrual rates are not directly rankable.** Indonesia’s 1% per contribution year is lower than Pakistan EOBI’s estimated 2% and Vietnam’s 2 percentage points after its initial 45% base. But the percentage applies to different salary bases, ceilings, floors, and service thresholds. GSIS combines a 37.5% base with 2.5% for each year above 15 and caps the pension at 90% of average monthly compensation. Thailand gives 20% for the first 15 years and 1.5% thereafter, producing 42.5% after 30 years. Sri Lanka’s reported 85%–90% after 30 years is an observed formula outcome rather than a confirmed annual rate.

**Salary ceilings can turn a strong formula into a weak replacement rate.** Thailand’s THB15,000 ceiling had remained fixed while wages rose; the ILO review found that 36% of Article 33 contributors earned above the ceiling in October 2020. A worker with 30 years and a THB30,000 final salary would receive 42.5% of the THB15,000 pensionable base, or THB6,375, equal to only 21.25% of actual final earnings. Vietnam’s 75% maximum accrual is a percentage ceiling rather than a monetary ceiling, while the Philippines’ 2025 maximum MSC of PHP35,000 limits the earnings input. Indonesia’s JP contribution ceiling was IDR9.1 million per month in the OECD model.

**Minimum benefits determine horizontal protection.** A formula tied to wages may still leave low earners with very small pensions. Indonesia’s JP has a modelled minimum and maximum, EOBI has a PKR10,000 minimum, SSS has PHP1,200 and PHP2,400 minimum branches, and GSIS has PHP1,300 and PHP2,400 statutory floors. Thailand’s OAA provides a broad floor, but THB600–THB1,000 is below the national poverty line. Malaysia’s RM350 targeted assistance at age 60 is a poverty benefit rather than an earnings-related pension. The supplied evidence gives no separate current minimum amount for several public-service or Sri Lankan DB rows, so their formula strength should not be mistaken for a measured national floor.

**Defined contribution transfers risk to the worker.** EPF, JHT, Sri Lankan EPF and ETF, Pakistan GPF and VPS, Philippine WISP and MySSS Pension Booster, Thailand’s GPF, NSF and PVD, and Vietnam’s supplementary products depend on contribution density, account returns, withdrawal rules, charges, and annuitization. The balance can be substantial without creating lifetime income. WISP is a partial counterexample because it converts accumulated assets into an annuity for at least 15 years, but that period is still not necessarily lifetime protection. Malaysia’s EPF permits lump-sum, installment, or combined withdrawals; Sri Lanka’s EPF is paid as a lump sum; Thailand’s GPF and PVD commonly permit lump-sum withdrawal.

**Voluntary schemes rarely repair mandatory gaps on their own.** PRS, DPPK, DPLK, VPS, MySSS Pension Booster, APPFs, NSF, Article 40, and Vietnam’s supplementary retirement tier can improve retirement income for workers who have disposable income and stable access to financial institutions. They are less effective as universal substitutes where workers have irregular earnings. The World Bank’s cross-country review finds that voluntary informal-sector schemes work better when contributions are flexible, digital collection is easy, matching is targeted, and short-term access is balanced against retirement preservation. A voluntary account with low contribution density remains a supplement rather than a replacement for mandatory social insurance.

### Cross-country rule judgments

**Indonesia and Malaysia show opposite funding strengths.** Indonesia has a mandatory DB/DC architecture but reported active participation of only 16.5% of the working-age population and BPJS assets of 3.3% of GDP in 2022. Malaysia’s EPF assets were 65.5% of GDP in the same year, but the EPF is primarily a funded account and can be accessed or depleted without producing a lifetime annuity. Indonesia’s main gap is reach and contribution density; Malaysia’s is conversion of accumulated assets into durable income.

**Pakistan and the Philippines show the cost of separate regimes.** Pakistan’s EOBI protects a limited private-sector establishment population, while civil servants receive a separate DB pension and public employees may also have GPF balances. The Philippines covers more worker categories through SSS and adds WISP, but GSIS remains a separate public-sector regime with a higher formula ceiling and different service rules. In both countries, switching sectors can change the benefit formula and the applicable vesting clock.

**Sri Lanka has the sharpest DB-DC contrast.** A long-serving public employee can receive a PSPS pension tied to final salary and service, while a private-sector employee generally accumulates EPF and ETF balances paid at exit. The difference is not merely the accrual rate: it is the difference between lifetime DB insurance and an account that places longevity risk on the retiree. The lower female retirement age increases that contrast for women.

**Thailand has the clearest evidence of contribution-density failure.** Article 33’s 15-year threshold and final-five-year formula could produce a reasonable statutory replacement rate after a full career, but only a minority of older cohorts had exceeded 180 months. The THB15,000 salary ceiling, no routine indexation, Article 39’s low reference salary, and Article 40’s small lump sums compound the problem. The OAA greatly broadens old-age coverage, but its low value means coverage and adequacy remain separate achievements.

**Vietnam is expanding coverage while retaining a high pre-cutoff threshold.** The 42.71% working-age social-insurance participation estimate at the end of 2024 is higher than the historical participation levels described for several neighboring systems, but it includes voluntary and compulsory insurance and does not show how many people qualify for a lifetime pension. The 20-year rule in force before July 2025 remains a substantial cliff. The scheduled 15-year rule is therefore a material transition, but it belongs to the post-cutoff policy environment.

**No single formula is the “most generous” without a common career.** Comparing 1% in Indonesia, 2% in EOBI, 2.5% above 15 in GSIS, 42.5% after 30 years in Thailand, and 45% plus 2 percentage points in Vietnam is not a valid ranking without harmonizing earnings histories, ceilings, indexation, gender, contribution density, retirement age, and payout duration. The conditions that would change the judgment are a common actuarial career simulation and current scheme-level data on actual contribution histories and pension receipt.

**The evidence boundary is substantive.** The OECD country profiles published on 6 December 2024 are modelled comparisons, while some Sri Lankan PSPS and PSPF parameters come from an older ILO review and some Thai ancillary schemes are named without current product rules. The early-2025 analysis excludes Vietnam’s 15-year rule before its 1 July 2025 effective date and excludes the Philippines’ 2026 PERA FAQ from operative rules. Values marked as estimates are arithmetic derivations from stated anchors—EOBI’s 1 ÷ 50, Pakistan’s 70% ÷ 30 years, and Sri Lanka’s 85%–90% ÷ 30 years—while provider-specific cells remain qualitative because the supplied material gives no common provider-level parameter.

**Bottom line.** Across the seven countries, retirement savings protection is strongest when a worker remains continuously in a covered formal or public-sector career and reaches the required service threshold. It is weakest for informal workers, self-employed workers, women with interrupted careers, and workers who move between public, private, and voluntary schemes. The most consequential rule changes are therefore those that reduce vesting cliffs, preserve portability, raise or index pensionable earnings ceilings, convert DC balances into lifetime income, and provide a credible minimum floor alongside mandatory contributions.

**Evidence conventions and unresolved cells**

The working cutoff is 31 March 2025. The World Bank inventory cautions that a country may have several programs of different types, including legacy programs closed to new entrants but still paying existing retirees; it defines DB as defined benefit, DC as defined contribution, and NDC as notional defined contribution [48]. Accordingly, an inventory classification is not treated as proof of a complete statutory formula. Missing accrual rates, salary bases, benefit floors, ceilings, service periods, or ages remain “not reported” or “requires verification,” never zero. Contribution measures also operate differently: in DB or PAYG schemes they affect the pension agency’s financing, whereas in funded schemes they directly affect participants’ future pensions [48].

**Sri Lanka**

Sri Lanka’s **Public Servant’s Pension Scheme (PSPS)** is a public-sector, PAYG DB scheme covering civil servants, armed forces, government-school teachers, local-government employees, and eligible widows, widowers, and orphans of public servants [54]. **Vietnam**

The Vietnamese Social Security Agency is described as managing **five compulsory social-insurance funds** and a **Voluntary Social Insurance Fund (VSIF)**, with the arrangements classified as DB. VSIF covers old-age and survivorship benefits; participants choose a monthly income subject to a minimum and pay the entire contribution themselves because they have no registered employer. The cited contribution rate is **22% of salary** [48]. VSIF contributors rose from **563,776 in December 2019** to **1,917,000 in December 2023**, but coverage was still only **around 4% of the labor force in December 2023** [48]. The latter is the available Vietnam-specific coverage indicator; it predates the cutoff and is not a complete measure of all pension coverage.

The source notes that flexible contribution timing and amounts, early-withdrawal options, fiscal incentives, and matching contributions can improve voluntary-scheme participation among informal workers. Vietnam is identified as offering matching contributions for certain income groups, although the subsidy size and eligible-member pool are not supplied [48]. No scheme-specific normal retirement age is reported for the compulsory or voluntary arrangement in this evidence. **Formula-comparison conventions and date status**

The OECD comparator is a dated, modelled benchmark rather than an early-2025 legal register. It was published on 6 December 2024, but its pension parameters reflect 2022; this qualification applies to Indonesia, Malaysia, Pakistan, the Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Vietnam alike.[86]

The OECD model assumes entry at age 22, uninterrupted full-career earnings until the applicable normal retirement age, and separate cases for 50%, 100% and 200% of average earnings.[86] Its country comparisons should therefore not be read as observed contribution histories. The report itself cautions that national pension systems are difficult to compare because their institutional, technical and legal details differ.[86] The cited report structure first identifies the schemes and their rules, then presents indicators and detailed country background; it combines modelling with information supplied and reviewed by national pension experts.[86]

**Thailand: mandatory defined-benefit formula evidence**

The public-sector rows also require care. The post-1997 arrangement combined a less generous DB formula with GPF accumulation; the new DB formula was expected to reduce lifetime DB benefits by about 10% relative to the old formula, while the GPF supplied additional retirement income.[97] Central employees who joined after 1997 were required to enter the new arrangement; pre-1997 employees could switch and, after the 2008 financial crisis, some were allowed to return to the DB arrangement. Voluntary GPF contributions of up to 15% of salary were permitted in the described arrangement.[97] The source describes GPF as a hybrid DB/DC arrangement with a target replacement rate and one administrator, so GPF should not be treated as a standalone mandatory DB formula row.[97]

**Thailand: normal retirement-age entries**

The source’s age-60 public/private-age-55 distinction is used in an analytical comparison, and the cited retirement-rules table reports a 1% annual late-retirement reward for male workers.[97] The same source says early retirement was not possible in the arrangement it describes.[97] A proposed long-run reform would gradually raise retirement age to 65, introduce actuarially fair early-retirement reductions, move to lifetime-average earnings, index pensions to prices and index the SSF earnings ceiling to wage growth; these are recommendations, not rules shown as effective by 31 March 2025.[97]

**Thailand: contribution continuity, benefit adequacy and protection gap**

The 15-year SSF threshold is a decisive entitlement condition. In the source’s 2017 observation, only 119,000 of 443,875 SSF retirees received pensions; \(119{,}000 / 443{,}875 \approx 26.8\%\), leaving approximately 73.2% receiving lump sums rather than pensions.[97] The source attributes delayed or absent pension entitlement to partial and irregular contribution histories. Its replacement-rate simulations assume contributions throughout the entire working life and therefore represent an upper bound for workers with interrupted histories.[97] The statutory 180-month rule is equivalent to 15 years, but the requirement is contribution months, not merely time spent in employment.[157]

The OAA mitigates legal non-coverage but does not remove the adequacy gap. The source reports age-based monthly amounts of Bt600 for ages 60–69, Bt700 for 70–79, Bt800 for 80–89 and Bt1,000 for age 90 or older, alongside an average benefit of approximately Bt7,900 per year as of 2019 compared with a poverty line of approximately Bt27,300.[97] It describes the OAA as varying by age, with an average level of about 3.3% of GDP per capita and less than 9% of household consumption; the benefit alone was insufficient to prevent poverty.[97] Program-specific coverage figures also differ: one passage reports SSF coverage of around 75% of the elderly, while another uses approximately 82% coverage for OAA recipients aged 60 and over.[97] These are not interchangeable denominators.

**Indonesia**

BPJS Jaminan Pensiun (JP) is also described as a DB arrangement with statutory minimum and maximum limits. For workers below 60, the employee rate is **11%**, while the employer rate is **12%** above RM5,000 and **13%** at or below RM5,000. **Thailand**

Thailand’s documented architecture comprises the tax-funded, age-banded Old Age Allowance for people aged **60+**; primarily non-contributory civil-servant DB pensions under the Government Pension Fund with a maximum replacement rate of **70%** plus a DC lump sum; a partially funded DB Social Security Fund (SSF) mandatory for private firms with at least one worker and some public employees; occupational funds mandatory for listed-company employees but otherwise voluntary, with contributions of **2–15%**; a mandatory DC National Pension Fund with employers and workers each contributing **3%**; and the fully funded, voluntary DC National Savings Fund for workers outside other funds, including the self-employed [92]. The SSF DB formula requires **15 contribution years**, has pensionable age **55**, uses `(20% + 1.5% × (CY − 15)) × BS`, and applies a five-year non-indexed average wage; its minimum and maximum benefits are inferred from minimum earnings rather than directly reported [92]. The NSF and Old Age Allowance age entries are **60**; other scheme-specific ages are not reported here. SSF active contributors were **33.6% of the working-age population in 2015**; contributory old-age recipients were **8.2% of people over 60 in 2016**, versus **74.8%** receiving the Old Age Allowance. Benefits were not automatically indexed, and the **THB15,000** contribution ceiling had not changed since 1999 [92].


Vietnam’s architecture includes a tax-funded pension for people aged 80+ and a means-tested benefit for poor people aged 60–79 without support, a pre-1995 civil-servant pension being phased out, a separate veteran merit allowance, mandatory social insurance for public and private workers with contracts of at least one month and other listed worker groups, and voluntary social insurance for uncovered people such as the self-employed. In 2018–20, voluntary contributions were subsidised by **30%, 25% or 10%**, corresponding to **VND46,200, VND38,500 or VND15,400 per month** [92].

**Malaysia: rule evidence and protection-gap mechanism**

Malaysia’s documented early-2025 coverage gap is substantial: only 60 of every 100 people were covered by a formal retirement scheme; about 50 were under the Employees Provident Fund (EPF), about 10 under the civil-service pension scheme, and 40—largely informal-sector workers—had no retirement coverage [135]. This is a formal-coverage measure, not a direct measure of benefit adequacy.

The **Public Service Pension Scheme (Skim Pencen Perkhidmatan Awam)** is a statutory public-sector pension arrangement under Akta Pencen 1980 (Act 227), applying to specified officers under the New Scheme throughout Malaysia [254]. The supplied service guidance covers permanent Federal and State public-service officers and employees of statutory bodies and local authorities; eligible new appointees choose the Pension Scheme or KWSP before confirmation in post [60]. Thus, public-service DB design and public-sector coverage are supported, but the evidence does not establish a single mandatory status for every public-service category. EPF/KWSP is identified as a DC arrangement primarily serving private-sector workers; the supplied January 2025 evidence reports active EPF contributors at 51.1% of workers and public-service pension coverage at 6.6%, with smaller groups in private schemes and LTAT [135]. LTAT is therefore a relevant retirement-scheme candidate, but its mandate, age and benefit formula remain unresolved in these cards [135].

The adequacy evidence explains the mechanism. MARS reports that **25.5% of Malaysians aged 60 and older had no monthly income**—22.6% at ages 60–69, 28.3% at 70–79 and 41.3% at 80+—using evidence reproduced in the January 2025 bulletin [135]. A related MARS statement rounds the result to a quarter without retirement income and says about half of such seniors relied on family, especially children [135]. A Malay-language excerpt reports a different population measure: **47.5% of retired people aged 65+ had no income source**, while fewer than 60% of workers were protected by a retirement scheme; these denominators and measures should not be treated as identical [135]. Separately, BNM reports that only **19.5% of people aged 60+ received old-age benefits in 2021**, and warns that inadequate Pillar 0 support and the absence of Pillar 1 leave private-sector retirees exposed when Pillar 2 savings are insufficient [137].
