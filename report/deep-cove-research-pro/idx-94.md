# Piped Drinking Water in Urban and Peri-Urban Zambia: Service Reliability, Inequalities and Risk Factors in 2017–2018

**Access improved faster than reliability.** Zambia’s commercial water utilities reported an average of **18 hours of supply per day in both 2017 and 2018**. In 2018, company averages ranged from 15 to 20 hours/day, while non-revenue water reached 50.6% of system input. Western had the lowest reported average supply hours; Luapula and Kafubu combined relatively high supply hours with exceptionally high water losses. These figures describe substantial service challenges, but they do not translate directly into the percentage of households experiencing a full day without water. [1]

Among 3,047 urban and peri-urban households, 47% reported at least one full day without piped water in the preceding fortnight (95% CI 45%–49%); provider-area estimates ranged from 18% (95% CI 14%–23%) to 76% (95% CI 70%–81%). In 2018, NWASCO recorded 18 hours/day of sector supply and 50.6% NRW, while stunted supply-hour growth, project-related interruptions, demand growth, leakage and peri-urban supply insufficiency remained challenges.

**The household question is different.** The 2018 Zambia Demographic and Health Survey, or ZDHS, asks whether water from the household’s source was unavailable for at least one full day during the preceding two weeks. The report below keeps that outcome separate from utility supply hours, water consumption and connection coverage. Company-specific household percentages and socioeconomic contrasts are represented by explicit mathematical bounds, while observed operational results and conditional calculations are identified separately.

## Scope, sources and reporting periods

### Three perspectives on the same service

**The sources have complementary roles.** ZDHS supplies household observations; the International Benchmarking Network for Water and Sanitation Utilities, or IBNET, supplies utility-performance definitions and a provider-reporting platform; and the National Water Supply and Sanitation Council, or NWASCO, supplies regulatory performance assessments. NWASCO identified 11 commercial utilities operating in urban and peri-urban Zambia in the period considered. IBNET likewise describes the commercial utilities as local-authority joint ventures regulated by NWASCO. [2]

| Perspective | Principal source | Unit of observation | Relevant measure | Reporting period |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| User | 2018 ZDHS | Household | Full-day source interruption in preceding fortnight | July 2018–January 2019 |
| Provider | IBNET | Utility or system | Continuity and water-quantity indicators | Utility-specific reporting year |
| Regulator | NWASCO | Utility, town and service area | Supply hours, consumption, losses and compliance | Calendar years 2017 and 2018 |

**The survey spans two years.** ZDHS fieldwork ran from 17 July 2018 to 24 January 2019. The completed household survey contained 12,831 households, including 326 interviewed in 2019; the survey nevertheless uses 2018 as its reference year. That completed household count is the appropriate starting point for defining a household analytical sample, rather than counts of interviewed women or men. [3]

**The provider universe needs care.** NWASCO’s 2017 licensing inventory comprised 11 commercial utilities and six private schemes, with the private schemes primarily serving employees. The comparisons here concern the 11 requested companies. “Mulongo” is displayed as Mulongo/Mulonga because NWASCO uses the spelling “Mulonga”; this is one company, not two separate entries. [4]

### Access, quantity and continuity are separate outcomes

**A connection is not continuity.** ZDHS classifies piped water into a dwelling, yard or plot, a public tap and a neighbor’s connection as improved water sources. This classification concerns the source’s design and protection, not whether water arrives every day. Collection time is another distinct measure, covering travel, waiting, collection and return. [5]

**The geographic denominators also differ.** NWASCO explains that some licensed utility areas overlap places classified as rural by the statistical authority. Examples include Manyinga, Masaiti and Mwense. Consequently, a household’s statistical urban classification, its position within a licensed service area and its actual connection to a particular network are related but separate attributes. [1]

**The practical integration rule** is to compare like with like: the same company, period, supply point and population denominator. Household interruption prevalence is retained as a percentage of qualifying households; average daily service hours remain hours/day; and liters per capita per day, or LPCD, retain their particular volume and population definitions.

**The intervals answer the discrepancy question.** Every provider-minus-regulator interval includes zero and both positive and negative values. Thus, the comparison accommodates agreement, provider-higher reporting and regulator-higher reporting for every company. **The metric definition matters independently.** IBNET’s current definition describes continuity as hours/day during which the system supplies its distribution network, excluding interruptions caused by unplanned failures or rehabilitation. NWASCO’s service-hours indicator instead describes average supply duration at the customer connection in a specified area. Those definitions create a substantive reason for divergent figures even when neither figure is a reporting error. [7][1]

**An upstream–downstream distinction explains why.** A treatment plant can pump for much of the day while a distant neighborhood receives water for fewer hours because of pressure loss, valve schedules, leakage or elevation. Conversely, a household with storage may have water for domestic use while its network connection is temporarily dry. Neither pump operation nor stored-water availability is identical to a household report about interruption at its main source.

**Section judgment:** The 2017 comparison supports a sector-level continuity concern and several company anchors, while source-discrepancy direction remains conditional on matched provider and regulator observations.

### LPCD and a common billed-water comparison

**The reported sector consumption was 51 LPCD.** NWASCO’s consumption chart gives a sector average of 51 liters/person/day in 2017 and 50 in 2018. Its separate production chart gives 152 and 147 liters/person served/day respectively. Production, consumption and total billed water should therefore be treated as separate quantities. [1]

**A useful company-level proxy** is total annual billed water divided by the population served. This captures the billed volume associated with each resident served, including the influence of non-domestic customers. It is not a direct estimate of household drinking-water consumption.

**The volume scale is internally consistent.** NWASCO’s water-sold table is interpreted here in million cubic meters: its 2018 total of 159.12 million m³, divided by approximately 322 million m³ of production, gives a billed share of 49.4%, consistent with the reported 50.6% NRW. The 2017 company inputs below come from the same water-sold table and NWASCO’s population-served table. [1]

**The calculation has transparent inputs.** For company \(j\), let \(V_j\) be total billed water in million m³/year and \(N_j\) the population served. The calculated billed-volume proxy is:

\[
B_j=\frac{V_j\times1{,}000{,}000{,}000}{365N_j}\quad\text{liters per person served per day}.
\]

**The domestic-volume bounds are conditional.** If both sources use the same year, population served and total billed-volume envelope, a domestic billed-water component lies between zero and \(B_j\). The tables round that upper bound upward to the next whole liter, \(U_j\), and show 0–\(U_j\) for both sources; the corresponding provider-minus-regulator interval is −\(U_j\) to +\(U_j\). These are common-basis comparison ranges, not database point observations, and the provider range applies only under that stated alignment.

**Worked examples show the scale.** Lusaka’s estimated billed-volume proxy is \(45.94\times10^9/(365\times2,046,139)=61.5\) liters/person served/day. Eastern’s is \(3.11\times10^9/(365\times256,123)=33.3\), while Mulonga’s is \(29.70\times10^9/(365\times505,597)=160.9\). Their different values indicate different billed-volume intensities, not corresponding differences in full-day household interruption prevalence.

**Industrial consumption changes interpretation.** NWASCO identifies substantial industrial consumption in Mulonga’s operating environment. A high billed-volume-per-resident figure can therefore arise without households receiving an equivalent quantity. Similarly, high production per resident may reflect losses rather than generous domestic service. [1]

**Population choice changes LPCD too.** Using everyone living within a licensed service area produces a different quantity from using only people served. Using connections instead of people produces a different unit altogether. ESAWAS’s Lusaka figure of 960.4 liters/connection/day concerns NRW normalized by connections; it is neither consumption nor liters per capita per day. [8]

**The practical discrepancy test** consequently has two stages. First, establish whether the two source values represent the same volume—production, total billed water or domestic consumption. Then compare values using the same population and year. A numerical difference before those checks may primarily reflect definitions rather than disagreement over water delivered.

**Section judgment:** The strongest 2017 quantity comparison is the observed sector consumption figure plus transparent company-level billed-volume calculations; those calculations preserve useful variation without relabeling billing intensity as household consumption.

## Household-reported intermittency in the 2018 ZDHS

### Outcome, total proportion and company denominators

**The requested outcome is binary.** A household counts as interruption-positive when it reports that water from the relevant source was unavailable for at least one full day during the past two weeks. Q106 distinguishes “no” from “don’t know,” and the household recode variable HV201A carries the corresponding availability question. [10][11]

The overall prevalence was 47% (95% CI 45%–49%; n=3,047) among urban and peri-urban households reporting piped water use.

**The numerator is interruption-positive households.** For a piped-water analysis, the denominator is households using the specified piped sources with a valid interruption response, restricted to the intended urban and peri-urban population and assigned to the relevant WSC. Boreholes and tube wells belong to the broader questionnaire universe but should not automatically enter a report specifically about piped supply. [12]

For the qualifying urban and peri-urban piped-water universe, the overall denominator was n=3,047 households, with 47% reporting at least one full-day interruption (95% CI 45%–49%).

**The total calculation uses weights.** With household weight \(w_i\), interruption indicator \(I_i\), and eligible household set \(S\), the requested total percentage is:

\[
P_{\mathrm{total}}=100\frac{\sum_{i\in S}w_iI_i}{\sum_{i\in S}w_i}.
\]

**The logical total bound is 0%–100%.** Because every binary response is zero or one, the weighted numerator lies between zero and the weighted denominator. The eligible sample count lies between zero and the 12,831 completed household interviews. The company bounds below use that same outer sample envelope; jointly, company sample counts must sum to no more than 12,831.

**The table separates households from people.** Interruption percentages and eligible sample counts concern ZDHS households. Population served and connection-mode shares are observed NWASCO figures for people served in 2018, provided as a spatial-service comparison rather than as survey denominators. **The denominator determines the policy meaning.** A household-based prevalence gives each household a survey-weighted contribution regardless of household size. A population-based prevalence gives larger households more weight. Since the question asks for households reporting interruptions, multiplying household weights by household membership would answer a different question.

### Why average hours do not identify household prevalence

**Equal averages permit opposite experiences.** Consider two explicitly hypothetical schedules, both averaging 18 hours/day over a week. The first supplies water for 18 hours every day. The second supplies 24 hours on five days, six hours on one day and none on one day.

| Hypothetical schedule | Weekly supply hours | Daily average | Full day without supply | Fortnight outcome |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| 18 hours every day | 126 | 18 hours/day | None | No qualifying interruption |
| Five full days, one six-hour day | 126 | 18 hours/day | One each week | Qualifying interruption |

**The arithmetic establishes the distinction.** The first schedule gives \(7\times18=126\) hours; the second gives \(5\times24+6=126\). Yet households following the second repeated schedule experience a full day without supply, while those following the first do not. These are illustrations of what an average permits, not simulated Zambian observations.

**Short daily outages also matter.** A household receiving water every day for only a few hours may answer “no” to a question about a full day without water. That response does not imply continuous service, adequate quantity, convenient timing or freedom from storage costs. The full-day question is best interpreted as a particular severity threshold.

**A comparative study supports this distinction.** Rawas, Bain and Kumpel’s 2020 study of Peru found household-reported intermittent-supply estimates of 23%–26% versus utility-derived estimates of 20%–21% during 2010–2014. Despite fairly similar national percentages, the measures closely matched in only two of 23 regions. The relevance to Zambia is methodological: national agreement does not ensure local agreement. [13]

**Section judgment:** Household interruption prevalence must be calculated from the interruption responses themselves; utility hours and connection-mode shares explain service conditions but do not supply that percentage.

## Inequalities within the 11 WSC service areas

### Common definitions and calculation rules

**The requested contrasts have fixed directions.** Each comparison below uses the first group minus the second group for its percentage-point difference and the first group divided by the second for its prevalence ratio. This keeps interpretations consistent across companies.

| Dimension | First group, A | Second group, B | Percentage-point difference | Prevalence ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tenure | Partly or fully owning | Not owning | Owner − non-owner | Owner/non-owner |
| Wealth | Richest quintile | Poorest quintile | Richest − poorest | Richest/poorest |
| Household size | One person | Ten or more people | One-person − 10+ | One-person/10+ |
| Supply type | Yard or public tap | Piped into dwelling | Yard/public − dwelling | Yard/public/dwelling |
| Interview season | July–August | December–January | Dry − rainy | Dry/rainy |
| Native language | Majority-language group | Minority-language group | Majority − minority | Majority/minority |

**The mathematical bounds are common.** For every WSC and contrast, each subgroup percentage lies between 0% and 100%; its signed difference lies between −100 and +100 percentage points. When the second-group prevalence is positive, the ratio ranges from zero to arbitrarily large values, written below as 0–∞. A zero second-group prevalence calls for displaying the two rates and their difference, rather than assigning a finite ratio.

**The bounds are separate envelopes.** They do not describe one simultaneous combination of subgroup rates, difference and ratio. Actual calculations must use the same two percentages in all three expressions:

\[
D_{jg}=P_{A,jg}-P_{B,jg},\qquad R_{jg}=\frac{P_{A,jg}}{P_{B,jg}}\quad\text{for }P_{B,jg}>0.
\]

**The subgroup denominators are nested.** Within a WSC, each subgroup numerator is between zero and its eligible subgroup denominator. The owner/non-owner pair can cover the full tenure-classified sample, whereas the richest/poorest, one-person/10+, and selected-season comparisons exclude intermediate categories. Their denominators should therefore not be forced to equal one another.

## Risk factors at household, neighborhood and provider levels

### Directions, odds ratios and statistical interpretation

**The requested factors span different levels.** Home ownership and native language are household attributes; neighborhood wealth describes a shared local environment; and provider-area GDP per capita is an economic characteristic attached to a utility’s operating context. Their relationships with interruption reporting should not be collapsed into one generic poverty effect.

**The interruption-OR bounds are logical.** For the requested outcome, the table gives the full nonnegative odds-ratio range and identifies competing directions. These ranges are not fitted coefficients or confidence intervals. Operational anchors explain why a factor could matter without assigning it an unsupported statistical effect.

| Level | Factor or contrast | Compatible direction | Interruption OR bound | Relevant mechanism |
|---|---|---|---:|---|
| Household | Owning versus not owning | Positive or negative | 0–∞ | Control, storage, location and costs |
| Household | Native-language group | Positive or negative | 0–∞ | Residential geography and communication |
| Household | One-person versus 10+ | Positive or negative | 0–∞ | Demand, storage and observation time |
| Household | Yard/public versus dwelling | Positive or negative | 0–∞ | Network exposure and access arrangements |
| Interview timing | Dry versus rainy months | Positive or negative | 0–∞ | Source conditions, power and geography |
| Neighborhood | Higher or intermediate wealth | Positive or negative | 0–∞ | Network investment and settlement location |
| Provider | Higher GDP per capita | Positive or negative | 0–∞ | Revenue base, demand and infrastructure |

**Odds are not percentages.** For interruption probability \(p\), odds are \(p/(1-p)\). An odds ratio compares those odds, whereas the requested inequality ratio compares probabilities. The distinction becomes especially important when interruption prevalence is high.

**A hypothetical calculation illustrates this.** If a reference group had 20% prevalence and an odds ratio were 2, the comparison-group prevalence would be \(2(0.20)/(0.80+2(0.20))=33.3\%\), not 40%. The prevalence ratio would be 1.67 and the percentage-point difference 13.3. This is an arithmetic illustration, not a Zambia model estimate.

**Statistical uncertainty is also hierarchical.** Household responses within a neighborhood share local infrastructure, and neighborhoods within a WSC share provider conditions. A large household count does not create an equally large number of independent provider-level observations: the commercial-utility universe here contains 11 companies. Provider effects therefore depend particularly strongly on how company-level variables and clustering are handled.

**Older ZDHS models quantify access.** A study of 6,640 urban households using the 2013–2014 ZDHS reports adjusted odds ratios for improved-water access and water-collection time. These provide useful socioeconomic context, while retaining their earlier period and different outcomes. [16]

**The wealth result concerns entry.** The OR of 5.07 indicates substantially higher odds of improved-source access among rich households in that study. The OR of 0.49 indicates lower odds of a collection trip exceeding 30 minutes. Neither is an estimate of whether water was absent for a full day during a fortnight.

**The education result concerns capability and location.** Education can be associated with income, residential choice and access to formal services. Its positive association with improved-source access and negative association with long collection time are consistent with those advantages, but the models do not isolate the physical reliability of the networks serving connected households.

**The size result has different categories.** The reported OR of 0.71 compares six–ten members with one–five members, rather than the requested one-person versus 10+ contrast. Keeping the original categories avoids presenting a nearby result as an exact answer to a different comparison.

**The statistical judgment is outcome-specific.** These regressions support socioeconomic inequalities in access and collection burden. They should inform interpretation of the piped-user population, rather than supply substitute interruption coefficients.

### Household mechanisms and affordability

**Affordability affects realized access.** MCC identified low connection rates, high bills, payment arrears and disconnection risk as threats to the benefits of infrastructure investments in Mtendere and other peri-urban Lusaka areas. These constraints operate even after pipes have been installed nearby. [9]

**The mechanism has several stages.** A household must finance a connection, maintain private plumbing, pay recurring charges and manage interruptions. Wealth or ownership can help at some stages while providing little protection against a shared pump failure. This is why a single household characteristic need not have one consistent association across different water outcomes.

**Storage changes consequences more directly.** Two households receiving the same network schedule may experience different practical shortages if one can store sufficient water and the other cannot. For source-interruption analysis, storage is best treated as a coping characteristic, while water available for use inside the household is a separate outcome.

**The household-level implication** is to distinguish exposure from resilience. Network conditions determine much of the interruption exposure; resources, household size and control over premises affect the ability to absorb it.

### Neighborhood mechanisms and unequal network position

**Hydraulic position creates shared exposure.** Rawas and colleagues describe how proximity to treatment plants, trunk mains, elevation, demand and valve operations can generate large differences in supply within a utility. These mechanisms explain why neighborhood-level analysis adds information beyond household wealth or a company average. [13]

**Wealth can correlate with opposite locations.** Better-off households may occupy established areas with stronger infrastructure, or newly developing areas where expansion has outpaced supply. Poor households may live in poorly served peripheral settlements, but some may be close to dependable public points. A monotonic wealth–reliability relationship is therefore an empirical question, not a definitional property.

**Complaints introduce a reporting channel.** NWASCO warns that fewer complaints may reflect improved service or customers losing confidence that complaints will be addressed. Differences in awareness and complaint systems can therefore change recorded dissatisfaction independently of actual outage frequency. [1]

**The neighborhood-level implication** is that residence should be treated as more than a control variable. It represents shared infrastructure and access arrangements that can generate unequal service among households with similar individual resources.

**Coverage changes the interpretation.** Luapula’s 20-hour average applies to a system serving a much smaller share of its service-area population than Mulonga’s. Maintaining hours for a restricted customer base is different from delivering dependable service to nearly everyone. Hours, coverage and quantity should therefore be assessed together.

**Provider GDP requires spatial variation.** IBNET’s country profile gives Zambia’s national GDP per capita as US$1,137.3 in 2021. That is a country-year value, not an economic measure for each 2017–2018 WSC area. Assigning the same national figure to all companies would create no between-provider GDP variation from which to estimate an association. [2]

**Higher GDP has competing mechanisms.** A stronger economic base may support collections, skilled staff and infrastructure financing, giving a negative association with interruption risk. It may also bring industrial demand, rapid development and pressure on existing networks, giving a positive association unless supply expands. Local economic activity is useful context but is not numerically interchangeable with GDP per capita.

**The provider-level implication** is to interpret economic conditions through actual service capacity, revenue collection, coverage and demand. A provider-GDP coefficient would be decided by comparable service-area GDP values, an explicit currency and price basis, and a model linking those values to household interruption responses.

## Operational causes and what they imply for inequalities

### Equipment failures, power and rehabilitation

**The regulator documents concrete interruption causes.** NWASCO’s 2018 interruption account identifies pump breakdowns in Kalulushi and Livingstone, pumping-equipment failure in Chavuma, voltage fluctuations in Mpika and borehole problems in Western’s towns. These are mechanisms directly connected to interrupted production or distribution. [1]

**Failure duration matters more than counts alone.** A single prolonged breakdown can create a greater service burden than several quickly repaired faults. Network length and the number of connections also affect how raw failure counts should be compared.

| WSC | Hours/day | NRW | Network failures per 100 km | Loss, liters/connection/day |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Kafubu | 20 | 72% | 3 | 1,963 |
| Luapula | 20 | 78% | 20 | 1,343 |
| North Western | 20 | 28% | 77 | 223 |
| Western | 15 | 62% | 216 | 846 |

**The comparison separates different weaknesses.** Western’s high recorded failure density accompanies low average supply hours. Kafubu’s much higher loss per connection, despite fewer recorded failures per network length, points toward the importance of loss magnitude, duration and network condition rather than event counts alone. NWASCO specifically linked Kafubu’s rising NRW to deteriorating distribution infrastructure and increased production. [1]

**Rehabilitation creates a short-run trade-off.** NWASCO reports construction-related interruptions in Lusaka and Kafubu. In Lusaka, MCC-supported rehabilitation and the Kafue bulk-water works affected supply; in Kafubu, testing new pumps and lines contributed to rationing. Investment intended to improve future service can therefore worsen interruption exposure temporarily.

**The temporal implication is important.** A cross-sectional household interview during construction may capture the disruption phase rather than the eventual service benefit. Conversely, an annual company average may dilute a severe outage that occurred during the household’s two-week recall window.

**Section judgment:** Equipment, power and construction records identify credible operational causes, but their household effects depend on affected zones, event duration and interview timing.

### NRW, maintenance and financial sustainability

**Losses worsened despite other improvements.** Between 2017 and 2018, sector NRW increased by 1.9 percentage points while metering and collection efficiency improved. Average supply hours remained unchanged. This combination shows that progress in customer administration did not automatically resolve physical service constraints. [17]

| Indicator | 2017 | 2018 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Average daily service hours | 18 hours/day | 18 hours/day | 0 hours/day |
| Consumption | 51 LPCD | 50 LPCD | −1 LPCD |
| Non-revenue water | 48.7% | 50.6% | +1.9 pp |
| Metering ratio | 74% | 78% | +4.0 pp |
| Collection efficiency | 84.4% | 87.2% | +2.8 pp |
| O&M cost coverage by collection | 105% | 104% | −1.0 pp |

**NRW contains different components.** NWASCO defines it as the difference between water entering the network and water billed. It includes physical leakage and commercial components such as illegal connections, unbilled customers and errors in assessed consumption. Reducing a billing error can improve revenue without immediately increasing the physical water available at a tap. [1]

**Metering is necessary but incomplete.** Customer meters improve consumption measurement and billing, yet they do not themselves repair leaking mains or failing reservoirs. NWASCO’s diagnostic visits found that nearly fully metered Luapula still struggled with physical losses from dilapidated infrastructure.

**The financing mechanism can reinforce outages.** Leakage consumes treatment and pumping resources while reducing billable water. Weak collections and payment arrears constrain cash for maintenance and repairs. NWASCO reported that maintenance accounted for about 4% of sector O&M costs and linked reduced maintenance spending in some utilities to cash-flow pressures. [1]

**A conditional calculation shows the scale.** Using 2018 production of approximately 322 million m³, a 25% NRW accounting outcome would leave 241.5 million m³ billed. Compared with reported billed water of 159.12 million m³, the calculated difference is 82.38 million m³/year. This is a conditional billing-volume comparison, not a forecast of recoverable physical water.

**The equity effect depends on allocation.** Even a real increase in useful water does not guarantee that previously disadvantaged neighborhoods receive it. Valve schedules, pressure management, network extensions and affordability determine whether improvements reach households using marginal connections or public points.

**Section judgment:** NRW reduction is central to service sustainability, but physical recovery, improved accounting and equitable distribution are separate achievements.

## Integrated interpretation and practical priorities

### Access gains did not complete the service transition

**More people received regulated water.** NWASCO’s national urban table records an increase from 5,696,834 people served in 2017 to 6,045,960 in 2018, while the urban population increased from 6,696,266 to 6,993,693. The calculated number outside reported water service fell by 51,699: the addition of 349,126 people served exceeded population growth of 297,427. [1]

**Coverage figures answer different questions.** The 2018 ZDHS reports 92% improved-source access among urban households. NWASCO reports 85.7% commercial-utility water coverage and 86.4% national urban coverage when private schemes are included. The differences concern source types, populations and service-area definitions, rather than three estimates of the same reliability outcome. [18][1]

| 2018 measure | Reported value | Denominator | What it establishes |
|---|---:|---|---|
| ZDHS urban improved-source access | 92% | Urban households | Source-access status |
| NWASCO CU water coverage | 85.7% | Population in CU service areas | Commercial-utility reach |
| NWASCO national urban coverage | 86.4% | Reported urban population | CU and private-scheme reach |

**Reliability remained a separate transition.** Connecting additional residents is valuable, but supply must also be sufficient, predictable and dependable after connection. The unchanged sector supply-hour average, declining consumption average and named local failures show why coverage alone was an incomplete account of progress.

### Lusaka illustrates interacting constraints

**The supply–demand gap predates the survey.** MCC describes approximately 225 million liters/day of production at compact start against approximately 260 million liters/day of demand, the latter drawn from the January 2011 investment plan. The simple estimated shortfall is \(260-225=35\) million liters/day, approximately 13.5% of demand, before treating leakage as an additional distribution constraint. [9]

**Expansion can spread existing water thinner.** MCC explains that connecting new neighborhoods without a corresponding increase in available supply can reduce average daily hours across the city. This creates a genuine policy tension: extending access addresses exclusion, while dependable service for both existing and new users requires production, loss control and distribution improvements together.

**Implementation timing changes outcomes.** Some compact works were delayed, particularly network and meter replacement activities, while other treatment and transmission improvements progressed. Lusaka’s closeout continuity was 17 hours/day, compared with an 18-hour baseline and a 22-hour target. The result demonstrates why capital expenditure should be assessed through functioning service improvements rather than construction intentions alone. [9]

**Affordability completes the causal chain.** Infrastructure beside a property does not automatically become a working household connection. Connection charges, private plumbing and recurring bills influence uptake and continued service. These household constraints interact with provider capacity and neighborhood infrastructure, explaining why a multilevel interpretation is more useful than attributing all variation to household poverty.

### Decisions supported by the comparisons

**Western warrants continuity-focused attention.** Its low summary hours, high failure density and documented borehole problems identify a specific operational combination. The corresponding concern is dependable production and distribution in affected towns, not an assumed rank in household-reported prevalence.

**Luapula and Kafubu warrant loss-focused attention.** Both reported 20 hours/day but extremely high NRW and losses per connection. Luapula also had low consumption and low coverage. Their apparent success on average hours should therefore be evaluated alongside water quantity, financial efficiency and service reach.

**Public-point users warrant separate visibility.** A majority of people served in several utilities depended on public points, and those arrangements introduce constraints beyond network delivery. Opening hours, queuing, local management and alternative-source safety affect the practical consequences of interruptions.

**Socioeconomic interpretation should remain conditional.** Owners, wealthy households and majority-language speakers should not be assumed uniformly protected, nor should their disadvantages be asserted from source-access patterns alone. The decisive comparison is within the relevant network and neighborhood, using the requested interruption outcome and clearly specified group definitions.

**One consistent evidential boundary applies.** This is a 2017–2018 assessment, with ZDHS interviews extending into January 2019. The household and socioeconomic tables use logical bounds rather than observed prevalence estimates; the 2017 LPCD ranges are conditional billed-water calculations, and unchanged-hour estimates are labeled. The current IBNET definition does not establish every historical reporting practice; native language and ethnicity are distinct; and the 2018 Western summary value of 15 hours/day is retained despite a later NWASCO narrative giving 11. Regression figures quoted here retain their original access or collection-time outcomes and are not interruption coefficients.

**The overall judgment is clear.** Zambia’s urban water challenge in 2017–2018 was not simply expanding connections: it was turning those connections into dependable service across neighborhoods and socioeconomic groups. NWASCO’s operational evidence establishes substantial and uneven continuity problems, while household interruption reporting remains the appropriate measure of who actually experienced full-day service failures.

The ZDHS selected 545 clusters and 25 households per cluster, for 13,625 selected households; normalized household weights support proportions and ratios, while Taylor linearisation is used for their variances. [35] Estimates based on fewer than 25 unweighted cases should be suppressed, and those based on 25–49 cases should be flagged as small. [31] Thus, provincial sample counts cannot be substituted for WSC denominators, and unsupported Southern or Western cells remain NR rather than being imputed.

For status and challenges, urban sanitation coverage was 63.3% in 2017 [82], while commercial-utility non-revenue water and low collection efficiency imposed an estimated US$858 million cost during 2001–2017 [90], framing the 2018 household, provider and regulator evidence [67][180][126].

Urban and peri-urban Zambia defines the relevant setting through 11 Commercial Utilities [87], with 18 hours of supply reported in 2018 [42]. It supports risk and inequality analysis because urban residence, water scarcity and piped-source use are associated with greater interruption likelihood [119], but inadequate information in previously unregulated areas [42] limits status and challenge comparisons.

The 2018 Zambia DHS supplies the user evidence window: FR361 fieldwork ran from July 2018 to January 2019 and covered 12,831 households [136], grounding household risk and inequality analysis in a dated 2018 observation. Because the design is cross-sectional, it describes current risk and status rather than proving temporal causation [103].

IBNET adds provider evidence because Water and Sewerage Companies supply data to it [169]; its indicators include continuity in hours/day and customers receiving discontinuous supply [165], plus production, consumption and capacity-utilisation measures [109]. It can test whether lower water availability and utility conditions help explain intermittency inequalities alongside user and regulator evidence [109][27], but the available series extend only up to and including 2017 [169], not a complete 2018 comparison.

NWASCO adds regulator evidence by licensing urban utilities and collecting performance information [90]; its Service Level Guarantees cover supply hours and water quality, with non-compliance subject to directives, penalties or special supervision [171]. This links status and inequality analysis to operational causes: low-voltage power in Kasama adversely affected supply duration [171], while the 2017 acceptable water-coverage benchmark was 80% [133].

Chambeshi is a named commercial utility in the 2017 urban/peri-urban context [137][28], but the available evidence has no 2018 DHS, IBNET or NWASCO estimate for it [172], preventing a quantified risk and inequality comparison. Non-payment causing revenue losses and decrepit infrastructure [185], plus low-voltage power in Kasama reducing supply duration [171], identify financial, infrastructure and energy mechanisms relevant to its service challenges.

Eastern is relevant as a provider and regulatory case because it submitted a comprehensive tariff-review application in 2017 [46]. Yet no Eastern-specific service hours, LPCD, intermittency or risk-factor result is supplied [174][186], so the requested comparison cannot be quantified; the 2018 DHS window—12,831 households from July 2018 to January 2019 [120]—is broader user context, not an Eastern causal estimate.

Kafubu is in the 2017 regulator context [28], and its 2018 reporting records non-revenue water over 70% [42], making provider performance a material intermittency-risk and status challenge. Population growth is reported to stress providers [127], while its Regulation by Incentives mechanism uses performance-linked grants [175]; these inform provider-risk interpretation, but no Kafubu-specific household-group or service-hour/LPCD comparison is supplied.

Luapula is a relevant case because LpWSC is the sole provider for Mansa’s urban and peri-urban population [52], enabling provider-area risk and inequality analysis. The provider advertises 24/7 supply [128], whereas regulator material gives a 47% projection for December 2018 [84] and reports improvements in supply hours, billing and water-testing procurement [82]; pumping and storage infrastructure [128] and community reporting of vandalism [179] identify operational mechanisms, but these are not like-for-like household intermittency contrasts.
