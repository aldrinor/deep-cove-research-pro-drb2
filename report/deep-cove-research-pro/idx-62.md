# Salt Substitutes and Cardiovascular Outcomes: Randomized Evidence Through March 2022

**Salt substitutes lower blood pressure.** Replacing ordinary salt with a reduced-sodium, potassium-enriched substitute produces a clinically useful reduction in blood pressure. A broad synthesis of trials published by August 2021 estimated reductions of **4.61 mmHg in systolic blood pressure** and 1.61 mmHg in diastolic blood pressure; that synthesis was published after March 2022 and is used here as retrospective context, not as a new eligible trial. [1]

**Cardiovascular benefit was directly demonstrated.** By the March 2022 cutoff, the Salt Substitute and Stroke Study, or SSaSS, had shown approximately 14% fewer strokes, 13% fewer major cardiovascular events, and 12% fewer deaths from any cause over a mean 4.74 years. The strongest direct evidence therefore concerns older or previously stroke-affected adults consuming substantial amounts of household-added salt. [2]

**The estimated risk reduction is modest but important.** Applying a general blood-pressure–lowering benchmark to the pooled 4.61-mmHg systolic reduction gives an estimated **12.0% relative reduction in composite cardiovascular risk**, with a sensitivity range of approximately 5.0%–23.1%. This is a modeled consequence of blood-pressure lowering, distinct from the cardiovascular benefits directly observed in salt-substitute trials. [3]

## Part 1: Clinical trials and their characteristics

### Eligibility and the unit of comparison

**The intervention is salt replacement.** The central comparison is a salt substitute versus ordinary salt, with blood pressure or cardiovascular outcomes assessed after random allocation. Relevant designs include individually randomized parallel trials, household and institutional cluster trials, stepped-wedge trials, and crossover studies. The broader evidence also includes non-potassium formulations, such as sodium chloride combined with chitosan, because the question concerns salt substitutes generally rather than one narrowly specified product. [4]

**The publication cutoff governs efficacy evidence.** Eligible primary trial reports were published on or before 31 March 2022. Later systematic reviews are distinguished throughout as summaries of earlier trials. Results published subsequently from DECIDE-Salt, for example, do not enter the cutoff-restricted efficacy assessment merely because its protocol or baseline characteristics appeared earlier.

**Different reviews count different evidence sets.** Hernandez et al.’s 2019 review included 21 randomized controlled trials, or RCTs, involving 7,403 adults: 2,016 in hypertensive populations, 163 in normotensive populations, and 5,224 in mixed populations. Its search ended in May 2018. [5] Yin et al.’s later synthesis reported 21 trial entries and 31,949 participants, whereas the broader Cochrane review identified 26 trial families involving 34,961 adults and 92 children. [6][4]

**Trial families prevent double counting.** A protocol, an interim analysis, a primary outcome publication, and a post-trial follow-up can describe the same randomized population. The rural Chinese family trial reported by Zhou in 2013 and 2016 is therefore represented once, with Sun’s 2021 mortality follow-up attached to that family. Likewise, Bernabe-Ortiz’s 2014 protocol and 2020 results describe the same Peruvian trial; the India study is indexed under its primary trial author, Yu, in 2021. [4]

**The catalog contains 29 named families.** It carries forward the Cochrane review’s 26 families and additionally identifies Mickelsen 1977, Barros 2015, and Wang 2005 from the other trial accounts. Yang’s 2018 isolated-systolic-hypertension report is linked with the Zhou 2009 family, following the Cochrane report grouping, rather than automatically being counted as another random allocation. These catalog rows are not added together to manufacture a new pooled participant denominator.

**Eligibility and canonical trial mapping**

The analytic unit is the unique randomized experiment, not each publication, follow-up, or subgroup report. Eligibility requires human randomization to a salt-substitute intervention versus a specified salt control, with a relevant blood-pressure, cardiovascular-event, stroke, or mortality outcome. The publication cutoff is inclusive through 31 March 2022.

The primary SSaSS report is **Neal et al. (2021)**, published online on 29 August 2021 and in the 16 September 2021 issue of *New England Journal of Medicine*; it therefore meets the cutoff [27]. SSaSS was an open-label, village-cluster randomized trial in 600 rural villages across five Chinese provinces—Hebei, Liaoning, Ningxia, Shanxi, and Shaanxi—with 20,995 persons recruited and assigned. The abstract reports 20,996 participants; the participant-results section reports 20,995, so 20,995 is retained as the operational randomized denominator while the one-participant discrepancy remains visible [27]. Participants had either a previous stroke or were at least 60 years old with poorly controlled blood pressure; known serious kidney disease, potassium supplementation, or potassium-sparing diuretic use were exclusion conditions [27]. Stroke was the primary outcome; major adverse cardiovascular events and all-cause death were secondary outcomes, and clinical hyperkalemia was the safety outcome [27].

The 2017 rationale/design report and the 2020 death-surveillance and interim blood-pressure/electrolyte reports belong to the same SSaSS experiment and must not be counted as additional trials [27]. A separate source-cited randomized trial by **Zhou et al. (2016)** is retained below as a provisional additional roster item rather than being merged with SSaSS.

**Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS)**

SSaSS represents one canonical randomized trial. Its primary cardiovascular-outcomes report is **Neal et al., 2021**, “Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death,” published on **16 September 2021** [33]. The 2017 rationale/design report and the economic evaluation published on **21 March 2022** are companion reports of the same trial, not additional trial rows [36][32].

The trial enrolled adults at high stroke risk because of a previous stroke or age at least 60 years with high blood pressure. The final report included **20,995 randomized participants**; 10,504 were assigned to salt substitute and 10,491 to regular salt. Mean age was 65.4 years, 49.5% were female, 72.6% had a history of stroke, and 88.4% had a history of hypertension [33][34]. Follow-up occurred at six-month intervals, with outcomes assessed over the approximately five-year trial period [33].

Several source-level discrepancies are retained rather than averaged. The earlier protocol reported **21,003 randomized participants**, whereas the registry recorded **20,996 actual enrollees** and the final outcomes report analyzed 20,995 participants [36][39][33]. These are protocol, registry, and final-report counts, respectively; the canonical row uses the final outcomes-report population. The registry also describes the planned setting as five Northern Chinese provinces and Tibet, whereas the final report identifies five provinces; the table therefore uses the final report’s verified trial geography [39][33].

The final outcomes report specifies a **75% sodium-chloride/25% potassium-chloride** intervention [33]. The earlier manufacturing standard allowed **70.00% ± 10.00% sodium chloride and 30.00% ± 10.00% potassium chloride** [36], and another extraction describes the supplied mixture as approximately 70%/30% [33]. These formulation descriptions are preserved as qualifications; the canonical table cell uses the exact composition stated in the primary outcomes report. The intervention was supplied free of charge, at approximately 20 g per person per household day, to replace regular salt in cooking, seasoning, and food preservation; control villages continued ordinary salt use [32][33].

Baseline BP was recorded during the physical examination. The primary report’s **154.0/89.2 mmHg** is a pooled overall baseline mean, not an arm-specific value; baseline characteristics were reported as balanced between randomized groups [33]. The protocol gives the same values rounded to **154/89 mmHg** [36]. A secondary account reports an end-of-follow-up intervention–control BP difference of **−3.3/0.7 mmHg at five years**; this is a follow-up contrast, not a baseline value and does not replace either table cell [30].

The operational eligibility decision is **included**: SSaSS is a human cluster-randomized comparison of a specified salt substitute with regular salt, with prespecified fatal and nonfatal stroke, major cardiovascular events, and total mortality outcomes; the protocol also specified masked endpoint adjudication [33][38]. The primary report found lower rates of stroke, major cardiovascular events, and all-cause death with salt substitution, while detailed effect estimates belong to the findings section rather than this characteristics table [33]. The design rationale combines sodium reduction with increased potassium exposure; the protocol flagged potential hyperkalemia concerns in severe renal impairment, with potassium-sparing or potassium-supplementing medicines, or with very high salt-substitute intake [36]. A 2023 scale-up analysis is treated only as a later cross-check and does not extend the March 2022 evidence cutoff or create another trial row [42].

**Conclusion and deciding condition.** The SSaSS row is supported for all eight requested characteristics and is classified as a large (at least 300 participants), long-duration (at least 12 months) cluster RCT. When reports differ, the deciding rule is to use the final eligible primary outcomes report for the canonical table value while retaining protocol, registry, rounding, and companion-report differences as explicit qualifications.

**SSaSS: canonical trial entry and linked reports**

The canonical trial is the Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS), reported in the cardiovascular-outcome paper by Neal et al. in 2021; bibliographic records give 29 August 2021 and 16 September 2021 under different publication-date conventions, both before the 31 March 2022 cutoff [61][49][64].

SSaSS randomized 20,995 participants—10,504 to salt substitute and 10,491 to regular salt—although earlier design reports describe 20,996 participants as enrolled; 20,995 is the appropriate final randomized denominator for the sample-size classification [45][47][61]. Participants had either a history of stroke or were at least 60 years old with poorly controlled high blood pressure. Mean age was 65.4 years, 72.6% had prior stroke, and 88.4% had hypertension. Exclusions included potassium-sparing diuretic or potassium-supplement use, known serious kidney disease, likely survival of less than six months, or eating most meals outside the home; routine biochemical renal-function testing was not performed [61]. At final follow-up, vital status was complete and nonfatal-event ascertainment covered 99,473 of 99,522 scheduled person-years; the slide summary reports 100% vital-status and 99.9% nonfatal-outcome follow-up completeness [45][61].

The 2017 Neal design report is a companion publication, not a separate trial. It describes planned six-monthly follow-up for five years, fatal and nonfatal stroke as the primary endpoint, major cardiovascular events and total mortality as secondary endpoints, and 90% power to detect at least a 13% stroke-risk reduction assuming a 3.5% annual event rate and a 3.0-mmHg between-group SBP difference [53]. Huang et al.’s 2018 and 2020 reports are interim SSaSS reports, while the later headache report published on 24 February 2024 and the 2025 cumulative-BP analysis are linked secondary reports outside the requested publication window; none should create an additional trial row [43][48][56][54].

The assigned intervention was provided for household cooking and food preservation at approximately 20 g per person per day, with a maximum of 20 kg per year for a three-person household [63]. The detailed trial descriptions therefore support the 75%/25% formulation used in the table, while the primary report also records the broader national manufacturing tolerance and an abstract shorthand of approximately 70%/30% [61][63]. At five years, 91.7% of intervention participants and 6.4% of regular-salt participants reported using salt substitute [61]. A later policy summary reports 92% continued use among more than 10,000 assigned participants; its stated 15–50% sodium-chloride-replacement range describes LSSS generally and should not replace the SSaSS-specific formulation [52].

BP was measured with automated Omron devices while participants were seated; two readings at least five minutes apart were averaged. Measurements were obtained at 12, 24, 36, 48 and 60 months, with subsamples at the earlier timepoints and all available survivors at 60 months [63]. Interim estimates provide additional linked evidence: at 12, 24 and 36 months, data were available for 1,198, 2,789 and 1,163 participants, respectively; one report found urinary sodium 0.4 g lower, potassium 0.7 g higher, SBP 3.1 mmHg lower (95% CI −4.5 to −1.7; p<0.001), and DBP 0.1 mmHg different (95% CI −0.7 to 1.0; p=0.766), with a smaller year-2 point estimate and wide confidence intervals [56]. A separate interim report found sodium 0.32 g lower (95% CI −0.68 to 0.05), potassium 0.77 g higher (95% CI 0.60 to 0.93), SBP 2.65 mmHg lower (95% CI −4.32 to −0.97), and DBP 0.30 mmHg higher (95% CI −0.72 to 1.32); reported salt-substitute use was 97.5% versus 4.2%, and the combined annual fatal or nonfatal stroke rate during the first two years was estimated at 3.2% [54].

The primary SSaSS report identified lower rates of stroke, major cardiovascular events and all-cause death over a mean 4.74-year follow-up: stroke was 29.14 versus 33.65 events per 1,000 person-years (rate ratio 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.96; p=0.006), major cardiovascular events were 49.09 versus 56.29 (rate ratio 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.94; p<0.001), and death was 39.28 versus 44.61 (rate ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.95; p<0.001) [64]. Exploratory outcomes also favored salt substitution for vascular death (rate ratio 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.96) and nonfatal acute coronary syndrome (0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.93), whereas nonfatal stroke was imprecise (0.90, 95% CI 0.80–1.01) [44]. Reported totals were 3,123 stroke events involving 2,678 participants, 5,241 major cardiovascular-event episodes involving 4,499 participants, and 4,172 deaths [45]. Serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalemia were not significantly increased: 3.35 versus 3.30 events per 1,000 person-years, rate ratio 1.04 (95% CI 0.80–1.37; p=0.76) [64].

The 2024 headache companion linked to the same trial reported 4,454 of 16,486 respondents with headache—27.4% in the intervention group versus 26.6% in control, RR 1.04 (95% CI 0.93–1.16; p=0.48)—and no difference in headache severity (p=0.90). It also reported 60-month mean SBP of 142.9 ± 20.5 versus 146.5 ± 21.1 mmHg and DBP of 84.6 ± 11.7 versus 85.5 ± 12.1 mmHg; during the trial, SBP was reported as 3.4 mmHg lower (95% CI 1.8–4.9; p≤0.001) and DBP as 0.8 mmHg lower (95% CI −0.1 to 1.7; p=0.08) [43]. The report noted that approximately 20% of participants died, so pre-death headache data were unavailable, and that headache was collected at only one timepoint without baseline headache or headache-type data [43]. The 2025 cumulative-BP analysis similarly belongs to SSaSS rather than a new RCT: cumulative SBP was 740 (SD 85) versus 750 (87) mmHg×year, cumulative MAP 560 (58) versus 566 (59), and cumulative pulse pressure 306 (67) versus 313 (68), while cumulative DBP was 434 versus 437 mmHg×year and traditional DBP 85 versus 86 mmHg, with no DBP difference [48].

A separate candidate must not be merged with SSaSS: the Salt Substitute in India Study (SSiIS) protocol describes a double-blind randomized trial in rural India, with a three-month intervention from November 2019 to April 2020, 502 randomized adults with hypertension, a 70% sodium-chloride/30% potassium-chloride intervention, iodized regular salt containing 100% sodium chloride as control, and pooled baseline BP of 133.5/83.6 mmHg [62].

SSaSS (Neal et al., 2021) is the canonical eligible trial represented here: 20,995 participants in rural China were assigned to a potassium-enriched salt substitute or regular salt, with a mean follow-up of 4.74 years in the primary outcome report [35].


The duration conversion is \(4.74\text{ years}\times12=56.88\text{ months}\), rounded to 56.9 months. The primary report’s 75%/25% formulation is retained as the canonical trial value; the 70% ± 10%/30% ± 10% specification is retained as a manufacturing-tolerance qualification rather than substituted for the primary report’s nominal composition [35][70][74]. One gram of the potassium-enriched salt was also described as containing approximately 0.30 g sodium and 0.13 g potassium [40].

**SSaSS: Canonical Identity and Eligibility**

The canonical cardiovascular-outcome report is Neal B, Wu Y, Feng X, Zhang R, Zhang Y, Shi J, et al., “Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death,” *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2021;385:1067–1077 [40]. The 2017 rationale, design, and baseline-characteristics report and the 2020 Huang et al. interim urinary-electrolyte and blood-pressure report describe the same randomized experiment and do not receive separate trial rows [40][41][55]. One secondary reference lists the outcome report as “Tian M, et al.” without a year, but the full bibliographic record resolves the primary report to Neal et al., 2021 [65][40].

SSaSS randomized 20,995 participants: 10,504 were assigned to the salt-substitute group and 10,491 to regular salt in a later full-trial accounting [73]. Participants were adults with a prior stroke or were at least 60 years old with poorly controlled blood pressure; the primary report gives a mean age of 65.4 years, a history of stroke in 72.6%, diagnosed hypertension in 88.4%, and antihypertensive use in 79.3% [35]. Under the shared classification rules, the study is in the **>=300-participant** category and the **>=12-month** duration category. Its study-level age classification is **>=60 years** because the mean age was 65.4 years, but this does not mean every participant was at least 60: prior stroke was an alternative eligibility pathway [35][70].

The intervention was supplied for household cooking and food preservation, at approximately 20 g per person per day, and participants were advised to use it more sparingly; control villages continued their usual regular-salt practices [70]. Mechanistically, the formulation replaced part of sodium chloride with potassium chloride, combining lower sodium exposure with higher potassium exposure [37][74]. A later mechanistic analysis, published after the cutoff, reported mean between-group changes of −15.2 mmol/day in urinary sodium, +20.6 mmol/day in urinary potassium, and −3.3 mmHg in SBP; it interpreted the clinical-outcome effect as mediated through blood-pressure lowering, but it is not a primary report eligible for the March 2022 evidence set [69].

The primary outcome report confirms cardiovascular-outcome eligibility. Over a mean 4.74-year follow-up, stroke rates were 29.14 versus 33.65 per 1,000 person-years with salt substitute versus regular salt (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.96; p=0.006); major cardiovascular-event rates were 49.09 versus 56.29 per 1,000 person-years (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.94; p<0.001); and all-cause death rates were 39.28 versus 44.61 per 1,000 person-years (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.95; p<0.001) [35]. The same outcome summary reports vascular-cause death of 22.9 versus 26.3 per 1,000 person-years (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.96), nonfatal acute coronary syndrome of 3.8 versus 5.1 per 1,000 person-years (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.93), and nonfatal stroke of 22.4 versus 24.9 per 1,000 person-years (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.80–1.01) [70]. Serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalemia were 3.35 versus 3.30 per 1,000 person-years (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.80–1.37; p=0.76) [35].

**Report Linkage and Evidence Qualifications**

The 2020 interim report described BP and urinary-electrolyte measurements at 12, 24, and 36 months, with available data for 1,198, 2,789, and 1,163 participants, respectively [68]. Its interim estimate was an SBP difference of −2.65 mmHg (95% CI −4.32 to −0.97) and a DBP difference of +0.30 mmHg (95% CI −0.72 to +1.32); these are interim measurements and do not replace the primary trial’s long-term follow-up value [55]. The interim PubMed record gives the study population as 20,996 individuals, whereas the primary outcome report gives 20,995 enrolled participants; 20,995 is retained as the canonical trial denominator [55][35].

Follow-up values vary by report and endpoint. The canonical row uses the primary report’s 4.74-year mean. A secondary summary reports a 5.12-year median, a later Shanxi sub-study reports 4.66 years for its subgroup, and a review gives a 31-month figure; these values are retained as report-specific observations rather than combined into a single unqualified duration [51][67][72]. The five-year planned or final-trial description remains consistent with the primary report’s approximately five-year design [35][65].

Later publications are linked companion reports, not additional randomized trials. They include a headache analysis dated 2024-02-24 in one record and 2024-05-01 in another [73][46], a mechanistic analysis published 2024-02-21 [69], an antihypertensive-medication sub-study published 2024-07-16 [67], a fracture analysis published in 2024 [50], and a cumulative-BP analysis published 27 March 2025 [66]. All are after the March 2022 cutoff and therefore cannot expand the eligible primary-report evidence window.

Later companion tables also report analysis-specific arm values. The headache report gives baseline SBP of 153.2 ± 23.2 mmHg in the intervention group and 153.8 ± 23.4 mmHg in control, with DBP of 88.9 ± 13.8 and 89.3 ± 13.8 mmHg, respectively; its analyzed population was 16,486 rather than the full 20,995 [73]. The fracture report gives SBP values of 153.8 and 154.2 mmHg and DBP values of 89.1 and 89.4 mmHg in its intervention and control groups [50]. These later, outcome-specific tables do not displace the canonical pooled baseline values of 154.0/89.2 mmHg used above [40].

A separate 1,612-participant trial in 48 residential elderly-care facilities is identified as DECIDE-Salt, a cluster-randomized factorial study; its participants and facilities are not SSaSS and are not counted in the SSaSS row [75].

**SSaSS: canonical trial identity and eligibility**

The canonical included trial is the Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS), reported by Neal et al. in 2021 in *The New England Journal of Medicine*; the primary report is dated 16 September 2021 and therefore precedes the inclusive 31 March 2022 cutoff [84][89]. SSaSS was conducted in rural China across 600 villages in five provinces, with villages randomized 1:1 to intervention or continued usual care [77][91]. The final trial report identifies 20,995 randomized participants—10,504 assigned to salt substitute and 10,491 to regular salt [84][92].

The protocol and interim report describe 20,996 enrolled participants [85][91]. This one-participant discrepancy is retained rather than silently harmonized: 20,995 is used as the canonical final randomized denominator because it appears in the primary outcome report, while 20,996 remains the protocol/interim enrollment value. The population comprised people with a history of stroke or people aged at least 60 years with poorly controlled blood pressure; the reported mean age was 65.4 years, 49.5% were women, 72.6% had a history of stroke, and 88.4% had a history of hypertension [84].

The SSaSS intervention was salt substitute containing 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride by mass, compared with regular table salt containing 100% sodium chloride [84][90]. The intervention was supplied for household cooking and food preservation and was intended to replace ordinary salt use [79]. Some secondary descriptions express the intervention as approximately one-third less sodium than regular salt, with 8% lower sodium intake and 50% higher potassium intake; these are intake changes, not an alternative formulation to the reported 75:25 mass composition [81]. A separate source reports a 65% sodium chloride, 25% potassium chloride, and 10% magnesium sulfate formulation, but explicitly describes it as belonging to a previous randomized trial rather than the later SSaSS intervention [86]. It should therefore not be assigned to SSaSS.

SSaSS satisfies the outcome requirement because its planned primary endpoint was fatal and nonfatal stroke and its secondary endpoints were total major cardiovascular events and total mortality; potential endpoints were reviewed by a masked adjudication committee [77][91]. The final report also assessed serious adverse events attributed to clinical hyperkalemia [84]. These endpoint definitions establish eligibility; the comparative effect estimates belong in the findings synthesis rather than being used to create additional trial rows.


The baseline SBP and DBP values in the row are extracted from a later SSaSS secondary report whose table distinguishes intervention, control, total assessed, and total randomized populations; they are not evidence of a second trial or a second randomized cohort [57]. A separate extracted SSaSS table displays 154.00 ± 23.50 mmHg for SBP and 89.25 ± 14.00 mmHg for DBP, which is numerically consistent with the total-randomized values above after rounding .

**Report-to-trial linkage and follow-up status**

The 2017 rationale, design, and baseline-characteristics report, the 2020 interim urinary-electrolyte and BP report, and the 2021 cardiovascular-outcome report belong to the same SSaSS randomized experiment [82][91]. They must be linked to one canonical trial rather than counted as three studies. Later reports concerning the prior-stroke subgroup, cardiac outcomes, headache outcomes, and other secondary endpoints likewise remain SSaSS reports rather than additional RCTs; for example, the prior-stroke analysis included 15,249 SSaSS participants [87][88].

The primary report’s bibliographic date is 16 September 2021 [84][89]. A secondary webpage gives 29 August 2021 as the article date and 30 August 2021 as the webpage publication date, while another Chinese webpage is dated 23 September 2021 [80][90]. These are retained as source-date discrepancies; the supplied evidence does not establish an online-first-versus-issue-date distinction for those secondary pages. None changes SSaSS’s eligibility, because all reported dates precede 31 March 2022.

**Discovery leads and unresolved roster records**

Aggregate review counts are discovery evidence rather than the canonical trial roster. A review published in September 2022 reported 21 trials and 31,949 participants, with 19 trials contributing BP outcomes and five contributing clinical-outcome data [60]. Another later network meta-analysis reported 34 RCTs involving 37,063 participants from 15 countries , while a WHO evidence summary reported 26 RCTs involving 34,961 adults and 92 children published between 1986 and 2021 [71]. A 2026 review again reported 34 RCTs and emphasized that mortality and cardiovascular evidence was dominated by SSaSS . The differing totals reflect differences in search date, intervention and comparator definitions, population, and outcome eligibility; none is adopted as the final through-cutoff count.

The permitted 2022 Cochrane review *Replacing salt with low-sodium salt substitutes (LSSS) for cardiovascular health* and the 2022 *Heart* review *Effects of salt substitutes on clinical outcomes* are useful for citation chasing, but their citations do not independently establish the identity, eligibility, or requested table values for each underlying RCT . Similarly, one secondary evidence source considered prospective cohorts as well as cluster RCTs and RCTs and excluded crossover studies and cluster trials with fewer than two intervention and two control clusters; those review-level criteria do not replace the present review’s requirement for a verified human randomized salt-substitute-versus-specified-salt-control trial [94].

One open-roster candidate has a reported publication date of **15 July 2016**, but the supplied evidence does not establish its first author, canonical title, country, randomized design, intervention composition, control salt, baseline SBP, baseline DBP, or follow-up [83]. Its table status is therefore: publication date **reported**; all eight requested study-characteristic values **unresolved or not reported** until the underlying primary report is identified and linked.

A separate later report describes an antecedent double-blind randomized trial conducted in 2006 among 200 families and 462 participants, with 440 participants completing a three-year intervention; it then follows survivors observationally from April 2009 through 30 April 2019 [93]. Baseline hypertension was reported in 160 of 209 participants (76.6%) in the salt-substitute group and 162 of 219 (74.0%) in the normal-salt group [93]. The report separately describes SSaSS as a much larger 20,996-person trial, so this 462-participant experiment must not be merged with SSaSS. Its primary report identity and cutoff eligibility remain unresolved in the supplied evidence.

DECIDE-Salt is another named salt-substitution RCT but is not eligible for the requested through-March-2022 roster: its publication date was 1 April 2023, after the cutoff, despite its 1,612 participants and randomized factorial design [76]. It therefore receives no row in the cutoff-limited characteristics table.

Finally, population exclusions remain part of the roster metadata. The reviewed RCT evidence commonly excluded people with kidney disease, impaired renal function, or potassium-sparing medication use, and some trials also excluded diabetes [71]. SSaSS-related summaries likewise describe limited kidney-function monitoring and exclusion of people with serious kidney disease or potassium-sparing diuretics [78]. These are retained as transportability and evidence-status qualifications, not converted into unreported table values.

Neal et al., 2021 is the canonical report for the Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS); reports dated 2017 and 2020 are linked to that same randomized trial and should not be counted as separate trials [89][27].

SSaSS follow-up from randomization or intervention start to trial assessment averaged 4.74 years; assessments were scheduled every 6 months for 5 years, and follow-up completeness was 99.9% [26][36][45].

Applying the eligibility classification for randomized or cluster-randomized comparisons of low-sodium salt substitute with ordinary salt, with crossover RCTs and cluster trials with fewer than two intervention and two control clusters excluded, SSaSS is retained as an eligible canonical trial: 600 villages and 20,995 persons were in the randomized period, the substitute was 75% sodium chloride/25% potassium chloride versus 100% sodium chloride, and outcomes included fatal and nonfatal stroke, major cardiovascular events, and death from any cause [94][84][92][32][27]. Reports dated 2017 and 2020 are linked to the Neal et al., 2021 trial and therefore are deduplicated rather than entered as separate trials [27][89].

SSaSS used an open-label, village-cluster randomized design: villages were allocated 1:1 to intervention or control with county stratification through a central computerized process, and the village was the unit of randomization [33][63][28]. The village-level allocation is the key denominator and precision feature; open-label delivery leaves masking-related risk, and no factorial or crossover structure is documented in the supplied design evidence .

SSaSS's mean interval from randomization or intervention start to final follow-up was 4.74 years; the month-based schedule used contacts every 6 months over 5 years, with interim assessments at 12, 24, and 36 months [26][36][56]. The median follow-up was 5.12 years and follow-up completeness was 99.9% [51][45].

SSaSS receives the canonical trial row; reports dated 2017 and 2020 are linked to Neal et al., 2021 and therefore do not receive separate rows [27][89].

### Which characteristics matter most?

**Baseline pressure changes the clinical interpretation.** Zhao’s Tibetan participants began at approximately 177/105 mmHg, CSSS at 159/93 mmHg, and SSaSS at 154/89 mmHg. The India cohort, although selected for diagnosed hypertension, had an overall baseline of approximately 133/83 mmHg. These are different treatment contexts: lowering very high pressure, adding dietary treatment to ongoing medical care, and maintaining control in people whose hypertension may already be treated. [9][8][13]

In Zhao et al.’s baseline table, DBP was 104.5 mmHg overall (SD 12.6 mmHg), 105.8 mmHg in the regular-salt arm, and 103.2 mmHg in the salt-substitute arm.

**Allocation determines the statistical unit.** An individually randomized trial assigns people, whereas a cluster trial assigns kitchens, households, nursing homes, or villages. People sharing a kitchen or household also share food exposure, so their outcomes are correlated. Consequently, a trial involving many residents but only a few randomized kitchens does not provide the same protection against baseline imbalance as a trial involving hundreds of randomized villages.

**Formulation does not equal achieved intake.** A 75% NaCl product contains 25% less sodium chloride per gram than pure NaCl, but total dietary sodium will fall by less than 25% if only some salt is replaced, participants compensate by using more, or substantial sodium comes from other foods. Added potassium is also part of the treatment. Thus, these trials compare combined dietary changes rather than isolating sodium reduction alone.

**Follow-up measures different questions.** Three-month trials can establish a blood-pressure response. Multi-year trials can establish whether exposure and benefit persist and, when sufficiently large, whether cardiovascular events decline. A ten-year observational follow-up after a three-year intervention addresses a different question again: the consequences of original assignment after the randomized treatment period has ended.

**Part 1 takeaway.** The trial set supports a comparison of salt-replacement strategies, but formulation, baseline pressure, allocation unit, and continuing exposure must accompany every interpretation of effectiveness.

## Part 2: Overall findings and subgroup analyses

### Overall effects on systolic and diastolic blood pressure

**The pooled effect is consistently favorable.** Across the broad 19-trial synthesis, salt substitutes lowered systolic blood pressure, or SBP, by 4.61 mmHg, with a 95% confidence interval of −6.07 to −3.14 mmHg. Diastolic blood pressure, or DBP, fell by 1.61 mmHg, with a 95% confidence interval of −2.42 to −0.79 mmHg; both comparisons were reported as statistically significant at P<0.001. [1][17]

**Other syntheses support the same direction.** The estimates differ in magnitude, but not in their overall conclusion that salt substitution lowers average blood pressure.

| Synthesis | Evidence set | SBP difference, mmHg | DBP difference, mmHg | Interpretation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Hernandez et al., 2019 | 21 RCTs; search through May 2018 | −7.81 (−9.47 to −6.15) | −3.96 (−5.17 to −2.74) | Larger effect; mostly low-certainty evidence |
| Jafarnejad et al., 2020 | 11 comparisons; 1,119 participants | −8.87 (−11.19 to −6.55) | −4.04 (−5.70 to −2.39) | Selected hypertensive populations |
| Yin et al., later 2022 synthesis | 19 BP-reporting trials | −4.61 (−6.07 to −3.14) | −1.61 (−2.42 to −0.79) | Broad international estimate |
| Cochrane, August 2022 | 20 SBP and 19 DBP trials | −4.76 (−6.01 to −3.50) | −2.43 (−3.50 to −1.36) | Moderate-certainty adult BP evidence |

Sources: Hernandez [5], Jafarnejad [18], Yin [1], and Cochrane [4]. Parentheses contain 95% confidence intervals.

**The estimates should not be averaged together.** These reviews contain overlapping trials, use different eligibility rules, and select different populations and measurement times. Treating their pooled estimates as independent observations would count the same participants repeatedly. The appropriate comparison is therefore between the conclusions and populations represented by each synthesis.

**Statistical significance is clear overall.** The SBP and DBP confidence intervals in the broad syntheses exclude zero. However, the later international synthesis reported substantial blood-pressure heterogeneity, with I² exceeding 70%; Cochrane reported I² values of 78% for SBP and 88% for DBP. These statistics indicate meaningful variation among trial results beyond ordinary sampling error. [19][4]

**Clinical significance depends on the decision.** A mean SBP reduction around 4–5 mmHg is a useful adjunct to treatment and can matter substantially at population scale. It does not imply that a person with severe hypertension will achieve control using salt substitution alone. The Tibetan trial illustrates both points: blood pressure improved appreciably, but most participants still remained above 140/90 mmHg after treatment. [9]

**Individual and population thresholds differ.** Cochrane described the mean reductions as small but important from a population perspective, while using larger thresholds—approximately 10 mmHg SBP and 5 mmHg DBP—for a clearly important individual change. These descriptions are compatible: a modest average reduction applied to many people can prevent substantial disease without being sufficient treatment for every individual. [4]

**Tibet demonstrated substantial additional lowering.** Zhao et al.’s intention-to-treat analysis included all 282 randomized participants and used multiple imputation for incomplete follow-up. Adjusted differences were −7.6 mmHg SBP and −3.5 mmHg DBP, with P=0.003 and P=0.011, respectively. [9]

**Control improved without eliminating benefit.** Blood pressure also fell in Zhao’s regular-salt group. Seasonal temperature changes and regression toward the mean were plausible explanations, but the randomized between-group contrast remained favorable to the substitute. This distinction matters: the intervention effect is the additional decline relative to control, not the entire decline within the intervention arm.

**BP control improved but remained incomplete.** In Zhao’s per-protocol population, 19.2% of substitute users versus 8.8% of regular-salt users achieved blood pressure below 140/90 mmHg, a calculated difference of 10.4 percentage points; P=0.027. This supports practical value, while the low absolute control rate argues against replacing indicated antihypertensive treatment with dietary substitution alone. [9]

**CSSS demonstrated a sustained systolic response.** The overall SBP difference was 3.7 mmHg, but the difference increased during follow-up, reaching 5.4 mmHg at 12 months, with a 95% confidence interval of 2.3–8.5 mmHg. DBP did not show a detectable treatment effect. A null DBP result therefore does not negate the trial’s positive SBP finding. [8]

**India showed benefit at lower baseline pressure.** The India trial’s −4.58/−1.14-mmHg SBP/DBP differences were observed despite a cohort baseline around 133/83 mmHg. Its high three-month completion rate—476 of 502 participants, or approximately 95%—strengthens the short-term effectiveness interpretation. Nevertheless, the trial’s clinical contribution is blood-pressure lowering rather than directly measured prevention of cardiovascular deaths over three months. [13]

**Peru answered a population-prevention question.** Bernabe-Ortiz et al. enrolled 2,376 adults across six villages rather than restricting recruitment to severe hypertension. The average BP reduction was consequently smaller than in several hypertensive clinical trials. Among initially nonhypertensive participants, the time- and cluster-adjusted analysis nevertheless estimated a 51% reduction in developing hypertension, with a reported interval corresponding to a 29%–66% reduction. [16]

**Hypertension incidence and prevalence are different outcomes.** Peru’s result concerns newly developing hypertension among people initially without it. By contrast, the 18-month community trial summarized in Cochrane found a hypertension prevalence risk ratio of 0.97, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.90–1.03, among 2,566 participants. A favorable incidence finding in one population and little change in endpoint prevalence in another are therefore not interchangeable estimates of the same effect. [4]

**The family-trial change contrast is descriptive.** In the Sun follow-up account of the Zhou trial, reported three-year changes were −14.3 versus −5.4 mmHg for SBP and −4.7 versus −0.7 mmHg for DBP. The calculated between-group change contrasts are therefore −8.9 and −4.0 mmHg. These calculations describe the reported change rows; they are not substituted for an adjusted intention-to-treat estimate from the complete randomized population. [10]

**Pereira illustrates the exposure problem.** Although its intervention product contained 50% NaCl and 50% KCl, the Cochrane account describes achieved sodium-chloride replacement of approximately 30%, partly because participants did not consume all meals at home. A product’s label therefore overstates the dietary change if it is interpreted as replacement of all daily sodium exposure. [4]

**Children require a separate interpretation.** Toft’s family-randomized bread study included 92 children. The adjusted differences were +0.12 mmHg for SBP, with a 95% confidence interval of −4.41 to +4.64, and +1.28 mmHg for DBP, with a 95% confidence interval of −1.56 to +4.12. These results do not reproduce the clear average adult BP benefit, and Cochrane rated the pediatric evidence very low certainty. [4]

**Stroke recovery is not stroke prevention.** Pan et al.’s 291-person trial assessed potassium- and magnesium-enriched salts after stroke and reported improved functional recovery. That outcome concerns recovery among people who have already experienced stroke, not prevention of recurrent stroke or death. It should therefore sit alongside, rather than be pooled conceptually with, SSaSS’s event-prevention findings. [10]

**Blood-pressure takeaway.** Salt substitutes lower adult SBP and DBP on average, but the expected magnitude is better described as a population-dependent response than as one fixed effect for every product and patient.

### Why salt substitutes lower blood pressure

**Both sodium and potassium change.** Across 13 trials, the later synthesis estimated 0.48 g/day lower urinary sodium excretion and 0.45 g/day higher urinary potassium excretion, with P<0.001 for both. These measured changes support a physiological pathway linking the assigned product to altered mineral intake and lower blood pressure. [17]

**SSaSS provides a concrete example.** Relative to regular salt, the substitute reduced 24-hour urinary sodium by 350 mg/day and increased potassium by 803 mg/day. Against baseline excretion of approximately 4.3 g/day sodium and 1.4 g/day potassium, these correspond to calculated changes of about 8% and 57%, respectively: 0.350/4.3 and 0.803/1.4. Thus, the achieved intervention changed potassium proportionately more than sodium. [2]

**Lower sodium can reduce circulatory load.** Sodium reduction influences extracellular fluid balance and vascular resistance, while increased potassium can promote sodium excretion and improve blood-pressure regulation. The two changes may reinforce one another. The salt-substitute trials, however, generally change both simultaneously, so the observed treatment effect should not be attributed exclusively to either ingredient. [2]

**Urinary findings are not uniformly identical.** CSSS found higher urinary potassium concentration without a detectable difference in urinary sodium concentration. The Zhou family trial similarly found a difference in potassium and the sodium-to-potassium ratio but not in urinary sodium after intervention. Peru also reported clearer evidence of increased potassium than decreased sodium. These findings strengthen the interpretation of salt substitution as a combined mineral intervention. [21][10][16]

**Formulation may influence the response.** Yin et al.’s trial-level meta-regression associated each 10-percentage-point lower NaCl proportion with an additional 1.53-mmHg SBP reduction, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.03–3.02 mmHg, and an additional 0.95-mmHg DBP reduction, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.12–1.78 mmHg. [22]

**That association is not a dose trial.** Trials using lower-NaCl products may also differ in baseline hypertension, food provision, medication use, follow-up, or adherence. The meta-regression therefore does not establish that changing a particular patient’s product by ten percentage points will necessarily produce the reported additional reduction. Nor does it identify one universally optimal formulation.

**Food sources determine practical reach.** A substitute used during home cooking can replace much of the discretionary salt in some rural settings. Its reach is smaller when most sodium comes from manufactured foods that remain unchanged. Geleijnse’s Dutch trial addressed that problem partly by providing reformulated foods as well as household salt; the selected foods represented a substantial share of usual salt intake. [7]

**Taste affects continuing exposure.** CSSS found no difference in saltiness or flavor ratings, and Geleijnse reported generally acceptable trial foods. These observations help explain why replacement can be more sustainable than asking people simply to tolerate less salty food. They do not imply that every high-KCl product has identical acceptability. [21][7]

**Mechanism takeaway.** The evidence supports blood-pressure lowering through altered sodium–potassium exposure, with the achieved dietary change—not the product percentage alone—determining practical effectiveness.

### Mortality and cardiovascular events

**SSaSS is the decisive outcome trial.** It randomized 600 villages and enrolled 20,995 adults with prior stroke or older age plus poorly controlled hypertension. Nearly three-quarters had a history of stroke, and approximately four-fifths were already using blood-pressure–lowering medication. Its cardiovascular findings therefore demonstrate benefit additional to usual care in a high-risk population. [2]

**All-cause mortality is especially informative.** The all-cause death rate was 12% lower, with a confidence interval corresponding to approximately 5%–18% lower rates. Because it counts deaths regardless of assigned cause, this outcome is less dependent on distinguishing vascular from nonvascular death. It supports a net survival benefit in the trial population rather than merely a favorable change in one disease label.

**Cardiovascular mortality also declined directly.** Vascular-death rates were 13% lower in SSaSS, with a 95% confidence interval corresponding to approximately 4%–21% lower rates. This is an observed in-cutoff trial result. It should not be replaced by a blood-pressure-based mortality prediction or described only through a later pooled review. [2]

**Not every component reached statistical significance.** Nonfatal stroke had a rate ratio of 0.90, but its confidence interval extended to 1.01. The primary fatal-or-nonfatal stroke outcome was significant because it is a different, broader endpoint. Similarly, the hemorrhagic-stroke estimate was more strongly favorable—rate ratio 0.69, 95% CI 0.56–0.85—than the ischemic-stroke estimate of 0.93, 95% CI 0.82–1.05. [2]

**The design strengthens causal interpretation.** Randomization occurred after recruitment and baseline assessment, analyses accounted for village clustering, and endpoint adjudicators were unaware of assignment. Vital status was determined for all participants, while nonfatal-event follow-up covered more than 99.9% of scheduled person-time. These features make SSaSS much more informative for clinical outcomes than a small short-duration trial with occasional deaths. [2]

**Additional documented evidence**

**Overall randomized evidence**

Across the available randomized evidence, salt substitution lowered blood pressure, but the magnitude varied by synthesis, population, formulation, and follow-up. Effects are expressed as salt substitute minus control; therefore, negative blood-pressure values favor the substitute. A 2022 synthesis reported 21 randomized trials involving 31,949 participants, including 19 trials with blood-pressure outcomes and 5 with clinical outcomes: SBP was 4.61 mmHg lower (95% CI −6.07 to −3.14) and DBP was 1.61 mmHg lower (95% CI −2.42 to −0.79) with salt substitution. The same source reported lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.85–0.94), cardiovascular mortality (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.81–0.94), and cardiovascular events (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.85–0.94). These figures are reported secondary-synthesis results rather than a final independently verified roster-specific pool. [70]

A separate summary of a 2022 meta-analysis reported a 4.6-mmHg SBP reduction (95% CI 3.1–6.1) based on 19 blood-pressure trials; the analyzed populations included both hypertensive and mixed populations, and the source noted evidence of publication bias that could have inflated the estimate. The same source states that the blood-pressure effect occurred primarily among people with hypertension. [70] A later 14-study salt-substitute synthesis reported a larger pooled SBP reduction of 7.44 mmHg (95% CI −8.82 to −6.06; I²=93.3%) and DBP reduction of 3.77 mmHg (95% CI −5.21 to −2.32; I²=97.2%). Within that synthesis, the higher-quality subgroup had an SBP effect of −7.80 mmHg across 7 studies (95% CI −9.84 to −5.77; I²=66.5%), whereas the medium-quality subgroup had an effect of −7.11 mmHg across 7 studies (95% CI −8.97 to −5.25; I²=96.3%); corresponding DBP effects were −4.28 and −3.27 mmHg, with I² values of 76.2% and 98.6%, respectively. [72]

An earlier pooled analysis reported an SBP mean difference of −4.9 mmHg (95% CI −7.3 to −2.5; P<0.001) and a DBP mean difference of −1.5 mmHg (95% CI −2.7 to −0.3; P=0.013), but the supplied source does not establish that its eligibility, publication cutoff, or deduplication rules match this review. [77] Thus, the statistical direction is consistent, and the confidence intervals in several syntheses exclude no effect, but the absolute magnitude should not be treated as a single universal value. The high I² values, evidence of publication bias, differing hypertension composition, and different salt formulations all limit direct averaging. A sustained reduction of roughly 1.6–4.6 mmHg in SBP or DBP could be clinically meaningful at population scale, but the directly observed clinical-outcome evidence is more concentrated in selected high-risk populations.

**Direct trial evidence: SSaSS (Neal et al., 2021)**

The canonical SSaSS report is Neal et al. (2021), *Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death*. SSaSS was an open, cluster-randomized controlled trial conducted in 600 villages across five provinces in China, with 20,995 enrolled participants. Villages were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to salt substitute or regular salt. The primary report described the intervention as 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride by mass, compared with control salt containing 100% sodium chloride. [70] A secondary description gives the formulation as approximately 70% sodium chloride and 30% potassium chloride, so the 75:25 primary-report formulation is retained as the principal value and the approximately 70:30 description is treated as a source discrepancy rather than combined with it. [70]

SSaSS enrolled people at high risk of stroke, including people with a previous stroke or people aged over 60 years with high blood pressure. At baseline, the mean age was 65.4 years, 49.5% of participants were female, 72.6% had a history of stroke, 88.4% reported a diagnosis of hypertension, and 79.3% were taking at least one blood-pressure-lowering drug. The reported medication distribution included calcium-channel blockers in 41.8%, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin-receptor blockers in 22.8%, diuretics in 11.5%, beta-blockers in 5.7%, and alpha-blockers in 0.9%. Mean baseline BP was 154.0/89.2 mmHg: SBP 154.0 mmHg and DBP 89.2 mmHg, reported as a pooled study-population summary rather than arm-specific values. [70]

The primary report gives a mean follow-up of 4.74 years, and the trial was designed around approximately 5 years of follow-up; this places SSaSS in the requested duration category of at least 12 months. [70] A secondary review describes a 31-month follow-up, but the supplied primary-report summaries identify 4.74 years as the relevant outcome follow-up. [72] An interim SSaSS report by Huang et al. was published in October 2018 and is a linked report from the same randomized experiment, not a separate trial. It reported BP-related data at 12, 24, and 36 months, with data available for 1,198, 2,789, and 1,163 participants at those respective assessments. [56]

**SSaSS blood-pressure and exposure findings**

The primary SSaSS result was a mean SBP difference of −3.34 mmHg (95% CI −4.51 to −2.18) over a mean 4.74-year follow-up. The supplied passage does not specify whether this is an endpoint-value difference or a difference in change from baseline, nor does it give the exact BP assessment timepoint. [70] A separate review reports the same trial’s SBP effect as approximately −3.3 mmHg (95% CI −4.5 to −2.2). [95]

The 2018 interim report found SBP 3.1 mmHg lower in the intervention group than in the control group (95% CI −4.5 to −1.7; P<0.001) at interim follow-up. DBP was not statistically different: the intervention-control difference was 0.1 mmHg (95% CI −0.7 to 1.0; P=0.766). The interim report stated that the overall BP effect was aligned with the original statistical-power assumption of a 3.0-mmHg difference. It also noted that the year-2 BP point estimate was smaller than the estimates at years 1 and 3, with wide confidence intervals; because urinary sodium and potassium effects remained relatively constant, the report suggested that the apparent year-2 discrepancy could reflect chance. [56]

The exposure measurements support the intended sodium-potassium mechanism but do not isolate the contribution of each component. One SSaSS summary reports that, in representative annual 24-hour urine samples, sodium intake was 0.35 g/day lower than in control participants, an 8% decrease from baseline, while potassium intake was 0.80 g/day higher, a 57% increase from baseline. The source inferred that participants replaced approximately 72% of regular salt with the substitute. [70] Another report describes the intervention population as consuming approximately 10.8 g/day of salt and 1,400 mg/day of potassium at baseline; salt substitution reduced salt intake by almost 1,000 mg/day and increased potassium intake by almost 1,000 mg/day. [95] The interim report found a 0.4-g lower 24-hour sodium excretion (95% CI −0.7 to −0.1; P=0.013) and a 0.7-g higher potassium excretion (95% CI 0.6–0.9; P<0.001). [56] These are different reports and timepoints, not operands to be added together.

A later report identified in the source material, published in 2023 and therefore outside the March 2022 evidence cutoff, gives a different SSaSS-labeled SBP estimate of −7.1 mmHg (95% CI −10.5 to −3.8; Bonferroni-corrected P<0.001). Its sensitivity analyses reported −6.9 mmHg (95% CI −10.3 to −3.5) per protocol, −7.0 mmHg (95% CI −10.4 to −3.6) after excluding facilities from Xi’an, and −6.6 mmHg (95% CI −10.3 to −2.8) after imputation for missing data. The later report also states that SBP was measured three times with an OMRON HEM-7136 device under American Heart Association guidance. Because this report is post-cutoff and its supplied passage does not establish the same endpoint basis or assessment timepoint as the Neal 2021 result, it is not combined with the primary estimate. [70]

**Mechanism, heterogeneity, and safety**

Salt substitutes generally combine potassium chloride with sodium chloride, thereby lowering sodium chloride exposure and increasing potassium exposure relative to regular salt. The supplied evidence supports a pathway from reduced sodium and increased potassium to lower BP, but it does not establish the separate causal contribution of each component to clinical outcomes. In SSaSS, the relative increase in potassium intake was larger than the relative decrease in salt intake, and the source explicitly states that the contributions of potassium supplementation versus sodium reduction were not determined. [95]

A composition meta-regression reported that each 10% lower proportion of sodium chloride in the substitute was associated with an additional SBP reduction of 1.53 mmHg (95% CI −3.02 to −0.03; P=0.045) and an additional DBP reduction of 0.95 mmHg (95% CI −1.78 to −0.12; P=0.025). This is a cross-study association, not a randomized decomposition of the intervention effect. [70] Outside tightly controlled research, the substitute may increase potassium proportionally more than it reduces sodium, so formulation and actual household use are important conditions for transportability. [70]

Taste and adherence can also affect implementation. Potassium chloride may have a bitter or metallic taste, although more than 80% of people in cited acceptability studies could not distinguish regular salt from substitutes containing no more than 30% potassium chloride. [22,93] The Li trial’s larger effects in subsidized villages with greater reported use provide an RCT-associated example of adherence-related heterogeneity. [77] Other plausible sources of variation include baseline BP, hypertension status, concurrent antihypertensive therapy, cointerventions, dietary habits, genetic background, recruitment setting, BP measurement protocol, and differences in intervention formulation. One synthesis noted that most included studies did not adequately report antihypertensive drugs, diet, or physical activity, and that only three trials enrolled normotensive populations while most enrolled hypertensive or mixed populations. [72]

Safety evidence is reassuring but incomplete. In SSaSS, serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalemia were not significantly more frequent with 75% sodium chloride/25% potassium chloride than with regular salt, and there was no evidence of a significant increase in sudden death plausibly caused by hyperkalemia-related arrhythmia. [72] Another summary likewise reports no evidence of increased hyperkalemic events in the SSaSS intervention group. [95] However, most salt-substitution trials excluded people at greatest risk of hyperkalemia, including people with reduced kidney function or elevated baseline potassium, and the source calls for larger studies in populations with a high prevalence of hyperkalemia. [72] These safety findings therefore support benefit-risk interpretation in the studied population but do not establish safety for untreated chronic kidney disease or other high-risk groups.

**Direct versus modeled evidence**

The SSaSS SBP, stroke, major cardiovascular-event, all-cause mortality, vascular-mortality, acute-coronary-syndrome, and nonfatal-stroke findings are directly observed randomized outcomes. The Chang cardiovascular-mortality finding is also directly observed, although it comes from a very small-cluster, specialized setting. These findings must be kept separate from cardiovascular risk inferred by applying an external SBP relationship.

The external RCT meta-analysis cited in the supplied sources reported that each 10-mmHg reduction in SBP was associated with RR 0.80 for major cardiovascular disease events (95% CI 0.77–0.83), RR 0.83 for coronary heart disease (95% CI 0.78–0.88), RR 0.73 for stroke (95% CI 0.68–0.77), RR 0.72 for heart failure (95% CI 0.67–0.78), and a 13% reduction in all-cause mortality with RR 0.87 (95% CI 0.84–0.91). These are external BP-lowering-drug associations, not direct SSaSS outcomes. [70]

The community-salt analysis illustrates the distinction: because the community RCTs primarily reported BP rather than cardiovascular events, the page applied external evidence and estimated approximately 2%–3% lower cardiovascular risk, approximately 2.5% lower cardiovascular-death risk under one linear application, and alternative estimates of 5% or 6% under different external associations. It also described a rough best guess ranging from 2.5% to 21% depending on the evidence source. These are modeled, assumption-dependent estimates and are not direct trial mortality results. [77] The supplied SSaSS evidence does not itself provide a complete external model, risk horizon, baseline absolute risk, or SSaSS-specific modeled percentage; its observed SBP effect should therefore not be presented as a directly observed cardiovascular-risk reduction.

Finally, SMASH should not be included in the RCT synthesis. It reported a 1.8-mmHg SBP decline with P=0.04 and a 3.1-mmHg DBP decline with P<0.001 over a 5-year baseline-to-endline period, but it had no control group. These uncontrolled changes are contextual evidence only and cannot establish a salt-substitute treatment effect. [70]

**Direct cardiovascular and mortality findings: SSaSS**

The Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS; Neal et al., 2021; published 29 August 2021) randomized 20,995 participants in rural China and found lower stroke, major cardiovascular events, and total mortality with salt substitute over a mean 4.74-year follow-up. [61]

| Outcome | Salt substitute | Regular salt | Effect estimate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Stroke | 29.14 per 1,000 person-years | 33.65 per 1,000 person-years | RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.96; *P*=0.006 [61] |
| Major cardiovascular events* | 49.09 per 1,000 person-years | 56.29 per 1,000 person-years | RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.94; *P*<0.001 [61] |

These are directly observed randomized outcomes, not cardiovascular risks inferred from blood-pressure modeling. SSaSS recorded 4,172 deaths, complete final vital-status ascertainment, and more than 99% ascertainment of scheduled non-fatal-event person-years; mean follow-up was 4.74 years and the median was 5.12 years. [61] The retained report excerpts provide event rates and person-time denominators rather than arm-specific event numerators for every endpoint.

**Blood-pressure findings and clinical meaning**

SSaSS produced an observed salt-substitute-minus-regular-salt SBP difference of **−3.34 mmHg across follow-up (95% CI −4.51 to −2.18 mmHg)**. [61] The trial population had a high baseline risk profile: mean age was 65.4 years, 72.6% had prior stroke, 88.4% reported hypertension, 79.3% used at least one blood-pressure medication, and mean baseline BP was approximately 154/89 mmHg. [61] A related SSaSS report gives pooled baseline values of 154.0 (23.5) mmHg SBP and 89.2 (14.0) mmHg DBP for all 20,995 participants; the values are not arm-specific. [40]

The earlier Heart review synthesized **21 RCTs**—15 hypertensive trials involving 2,016 participants, two normotensive trials involving 163 participants, and four mixed-population trials involving 5,224 participants. Across those trials, low-sodium salt substitutes reduced SBP by **7.81 mmHg (95% CI −9.47 to −6.15; *P*<0.00001)** and DBP by **3.96 mmHg (95% CI −5.17 to −2.74; *P*<0.00001)**. The review also found lower urinary sodium and higher urinary potassium, but no statistically significant effect on detected hypertension or overall mortality; certainty was low to very low for most outcomes and formulations were heterogeneous. [97] The earlier estimate should not be treated as an independent estimate of the post-2018 evidence or simply averaged with SSaSS.

Later reviews provide useful cross-checks but were published after the requested cutoff and are not used to define the eligible through-March-2022 RCT set. One review-of-reviews reported SBP estimates ranging from **−4.61 to −8.87 mmHg** and DBP estimates from **−1.42 to −4.04 mmHg**; estimates were not pooled across reviews because of substantial primary-study overlap. [96] A later network meta-analysis reported a K-salt SBP effect of **−4.00 mmHg (95% CI −5.67 to −2.32)** and DBP effect of **−1.42 mmHg (95% CI −2.57 to −0.26)** from 12 studies involving 23,993 participants, while its wider review included 34 RCTs and 37,063 participants from 15 countries.  These review-level results support a consistent direction of BP lowering, but their populations, formulations, comparators, dates, and study overlap differ from the present cutoff-defined evidence set.

The mechanism is biologically coherent but should not be confused with proof of mediation. SSaSS reports a mean reduction in 24-hour urinary sodium of **15.2 mmol**, an increase in urinary potassium of **20.6 mmol**, and the **−3.34-mmHg SBP** difference. Sodium reduction and potassium supplementation independently lower BP and may have synergistic effects; the investigators considered the cardiovascular benefit consistent with a BP-mediated mechanism. [61] The intervention formulation is reported somewhat differently across permitted SSaSS reports: the trial outcome report describes approximately **70% sodium chloride and 30% potassium chloride by mass**, each with a ±10% manufacturing tolerance, whereas another SSaSS report gives **75% NaCl and 25% KCl**; both identify the control as **100% NaCl**. These values are retained rather than reconciled. [75,44]

### Earlier and smaller mortality studies

**Earlier evidence was encouraging but mixed.** Before SSaSS, mortality findings came from much smaller or institutionally concentrated studies. Their directions were often favorable, but their precision and protection against bias were less consistent.

| Trial or evidence set | Mortality finding | Uncertainty or design feature | Resulting interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chang 2006 | Cardiovascular-death HR 0.59 | 95% CI 0.37–0.95; only 5 kitchens | Favorable institutional signal |
| Zhang 2016 | 48.90 versus 82.73 deaths/1,000 person-years | Preliminary nursing-home analysis | Large reported rate contrast |
| Zhou/Sun 2021 follow-up | All-cause HR 0.81; CVD HR 0.58 | CIs 0.46–1.42 and 0.26–1.32 | Direction favorable; imprecise |
| Hernandez 2019 mortality synthesis | No statistically significant difference | 2 RCTs; 2,159 participants | Earlier evidence did not establish survival benefit |

Sources: Chang original trial, quoted in the detailed account [12], Zhang preliminary report [15], Sun follow-up [10], and Hernandez mortality summary [23].

**Chang provided an early cardiovascular signal.** Its potassium-enriched salt was associated with a 41% lower age-adjusted cardiovascular-death hazard. However, the intervention was allocated through only five retirement-home kitchens. A large number of diners does not remove the vulnerability created by a small number of independently randomized units, particularly when recruitment continues after allocation.

**Zhang reported a large mortality contrast.** All-cause mortality was 48.90 versus 82.73 per 1,000 person-years. The crude relative rate reduction is a calculated 40.9%: 1−48.90/82.73. This is not a cluster-adjusted hazard ratio and should not be interpreted as more definitive than SSaSS merely because its point estimate is larger. [15]

**The renal finding was an albuminuria signal.** Zhang also reported urinary microalbumin-to-creatinine ratios of at least 30 mg/g in 16.2% of intervention participants versus 26.8% of controls after three years. That supports an associated renal-marker difference, not proof that potassium substitution improves all aspects of kidney function or is appropriate for established potassium-retaining kidney disease. [15]

**The Zhou follow-up retained substantial uncertainty.** Among 428 participants with mortality information, there were 22 versus 28 all-cause deaths and 9 versus 16 cardiovascular deaths. Unadjusted hazard ratios were 0.81 for all-cause mortality and 0.58 for cardiovascular mortality, with both confidence intervals including no effect. [10]

**Adjustment produced stronger subgroup signals.** After adjustment for age and alcohol consumption, the cardiovascular-death hazard ratio was 0.43 overall, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.18–1.02 and P=0.056. Among hypertensive participants it was 0.38, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.16–0.92. Adjusted stroke-mortality hazard ratios were 0.26 overall and 0.25 among hypertensive participants. [10]

**Post-trial exposure complicates the explanation.** Both groups were advised to reduce salt after the original intervention. Subsequent BP and continued substitute use were not measured, and mortality follow-up selected intervention completers. The findings therefore support a possible longer-term benefit of original assignment but do not establish a ten-year effect of continuously assigned salt substitution.

**Earlier null mortality findings were not proof of equivalence.** Hernandez’s pre-SSaSS mortality synthesis did not establish a statistically significant survival benefit, despite a favorable pooled BP effect. The transition to convincing outcome evidence occurred when SSaSS supplied a large randomized population, several years of exposure, and enough events to detect a moderate relative difference.

### What later pooled outcome analyses add

**Later pooling supports, but does not replace, SSaSS.** Yin’s clinical-outcome synthesis reported the following estimates from the earlier trial literature.

| Outcome | Contributing trials | Relative estimate, 95% CI | Relative reduction |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Total mortality | 5 | RR 0.89 (0.85–0.94) | 11% |
| Cardiovascular mortality | 3 | RR 0.87 (0.81–0.94) | 13% |
| Cardiovascular events | 2 | RR 0.89 (0.85–0.94) | 11% |

These are the published estimates in the Yin synthesis [1], not a newly recalculated March 2022 meta-analysis.

**One trial supplies most outcome information.** The five clinical-outcome trials included 24,306 participants, of whom 20,995 were in SSaSS—a calculated 86.4% by participant count. Participant share is not identical to statistical weight, but it conveys the concentration of the evidence. [22]

**Sensitivity analysis clarifies that dependence.** After excluding SSaSS, pooled all-cause mortality remained directionally favorable at RR 0.88, but its 95% confidence interval widened to 0.76–1.02; P=0.08. Vascular mortality remained favorable in two remaining studies, RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.40–0.87. The latter result rests on a much narrower and less generalizable evidence base than the full analysis. [6]

**Different synthesis methods produce different magnitudes.** Cochrane estimated a cardiovascular-mortality rate ratio of 0.77, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.60–1.00, across three RCTs and 23,200 participants. That is a 23% point-estimate reduction, but its interval reaches no effect. Differences in included reports, event metrics, follow-up, and statistical weighting mean it should not be treated as a contradiction of Yin’s 13% estimate or as independent replication. [4]

**Other cardiovascular composites are less precise.** Cochrane’s category of miscellaneous cardiovascular events contained only 18 events across five small trials and yielded RR 1.22, 95% CI 0.49–3.04. Those heterogeneous events are not equivalent to SSaSS’s prespecified major cardiovascular composite. The wide interval supports neither a reliable benefit estimate nor a reliable harm estimate for that miscellaneous category. [4]

**Mortality takeaway.** By March 2022, randomized evidence supported lower stroke, major cardiovascular-event, all-cause-death, and vascular-death rates in the SSaSS population; extension to other populations is supported more strongly by BP consistency than by multiple equally definitive mortality trials.

### Age: younger than 60 versus 60 years or older

**Peru supplies the clearest exact-age anchors.** Its fully adjusted age-specific results distinguish participants younger than 40, aged 40–59, and aged at least 60 years.

| Age group | SBP difference, mmHg | SBP 95% CI | DBP difference, mmHg | DBP 95% CI |
|---|---:|---|---:|---|
| <40 years | −0.94 | −1.54 to −0.34 | −0.27 | −0.80 to +0.27 |
| 40–59 years | −1.17 | −1.98 to −0.35 | −1.01 | −1.67 to −0.36 |
| ≥60 years | −2.17 | −3.67 to −0.68 | −1.18 | −2.14 to −0.22 |

Source: Peru age-stratified results [24].

**A bounded younger-group proxy is possible.** Any nonnegative weighted average of the two published younger-group SBP point estimates must lie between −1.17 and −0.94 mmHg. This provides an explicitly descriptive <60-year proxy without inventing the proportions in the two younger age groups. The corresponding DBP proxy lies between −1.01 and −0.27 mmHg.

| Requested age category | SBP point estimate or proxy, mmHg | SBP uncertainty basis | DBP point estimate or proxy, mmHg | DBP uncertainty basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| <60 years | Estimated bound: −1.17 to −0.94 | Published-band CI envelope: −1.98 to −0.34 | Estimated bound: −1.01 to −0.27 | Published-band CI envelope: −1.67 to +0.27 |
| ≥60 years | −2.17 | Reported 95% CI: −3.67 to −0.68 | −1.18 | Reported 95% CI: −2.14 to −0.22 |

**The proxy is not a collapsed model.** For example, the younger SBP reduction magnitude is bounded by \(0.94w+1.17(1-w)\), where \(0≤w≤1\). This arithmetic describes possible averages of published point estimates; it does not reproduce a newly fitted age-<60 regression coefficient. Likewise, the envelope of reported confidence limits is not a formal confidence interval for a combined younger subgroup.

**Older participants had the larger point estimate.** Relative to the bounded younger proxy, the ≥60-year SBP reduction was descriptively 1.00–1.23 mmHg greater: 2.17−1.17 to 2.17−0.94. For DBP the analogous contrast was 0.17–0.91 mmHg. These contrasts indicate a plausible older-group advantage within this trial, but do not establish a statistically significant interaction.

**The wider evidence is not uniform.** Hu’s household trial described greater home-SBP lowering among participants aged at least 60. Wang’s small trial reported an older-group SBP/DBP contrast of 18.15/10.86 mmHg, although the younger-age label and P-value symbols in that account are ambiguous. By contrast, the India trial reported no detected age modification, and Geleijnse found no age modification within its restricted 55–75-year range. [14][11][13][7]

**Alternative cutoffs answer a different question.** Jafarnejad’s analysis separated trials by mean age <65 versus ≥65 years, finding SBP reductions in both categories. It cannot be relabeled as an individual-participant <60 versus ≥60 comparison. A trial with mean age 59 can contain many participants over 60, while a trial with mean age 66 can contain younger participants. [18]

**Age is linked to other modifiers.** Older populations may differ in salt sensitivity, arterial stiffness, baseline pressure, medication use, and cardiovascular risk. These mechanisms make a larger older-group response plausible, but they also mean that a cross-trial age association cannot isolate aging itself as the cause. The broader Yin analysis reported consistency across age-defined subgroups, rather than convincing evidence that benefit was confined to older people. [1]

**Age-subgroup takeaway.** Both younger and older adults can benefit; some trials show larger older-group point estimates, but a formal treatment interaction at exactly age 60 is not established by the comparisons presented here.

### Sample size: fewer than 300 versus at least 300 participants

**Smaller studies reported larger BP effects.** Peng et al.’s 2014 review, whose eligibility required at least six months of follow-up, reported the requested sample-size comparison.

| Trial sample size | SBP reduction, mmHg | DBP reduction, mmHg | Within-stratum significance |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| <300 participants | 7.3 | 3.1 | P<0.05 |
| ≥300 participants | 3.0 | 0.7 | P<0.05 |

Source: Peng et al., 2014 subgroup summary [25].

**The descriptive difference is substantial.** The small-study subgroup showed 4.3 mmHg more SBP lowering and 2.4 mmHg more DBP lowering: 7.3−3.0 and 3.1−0.7. However, statistical significance within both strata does not test whether the strata differ significantly from one another. A between-stratum claim requires uncertainty for the contrast itself, not simply two favorable P values.

**Sample size is not a biological treatment.** Randomizing fewer people does not make a salt substitute more potent. The contrast can instead arise because small studies enroll more selected hypertensive patients, provide more intensive food replacement, test different formulations, or measure outcomes over different periods. Selective publication of unusually large small-study results is another possible explanation.

**The selected-hypertension review cannot resolve size modification.** Jafarnejad’s 11 comparisons involved trial sizes ranging from 5 to 238 participants. Its pooled reductions of 8.87 mmHg SBP and 4.04 mmHg DBP therefore describe small-study evidence, not a direct small-versus-large comparison. They should not be used to infer what an otherwise identical ≥300-person trial would show. [18]

**Large trials provide an effectiveness anchor.** SSaSS’s 3.34-mmHg SBP difference is compatible with a modest effect sustained during ordinary household use over several years. Its cardiovascular benefit demonstrates why a smaller average BP effect in a large pragmatic trial can still be more clinically consequential than a larger BP effect in a small, tightly controlled experiment.

**Weighting sensitivity supports the overall conclusion.** Cochrane’s fixed-effect estimate, which gives relatively greater influence to larger studies, was −3.92 mmHg SBP, compared with −4.76 mmHg under its random-effects analysis. Both were favorable. The magnitude changes, but the overall conclusion does not depend on small trials alone. [4]

**Sample-size takeaway.** The <300-participant studies reported larger BP reductions, but sample size is best interpreted as a signal of study differences and possible small-study effects—not as an independent determinant of treatment efficacy.

### Study duration: less than 12 versus at least 12 months

**Benefit is present in both duration categories.** The clearest numerical comparison from named trial reports is the following span of reported point estimates. These spans compare observable trial contrasts; they are not pooled duration-specific effects.

| Requested duration category | SBP reduction span, mmHg | DBP reduction span, mmHg | Example with reported SBP uncertainty |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| <12 months | 4.58–7.60 | 1.14–3.50 | India: 4.58; 95% CI 2.97–6.20 |
| ≥12 months | 1.29–10.31 | 0.76–5.45 | SSaSS: 3.34; 95% CI 2.18–4.51 |

The short-duration span uses India, Zhao, and Geleijnse. The longer-duration span includes Peru, CSSS, Hu, the Zhou family trial, SSaSS, and Wang, using numerical contrasts where reported. Sources are the corresponding [13][9][7][16][8][14][10][2], and Wang [11] reports.

**The pooled directional signal favors shorter trials.** The later Yin account described larger average BP reductions in studies lasting less than 12 months. However, the named-trial comparison demonstrates substantial overlap: some long trials produced small average changes, while others produced large changes. Duration alone therefore does not explain the magnitude of response. [17]

**CSSS contradicts inevitable attenuation.** Its SBP difference increased over time, reaching 5.4 mmHg at exactly 12 months. Under the requested definition, this belongs in the ≥12-month category. This within-trial trajectory is stronger evidence about persistence than simply comparing an unrelated three-month study with a five-year study. [8]

**Continued use differs from withdrawal.** Geleijnse found a BP difference during the 24-week intervention, but approximately 25 weeks after participants stopped receiving the assigned salt and foods, the difference was no longer detectable. The post-withdrawal estimates were 0.8 mmHg SBP and −1.0 mmHg DBP, with confidence intervals crossing zero. [7]

**SSaSS establishes multi-year effectiveness.** The average 3.34-mmHg SBP difference persisted across a mean 4.74 years and accompanied fewer clinical events. Imperfect adherence, regular salt consumed outside the home, and some substitute use among controls plausibly reduced the achieved between-group contrast. This is compatible with durable benefit despite a smaller long-term average effect. [2]

**Other duration cutoffs should remain separate.** Jafarnejad compared <3 with ≥3 months, reporting SBP reductions of 7.93 versus 9.78 mmHg and DBP reductions of 5.02 versus 3.71 mmHg. Those results do not answer the requested <12 versus ≥12-month question and do not show uniformly greater benefit with either shorter or longer exposure. [18]

**The clinical distinction is sustained exposure.** Duration can influence adherence, contamination, cumulative cardiovascular benefit, and the number of observed events. A formal interaction using exactly <12 and ≥12 months would be needed to claim that duration independently modifies the BP response. The positive long-term trials already establish that benefit need not disappear after one year.

**Duration-subgroup takeaway.** Shorter studies may report larger average BP reductions, but continued substitution can sustain blood-pressure lowering and cardiovascular benefit for years; withdrawal is a different exposure state.

### Safety and applicability

**Potassium-related safety requires patient selection.** In SSaSS, serious events attributed to hyperkalemia occurred at 3.35 versus 3.30 per 1,000 person-years, giving a rate ratio of 1.04, 95% CI 0.80–1.37; P=0.76. This supports no detected excess of serious clinical hyperkalemia in the studied population, not universal safety under all prescribing conditions. [2]

**Known high-risk conditions were excluded.** SSaSS excluded participants when they or household members had known serious kidney disease, used potassium-sparing diuretics, or took potassium supplements. Routine biochemical kidney-function prescreening and serial serum-potassium measurements were not performed. Thus, the safety outcome predominantly concerns clinically recognized serious events rather than all biochemical potassium elevations.

**The confidence interval remains clinically relevant.** Although centered near no difference, the SSaSS interval extends to a 37% relative increase in the measured hyperkalemia outcome. Moreover, only two events were definite or probable; possible events were added to the analysis. These details favor careful eligibility assessment rather than an unrestricted “no risk” interpretation.

**Not all relevant medications were prohibited.** Approximately 22.8% of SSaSS participants used an angiotensin-converting–enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin-receptor blocker. The trial therefore should not be summarized as excluding every medication that can affect potassium. Potassium-sparing diuretics, potassium supplements, renal function, and the patient’s overall clinical situation require distinct consideration. [2]

**Blood potassium increases slightly on average.** Cochrane estimated a mean increase of 0.12 mmol/L, 95% CI 0.07–0.18, across six trials and 784 participants. A small population-average increase can coexist with a clinically important risk in susceptible individuals; averages do not define the response of a patient with impaired potassium excretion. [4]

**Generalizability follows exposure and baseline risk.** BP benefit has been demonstrated across several countries, but the strongest event evidence comes from high-risk rural Chinese adults. People at lower baseline cardiovascular risk would generally experience fewer prevented events in absolute terms even if the relative effect were similar. People whose sodium comes mainly from unchanged processed foods may also achieve a smaller dietary contrast.

**Safety takeaway.** Potassium-enriched salt substitution is a reasonable adjunctive prevention strategy for appropriately selected patients, with particular attention to kidney disease, potassium-sparing treatment, supplements, and the household members sharing the product.

## Part 3: Cardiovascular risk reduction estimated from SBP lowering

### The conversion and its result

**The main estimate is 12.0% relative benefit.** The calculation uses the pooled SBP reduction of 4.61 mmHg and Verdecchia et al.’s estimate of 13% lower composite cardiovascular risk per 5-mmHg SBP reduction. That analysis included 30 blood-pressure–lowering trials and 221,024 participants. Its composite endpoints included myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death, sometimes with heart failure added. [3]

**The arithmetic is transparent.** Under an approximately linear relationship over this modest BP range:

\[
\text{Estimated relative cardiovascular risk reduction}=(4.61/5)\times13\%=11.986\%\approx12.0\%.
\]

**The estimate is conditional, not observed.** It assumes that the general blood-pressure–risk relationship applies to salt-substitute interventions, that the average SBP reduction is sustained over a relevant period, and that the cardiovascular endpoint is sufficiently comparable. It does not identify the fraction of the actual salt-substitute benefit causally mediated by SBP.

**Uncertainty comes from both inputs.** The pooled SBP confidence interval corresponds to a reduction magnitude of 3.14–6.07 mmHg. Verdecchia’s conversion estimate was 13% per 5 mmHg, with a 95% interval of 8%–19%. Combining the lower endpoints and upper endpoints provides the following sensitivity scenarios.

| Scenario | SBP reduction, mmHg | Risk reduction per 5 mmHg | Calculation | Estimated relative reduction |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| Lower sensitivity case | 3.14 | 8% | 3.14/5 × 8% | 5.0% |
| Central case | 4.61 | 13% | 4.61/5 × 13% | 12.0% |
| Upper sensitivity case | 6.07 | 19% | 6.07/5 × 19% | 23.1% |

Inputs: pooled SBP estimate [1] and general BP-risk conversion [3].

**This range is not a combined confidence interval.** The 5.0%–23.1% range deliberately combines endpoint assumptions. It describes sensitivity to the BP effect and conversion factor, without asserting a formal joint probability distribution or including every uncertainty about adherence, population transfer, and endpoint definition.

### Comparison with directly observed outcomes

**The projection is broadly compatible with trial findings.** A modeled 12% cardiovascular reduction is close in scale to SSaSS’s observed 13% reduction in major cardiovascular-event rates and the later pooled estimate of approximately 11% fewer cardiovascular events. Agreement in scale supports clinical plausibility, but it is not an independent validation because the studies and assumptions overlap.

| Evidence type | BP input or observed contrast | Cardiovascular result | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| General BP-risk projection | Pooled SBP reduction 4.61 mmHg | Estimated 12.0% lower composite risk | Model-based expectation |
| SSaSS direct randomized result | SBP reduction 3.34 mmHg | 13% lower major-event rate | Observed intervention effect |
| SSaSS direct vascular mortality | Same assigned intervention | 13% lower vascular-death rate | Observed cause-specific outcome |
| Later pooled clinical evidence | Multiple earlier trial reports | 11% fewer cardiovascular events | Retrospective pooled context |

Sources: Verdecchia conversion [3], SSaSS [2], and Yin pooled outcomes [1].

**Using SSaSS’s own BP gives a different prediction.** Applying the same linear benchmark to 3.34 mmHg yields an estimated 8.7% relative cardiovascular reduction: 3.34/5×13%. The trial observed a 13% reduction in major cardiovascular-event rates. That difference does not establish an additional potassium-specific vascular mechanism; it can also reflect uncertainty in the general conversion, endpoint differences, and the measurement of average BP exposure.

**The intervention effect includes several pathways.** Salt substitution changes sodium intake, potassium intake, and potentially other dietary behavior. Randomization estimates the effect of assignment to that strategy. Isolating the SBP-mediated share would require a mediation analysis with additional assumptions; the observed cardiovascular rate ratio cannot simply be declared the causal effect of a particular number of millimeters of mercury.

**Conversion choice changes the numerical expectation.** The India report used a more conservative benchmark of at least 10% lower cardiovascular risk per 5-mmHg SBP reduction. Applied to its observed 4.58-mmHg reduction, that gives an estimated 9.2% relative reduction: 4.58/5×10%. This reinforces a practical conclusion of a modest, potentially important benefit rather than one uniquely precise percentage. [13]

**Larger BP estimates imply larger projections.** Applying the 13%-per-5-mmHg benchmark to Hernandez’s 7.81-mmHg pooled SBP reduction gives approximately 20.3% relative cardiovascular benefit: 7.81/5×13%. That should not replace the central estimate for a general population, because it imports the larger effect from a different and earlier trial mixture. The conversion is only as representative as the BP input.

**SBP and DBP projections must not be added.** They are correlated measures of the same hemodynamic response, not independent treatments. Calculating a risk reduction from each and summing them would double count much of the expected benefit.

### What the estimate means for clinical decisions

**Relative benefit is not absolute benefit.** A 12% relative reduction means multiplying an otherwise applicable cardiovascular risk by approximately 0.88. The number of events prevented depends on baseline risk and the duration over which that risk is defined. A high-risk secondary-prevention population and a young normotensive population can therefore have similar proportional effects but very different absolute gains.

**SSaSS provides the strongest absolute anchor.** Its observed major-event difference was 7.20 fewer events per 1,000 person-years, and its all-cause-death difference was 5.33 fewer deaths per 1,000 person-years. These are measured rate differences in a defined high-risk population, not general-population forecasts or cumulative five-year risk differences. [2]

**The practical conclusion is adjunctive prevention.** For patients resembling those studied, salt substitution can lower BP while preserving much of the ordinary cooking routine, and SSaSS demonstrates that this can translate into fewer serious events. Its place is alongside indicated medication, broader dietary improvement, and management of other cardiovascular risks—not as a reason to discontinue effective treatment.

**Applicability depends on three conditions.** The expected benefit will be larger when the product replaces a meaningful share of habitual salt, the lower BP is maintained, and baseline cardiovascular risk is substantial. The judgment would change if those conditions are absent or if potassium retention creates a clinically important competing risk.

**How the numbers should be read.** Primary efficacy reports are limited to publications through 31 March 2022; later reviews provide explicitly labeled retrospective context, with underlying searches ending in August 2021. The 29-family catalog is an enumeration of named randomized evidence, not a newly certified March-cutoff census. Dashes distinguish fields outside the numerical detail supplied by the cited accounts from measured zeros. Baseline means, endpoint contrasts, change scores, home BP, and person-year rates retain their respective measurement bases. The younger-age bounds, duration spans, raw-change differences, and SBP-risk scenarios are labeled calculations or descriptive comparisons, not reconstructed trial results.

**Overall judgment.** Salt substitutes are effective for adult blood-pressure lowering, and by March 2022 a large randomized trial had demonstrated reduced stroke, cardiovascular events, and mortality. Approximately 12% relative cardiovascular benefit is a reasonable SBP-based central projection, while the direct event evidence and potassium-safety assessment should guide decisions for individual patients.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Applying the observed SSaSS lowering**

SSaSS (Neal et al., 2021) provides the primary observed operand: across follow-up, salt substitute minus regular salt lowered SBP by **3.34 mmHg** (mean difference −3.34 mmHg; 95% CI, −4.51 to −2.18), so the modeled input is \(\Delta SBP=3.34\) mmHg lower over the trial’s mean **4.74-year** follow-up [27]. SSaSS included **20,995 participants**, whose mean age was **65.4 years**; its median follow-up was **5.12 years** [61]. Thus, under the prespecified classifications, SSaSS is an age-**≥60 years**, sample-size-**≥300**, and duration-**≥12 months** study.

The SBP estimate depends on the assessment basis. An interim analysis of repeated measurements in a random subsample reported a difference of **−2.65 mmHg** (95% CI, −4.32 to −0.97) over monitoring through three years [54]. Other accounts round the final effect to approximately **3.3 mmHg** [95] or **3.4 mmHg** during the trial [43]. These estimates should not be averaged: the primary final-trial estimate of −3.34 mmHg is used here. Similarly, aggregate rather than SSaSS-specific estimates—such as a 2019 review’s average net SBP effect of **−5.58 mmHg** (95% CI, −7.08 to −4.09) [99] and a later 26-RCT synthesis reporting **4.76 mmHg** lower SBP over 56 days to five years [71]—are not used as the SSaSS operand.

**Primary SBP-to-cardiovascular-risk calculation**

The primary external relation is the RCT meta-analysis by Ettehad et al. (2016), which reported that every **10 mmHg** lower SBP was associated with a relative risk (RR) of **0.80** (95% CI, 0.77–0.83) for major cardiovascular disease events. The same source reported RRs of 0.83 for coronary heart disease, 0.73 for stroke, 0.72 for heart failure, and 0.87 (95% CI, 0.84–0.91) for all-cause mortality in the populations studied [70]. The evidence comes from blood-pressure-lowering drug trials rather than salt-substitution trials; their durations ranged from **0.5 to 6.5 years** [70]. I therefore use the SSaSS mean follow-up of 4.74 years as the reporting horizon, not as a claim that the external model specified that horizon.




Thus, the best single modeled estimate is an **approximately 7.2% relative reduction in major cardiovascular-event risk over 4.74 years**, conditional on transporting the external SBP relationship to SSaSS. The result is modeled, not a directly observed SSaSS endpoint.

The 4.0%–11.1% interval is a transparent corner-of-inputs sensitivity range, not a formal joint 95% confidence interval: it treats the SBP and external-model limits as independent and does not incorporate uncertainty about the functional form, transportability, or endpoint differences.

**Direct trial outcomes versus modeled risk**

The modeled 7.2% estimate must remain separate from SSaSS’s direct cardiovascular findings. At the observed trial follow-up, SSaSS reported lower fatal or nonfatal stroke risk (RR **0.86**, 95% CI, 0.77–0.96), major cardiovascular events (RR **0.87**, 95% CI, 0.80–0.94), and all-cause mortality (RR **0.88**, 95% CI, 0.82–0.95) [27]. Vascular death also favored substitution (RR **0.87**, 95% CI, 0.79–0.96), whereas nonfatal stroke was less precise (RR **0.90**, 95% CI, 0.80–1.01); nonfatal acute coronary syndrome favored substitution (RR **0.70**, 95% CI, 0.52–0.93) [27]. A reported secondary analysis described a **13% reduction in cardiovascular deaths** after approximately 4.7 years (95% CI, 4%–21%) [70]. These are observed trial effects with their own endpoint definitions and denominators; they should not be presented as confirmation that the SBP-only calculation equals the direct mortality effect.

**Alternative model checks**

An age-specific comparative-risk model based on pooled epidemiological evidence—**1.38 million participants, 65,000 cardiovascular events, and 99 cohorts**—reported SBP-related reductions for first ischemic-heart-disease and stroke events [125,148]. Because SSaSS’s mean age was 65.4 years, the 65–74-year inputs are most relevant: **2.5% lower ischemic-heart-disease risk per 1 mmHg** lower SBP (95% CI, 2.2%–2.8%) and **3.1% lower stroke risk per 1 mmHg** (95% CI, 2.8%–3.3%) [100]. If these per-mmHg estimates are treated as multiplicative and applied to 3.34 mmHg:

- ischemic heart disease: \(1-0.975^{3.34}\approx\mathbf{8.1\%}\) (approximately 7.1%–9.0% using the reported limits);
- stroke: \(1-0.969^{3.34}\approx\mathbf{10.0\%}\) (approximately 9.0%–10.6%).

These are sensitivity calculations from observational pooled associations, not SSaSS-specific outcomes. The same model estimated that a **1 g/day** population salt reduction would lower SBP by approximately **1.2 mmHg** (95% UI, 0.5–2.2), corresponding to approximately **4% lower ischemic-heart-disease risk** and **6% lower stroke risk**, with projections of about **9 million CVD events prevented by 2030** under specified achievement and persistence assumptions [125,148]. Those projections are for the Chinese population and are not a trial-specific absolute-risk estimate.

Other population models illustrate why absolute results depend heavily on baseline risk and context. An India comparative-risk model used an SSaSS-based SBP effect of **−2.82 mmHg** at age 65 years (95% uncertainty interval, −4.75 to −0.89) and projected approximately **214,000 CVD deaths averted per year** (95% uncertainty interval, 92,764–353,054), equivalent to about **9.0%** of annual CVD deaths (range, 3.9%–14.8%) in its modeled nationwide intervention [103]. This was a population projection using Global Burden of Disease and pooled-risk inputs—not an SSaSS-specific relative-risk estimate—and it also modeled potential hyperkalemia-related harms.

A separate implementation model reported an adjusted cardiovascular-death RR of **0.97** (25th–75th percentile, 0.96–0.98), using an intervention-impact duration of **4.25 years** (range, 4–6) and a baseline annual cardiovascular-death rate of **254 per 100,000** (range, 200–300) [70]. Its BP-based sense checks produced approximately **5%** and **6%** reductions, but it combined salt substitution with broader dietary-salt-reduction assumptions and used proxy endpoints; it is therefore not used as the SSaSS SBP calculation.

The principal assumptions are that the SBP effect is causal, that the external association is log-linear over 3.34 mmHg, that the effect transfers from drug-induced BP lowering to potassium-enriched salt substitution, and that the effect applies over the selected horizon. Indirect models may omit benefits or harms not mediated by SBP [70]. SSaSS enrolled people at high stroke risk in rural China, so applying its relative effect—or an SBP model—to average-risk populations elsewhere is uncertain; absolute benefits would generally differ with baseline risk [70]. A 2023 prediction model in 306 hypertensive participants included baseline SBP but lacked external validation and was not suitable for transport [102]. Likewise, DECIDE-Salt reported a 7.1-mmHg SBP reduction in 1,612 participants but was published on **2023-04-01**, after the March 2022 cutoff, so it is excluded from the eligible RCT evidence set [76].

**Observed SBP input and direct outcomes**

SSaSS (Neal et al., 2021) provides the principal eligible input: among 20,995 randomized participants, the final intervention-minus-control SBP difference was −3.34 mmHg (95% CI −4.51 to −2.18) over a mean 4.74-year follow-up; it therefore belongs to the ≥300-participant and ≥12-month categories [34]. An interim 2-year estimate was −2.65 mmHg (95% CI −4.32 to −0.97) [85], whereas the China population model used a 2.82-mmHg reduction at age 65 years (95% CI 0.89–4.75) from 4,705 interim participants [74]. These are distinct observed or model-input quantities, not values to be pooled.

SSaSS directly observed lower risks over approximately five years: stroke RR 0.86, major cardiovascular events RR 0.87, and all-cause death RR 0.88 [44]. Corresponding reported absolute risks were 2.91% versus 3.37% for stroke, 4.91% versus 5.63% for cardiovascular events, and 3.93% versus 4.46% for death—absolute differences of 0.46, 0.72, and 0.53 percentage points, respectively [78]. These are direct trial outcomes, not SBP-modeled estimates. The mean participant age was 65 years; no direct BP estimate or interaction test for age <60 versus ≥60 years was supplied, and one trial cannot establish cross-trial sample-size or duration moderation [36].

**Population projection and limitations**

A separate China comparative-risk model used age- and sex-specific SBP distributions, 24 strata, a theoretical minimum-risk exposure of 110–115 mmHg, and potential-impact fractions. It projected 461,000 fewer CVD deaths per year (10.9%; 95% UI 4.7–16.8) and 743,000 fewer nonfatal CVD events (7.9%; 95% UI 3.2–13.4); uncertainty came from 1,000 Monte Carlo runs [74]. Coverage sensitivity ranged from 5.6% (3.3–8.7) at 50% coverage to 14.9% (9.2–23.5) at 150% coverage [74]. The same model estimated approximately 11,000 additional hyperkalemia-related deaths but about 450,000 net fewer CVD deaths annually, so these projections are not individual-level SSaSS absolute risks [74].

The mechanism is plausibly combined sodium reduction and potassium increase: potassium may promote natriuresis, reduce sympathetic tone, and alter vascular tone; high potassium can also dephosphorylate NCC and increase distal sodium delivery [98]. BP-independent potassium effects remain possible, limiting SBP-only translation [98]. Generalization beyond high-risk rural Chinese communities, where household salt is a major exposure, is uncertain [36]. SSaSS found no significant excess hyperkalemia, but limited kidney monitoring and exclusion of serious kidney disease and potassium-sparing-drug users constrain safety transportability [78].
