# Inorganic Corrosion Inhibitors: Mechanisms, Protective Reactions, and Industrial Applications

**Four families provide complementary protection.** Through early 2023, the four requested families can be distinguished as follows: metal-based materials protect by sacrificial action or metallic barriers; metal oxides reinforce passive films, obstruct transport, or block cathodic reactions; phosphates promote passivation and insoluble phosphate deposits; and silicates form protective silicate-rich films or serve in protective coating systems. Their principal applications include water-treatment systems, marine and oil-and-gas equipment, reinforced concrete, and metal-surface pretreatment. These mechanisms overlap, so selecting a family requires matching its chemistry to the substrate and service environment, not assuming that one family is universally superior. [1][2]

**Three parts connect chemistry to applications.** Part One explains the four families, their named examples, and their operating principles. Part Two presents the five requested reaction cases, distinguishing source-reported reactions from explicitly derived stoichiometric balances. Part Three provides the application table and explains why the listed examples suit their respective environments.

| Inhibitor family | Dominant protective action | Representative examples | Protective surface feature | Main application contexts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Metal-based | Sacrificial current or metallic barrier | Zn, Al, Mg and their alloys | Metallic coating; associated corrosion products | Pipelines, marine structures, tanks |
| Metal oxide-based | Passivation, barrier action, cathodic blocking | PbO, Pb₃O₄, ferrites, CeO₂ | Oxide-rich or oxide/hydroxide layer | Steel primers, galvanized steel, aluminum alloys |
| Phosphate-based | Insoluble precipitation and passivation | Na₃PO₄, zinc phosphate, calcium phosphate | Metal-phosphate/oxide film | Concrete reinforcement, phosphating, water treatment |
| Silicate-based | Silicate-rich films and coating protection | Sodium, potassium and lithium silicates | Hydrated silicate/silica-rich layer | Cooling water, saline drilling fluids, coatings |

**Classification describes materials, not one mechanism.** This report uses the four families requested in the question as a practical material-based organization. Conventional corrosion science also classifies inhibitors as *anodic*, *cathodic*, or *mixed*, according to which electrochemical reactions they suppress. Those labels describe behavior rather than additional material families: a phosphate, for example, may suppress metal dissolution, obstruct cathodic reactions, or do both under different conditions. [1]

**Physical form changes the terminology.** A chemical inhibitor, in the narrower industrial sense, is added to a corrosive environment in relatively small quantities. Sacrificial anodes and metallic coatings are normally classified separately as corrosion-protection methods. They are included here within the requested “metal-based” family, while retaining the distinction between an expendable metallic electrode, an applied coating, and a dissolved inhibiting chemical. [3]

## Part One: Main Types and Mechanisms of Action

**Protection interrupts an electrochemical circuit.** In aqueous steel corrosion, anodic regions release iron ions and electrons, while cathodic regions consume those electrons through reactions such as oxygen reduction. Water or moist soil conducts ionic current, and the metal provides the electronic pathway. Inhibiting dissolution, slowing the cathodic reaction, restricting transport, or supplying electrons from a sacrificial metal can therefore reduce steel corrosion. [2]

**A film must perform a function.** Merely forming an oxide, hydroxide, or precipitate does not establish protection. A useful layer must sufficiently restrict reactive species or charge transfer and remain effective during exposure. Its continuity, adhesion, porosity, chemical stability, and ability to reform at damaged sites matter more than its chemical name alone. [2]

### 1. Metal-based inhibitors

**Active metals can protect another metal.** Zinc, aluminum, and magnesium, usually as suitably formulated alloys, are the principal examples of sacrificial-anode materials. When electrically connected to steel in an appropriate electrolyte, the active metal oxidizes preferentially and supplies electrons to the steel. The steel becomes cathodically polarized, reducing its tendency to dissolve anodically. [4]

| Named material or subcategory | Main protective role | Typical deployment | Reference-backed applications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zinc and zinc-alloy anodes | Sacrificial cathodic protection | Electrically attached anode | Ship hulls, offshore pipelines, tanks |
| Aluminum-alloy anodes | Sacrificial cathodic protection | Expendable alloy electrode | Offshore structures and saltwater equipment |
| Magnesium-alloy anodes | Sacrificial cathodic protection | Buried or immersed anode | Onshore pipelines, hot-water heaters |
| Zinc and zinc-alloy coatings | Barrier plus sacrificial protection | Galvanized or plated surface | Steel structures and reinforcing bars |
| Aluminum-flake pigments | Overlapping barrier particles | Pigmented coating | Coated steel substrates |

The three anode materials and their principal environments are described by the American Galvanizers Association [4]; zinc coatings and alloy variants are discussed by Doi and Hiromoto, 2022 [5], and aluminum-flake barriers by the Reclamation review [2].

**Electrical continuity enables sacrificial protection.** Electrons travel through the metallic connection from the anode to the protected structure; ions carry the corresponding current through the surrounding electrolyte. Placing magnesium or zinc near steel without completing this circuit does not reproduce sacrificial-anode protection. Equally, an anode that is consumed, disconnected, or unable to deliver sufficient current will no longer provide the intended protection. [6]

**The protected steel supports reduction.** In oxygenated water, the cathodic reaction on steel commonly consumes dissolved oxygen and produces hydroxide ions. The externally supplied electrons reduce the need for iron oxidation to sustain that reaction. Protection thus comes from changing the steel’s electrochemical operating condition; it does not require a magnesium-containing film to coat the entire steel surface. [7]

**Anode selection depends on the electrolyte.** Magnesium has a more negative potential than zinc or aluminum and can provide a larger driving voltage. This makes magnesium useful in relatively high-resistivity onshore environments, including buried pipelines. Zinc and aluminum alloys are commonly used in conductive saltwater environments such as hulls, offshore pipelines, production platforms, and marine cooling equipment. [4]

**More driving voltage is not automatically better.** A highly active anode may deliver useful current where electrical resistance is substantial, but excessive cathodic polarization can promote hydrogen evolution. Conversely, a metal that develops a strongly passive surface may cease to deliver adequate protective current. Practical selection therefore balances operating potential, current capacity, dissolution behavior, and compatibility with the electrolyte. [8]

**Zinc coatings combine two mechanisms.** An intact galvanized coating separates steel from water, oxygen, and chloride. At a scratch or exposed edge, the surrounding zinc can become the sacrificial anode and protect adjacent exposed steel. This combination explains why galvanized steel differs fundamentally from a coating that protects only while it remains completely intact. [5]

**Alloy coatings modify the protective system.** Named developments include Zn–Al, Zn–Al–Mg, and Zn–Ni coatings. These remain metal-based protection systems, but their behavior depends on alloy composition, microstructure, and the corrosion products formed during exposure. Their names alone do not establish a universal performance order across atmospheric, concrete, and immersed service. [5]

**Barrier metals behave differently at defects.** Less-reactive metallic coatings, exemplified by nickel-plated steel, principally isolate the substrate from its environment. Here “barrier” means separation from the electrolyte, not electrical insulation: nickel is electrically conductive. If a defect exposes the steel and establishes an unfavorable galvanic couple, the protection mechanism differs from that of sacrificial zinc. [3]

**Aluminum can shield or sacrifice.** Aluminum-alloy anodes are designed to remain electrochemically active under their intended service conditions, whereas aluminum-flake pigments can protect primarily by forming overlapping, scale-like barriers in a coating. Aluminum’s own oxide/hydroxide film also influences its dissolution. These are related uses of the same element, but they should not be described as one interchangeable mechanism. [2][6]

**Corrosion products can supplement metallic protection.** Zinc initially forms products such as zinc hydroxide and zinc oxide; in chloride-containing solutions, zinc hydroxychloride can also occur. These deposits can restrict further zinc dissolution, extending the barrier contribution of the coating. However, their effectiveness depends on their morphology and environment: a heterogeneous or loosened product layer is not equivalent to a continuous passive film. [9]

**Concrete produces a distinctive zinc film.** In saturated calcium hydroxide solution, Doi and Hiromoto investigated calcium hydroxyzincate, Ca[Zn(OH)₃]₂·2H₂O, abbreviated CHZ. They described it as an insulating layer that reduces the exposed zinc area. Increasing oxygen availability accelerated its formation, illustrating how oxygen can assist formation of a protective corrosion product even though it also participates in the initial electrochemical reaction. [5]

**Film chemistry changes measured performance.** After seven days of CHZ formation at an oxygen partial pressure of 0.5 MPa, the reported corrosion current density was approximately 5 μA/cm², compared with approximately 25 μA/cm² for polished zinc. The CHZ-treated specimens also showed substantially smaller weight changes during the specified wet/dry cycling test. These observations connect a named surface product to reduced zinc corrosion under the study conditions. [5]

**Magnesium is useful but condition-sensitive.** Riaz and colleagues tested magnesium–mild-steel couples in tap water and 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate over 40–80 °C. Magnesium protected steel in tap water, but became passive in the bicarbonate solution; polarity reversal made the steel anodic, with damaging behavior reported at 60–80 °C. This counterexample demonstrates why the operating electrolyte can outweigh a simple comparison of standard electrode potentials. [10]

**Alloy quality affects useful current.** Copper, iron, and nickel impurities can establish local cathodic sites within magnesium anodes, encouraging self-corrosion rather than useful external current. A 2017 comparison of four magnesium-anode products linked high impurity contents to poorer electrochemical properties and anode efficiency. The engineering requirement is therefore a qualified anode alloy, not merely a material labeled “magnesium.” [11]

**Metal-rich primers extend the same principle.** Pathak and colleagues reviewed magnesium-rich primers for aerospace aluminum alloys, including the commercial Aerodur® 2100 MgRP system developed in 2007. Electrically connected magnesium particles supply sacrificial current, while the binder and subsequent magnesium corrosion products provide additional barrier effects. Particle contact, pigment loading, adhesion, and hydrogen evolution determine whether this combination remains beneficial. [7]

**Typical applications follow current delivery.** Metal-based protection is particularly relevant to marine hulls, offshore structures, oil-and-gas pipelines, storage tanks, heat exchangers, hot-water heaters, and galvanized reinforcement. Separate anodes are attractive where an electrolyte and a reliable electrical connection are present; metallic coatings are useful where continuous surface coverage can also reduce environmental access. [7][6]

**Takeaway: preserve the intended electrochemical role.** Metal-based protection succeeds when the active metal continues supplying adequate current, or when its coating maintains an effective barrier, without passivation or excessive polarization undermining the system.

### 2. Metal oxide-based inhibitors

**Oxides protect through surface-controlled processes.** Metal oxide-based materials may form or reinforce passive layers, provide an insoluble barrier, promote substrate passivation, or obstruct cathodic reactions. This family includes inhibitive oxide pigments and engineered oxide-rich surface treatments. It does not imply that every naturally occurring oxide, including ordinary rust, provides effective corrosion resistance. [2]

| Specific compound or subcategory | Chemical identity | Principal protective contribution | Typical environment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Litharge | PbO | Binder-dependent inhibitive chemistry | Legacy protective paints |
| Red lead | Pb₃O₄ | Inhibitive products and passivation | Legacy structural-steel primers |
| Ferrite pigments | Mixed iron oxides | Alkaline interface and passivation | Anticorrosive steel coatings |
| Cerium dioxide | CeO₂ | Insoluble deposits and cathodic blocking | Aluminum and galvanized-steel systems |

The lead oxides and ferrites are described in the 2017 Reclamation review [2]; cerium oxide is supported by studies on galvanized steel [12] and aluminum 3003 [13].

**Passive films suppress metal dissolution.** A sufficiently compact and adherent oxide-rich layer restricts the transport processes needed for continued anodic dissolution. In engineered surface treatments, oxidation can be produced thermally, chemically, or electrochemically. The resulting resistance depends on the film’s composition and uniformity, rather than simply on increasing the amount of oxide present. [3]

**Pigments need not become continuous oxides.** An oxide pigment dispersed in a coating can protect through reactions with the binder or through changes in the chemistry of the coating–metal interface. Its role may therefore be indirect: the initial particle is the source of an inhibitive species, not necessarily the final material covering the steel. Reclamation’s review identifies this distinction as important for understanding red lead and several replacement pigments. [2]

**Lead oxides illustrate indirect inhibition.** Litharge and red lead are specific historical examples. In compatible oil-containing binders, lead compounds can form metal soaps—metal salts of organic acids—which subsequently generate inhibitive species capable of migrating toward defects. Red lead can also contribute to substrate passivation, so its action cannot be reduced to an inert pigment barrier. [2]

**Historical effectiveness does not justify new use.** Lead-based pigments are included because they are chemically important examples of this family, not because they are preferred modern choices. The Reclamation report describes their phaseout from its specifications because of health and environmental hazards. That documented institutional practice should not be confused with a claim that every jurisdiction regulates every oxide identically. [2]

**Ferrites offer a different oxide route.** The Reclamation review describes ferrite pigments as Fe₂O₃ combined with metals such as magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium, zinc, or manganese. In suitable binders, they can participate in metal-soap formation; they also create an alkaline interface favorable to steel passivation. Their protective behavior consequently combines pigment chemistry with the response of the steel surface. [2]

**Binder chemistry changes the dominant contribution.** Soap formation is relevant to compatible fatty-acid-containing binders, but should not be assigned automatically to every coating resin. The Reclamation review also discusses ferrite-pigmented epoxy coatings, where interfacial passivation remains important even though the binder does not normally generate the same soaps. This distinction explains why pigment performance cannot be transferred unchanged between resin systems. [2]

**Cerium deposits can block cathodic activity.** On aluminum, cathodic reactions can make the nearby solution alkaline. Cerium-containing oxide/hydroxide deposits can remain insoluble at these sites and impede the reduction reactions that sustain corrosion. By limiting cathodic activity and the associated local alkalization, they can reduce the conditions promoting localized aluminum dissolution. [13]

**The precursor differs from the protective product.** A cerium nitrate treatment supplies soluble cerium species, whereas the deposited protective material contains cerium hydroxides and oxides. The 2022 aluminum-3003 study identified Ce(OH)₃, Ce₂O₃, and CeO₂ in proportions that depended on treatment duration. Thus, a soluble rare-earth salt can act as a precursor to protection that ultimately resides in an oxide/hydroxide layer. [13]

**Longer treatment was not consistently better.** In that study, aluminum 3003 was treated in 50 mM Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O with 10 vol.% hydrogen peroxide at 50 °C. Subsequent electrochemical testing used 3.5% NaCl after nitrogen bubbling to remove dissolved oxygen. The two-minute treatment performed better than the much longer treatment, and microscopy provided a clear comparison of localized damage. [13]

| Surface treatment | Principal cerium condition | Approximate pit depth after testing |
|---|---|---|
| Untreated aluminum | No cerium treatment | 4 μm |
| Two-minute treatment | Ce(III)-rich hydroxide deposit | 1 μm |
| Sixty-minute treatment | More heavily oxidized deposit | 25 μm |

These microscopy results are reported in the aluminum-3003 cerium study [13].

**Reactive distribution matters alongside oxide quantity.** The authors attributed protection at suitable short treatment times to cerium species that could subsequently form insoluble deposits at active sites. Excessive treatment produced a different surface condition and more severe attack. The practical conclusion is not that CeO₂ is inherently harmful, but that the deposition process and the remaining capacity for local protective reactions matter. [13]

**Mixed pigments demonstrate formulation dependence.** Deflorian, Fedel, and Rossi studied mechanically treated CeO₂ and SiO₂ particles for galvanized steel in 0.1 M NaCl. Milling promoted useful pigment behavior, the combined particles were beneficial, and the effect increased under alkaline conditions. SiO₂ is a metalloid oxide rather than a metal oxide, so it is best identified here as a supporting component in an oxide-based formulation, not misclassified as a metal oxide itself. [12]

**Layered hydroxides add active functions.** Layered double hydroxides, or LDHs, are related hydroxide-based protective materials rather than simple metal oxides. Examples reviewed before the cutoff include ZnAl-LDH, MgAl-LDH, LiAl-LDH, CaAl-LDH, and NiAl-LDH systems. Their layered structure can provide a barrier, exchange interlayer anions, and carry inhibitors that become available when corrosion threatens the substrate. [14]

**Anion exchange can protect vulnerable sites.** With an appropriate interlayer composition, an LDH can absorb aggressive anions or release inhibiting anions. This adds a chemical response to the physical barrier. “Self-healing” in this context generally means restoring local corrosion resistance through inhibitor action or deposit formation, not regenerating metal already lost to corrosion. [14]

**Thicker layers can become more porous.** The LDH review describes a compact inner layer and a more porous outer platelet structure. Increasing thickness can reduce resistance if the additional material introduces greater porosity, and chloride exposure can attack the protective system. A dense, well-integrated coating can therefore outperform a thicker but more permeable one. [14]

**Early-2023 composites combined several functions.** An AA2024 study used a ZnAl-LDH layer containing vanadate and a cerium-doped silane sol–gel seal. In 3.5 wt.% NaCl, the composite reached a reported corrosion current density of 1.92 × 10⁻⁸ A/cm², while its low-frequency impedance modulus remained above 10⁵ Ω·cm² after two and fourteen days. The system combined inhibitor release with barrier layers rather than relying on an oxide particle alone. [15]

**Applications favor engineered coating interfaces.** Typical settings include structural-steel primers, galvanized steel exposed to saline environments, and aluminum-alloy treatments relevant to building, electrical, transport, and aerospace applications. Oxide-based protection is especially useful where the surface can be prepared and the protective layer integrated with a compatible coating system. Changes in pH, chloride exposure, particle treatment, or coating porosity can change the outcome. [2][15]

**Takeaway: engineer the protective interface.** Oxide-based materials are most effective when their insolubility, interfacial chemistry, and coating structure work together to suppress corrosion, rather than merely increasing the quantity of deposited solids.

### 3. Phosphate-based inhibitors

**Phosphates protect through controlled surface reactions.** Their central mechanisms are adsorption, stabilization of passive iron oxides, and precipitation of sparingly soluble metal phosphates. The protective film may contain iron, zinc, calcium, or mixed phosphates together with oxides and hydroxides. Which contribution dominates depends on phosphate speciation, metal-ion supply, oxygen, pH, and the location of precipitation. [16]

**Three forms serve different purposes.** Soluble phosphate salts are added to water or cementitious systems; sparingly soluble phosphate pigments are dispersed in coatings; and conversion treatments deliberately react with a metal surface to create a phosphate-containing layer. The same family therefore covers both chemicals delivered through a liquid and films manufactured before service. These forms should be compared by delivery and surface reaction, not treated as equivalent products. [2][17]

**Soluble examples include three established sodium compounds.** Sodium monofluorophosphate, disodium hydrogen phosphate, and trisodium phosphate are well-documented examples for reinforcing steel in ordinary Portland cement mortar. The 2021 review discusses both surface-applied migrating treatments and additions made during mortar preparation. It reports a comparative inhibition-efficiency order of MFP > DHP > TSP for the reviewed treatment comparisons. [16]

| Soluble phosphate | Formula | Abbreviation | Main documented use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium monofluorophosphate | Na₂PO₃F | MFP | Migrating or admixed mortar inhibitor |
| Disodium hydrogen phosphate | Na₂HPO₄ | DHP | Mortar inhibition and boiler pH control |
| Trisodium phosphate | Na₃PO₄ | TSP | Reinforcement and water-treatment programs |
| Ammonium dihydrogen phosphate | NH₄H₂PO₄ | APMB | Reinforcing-steel pore-solution studies |
| Sodium tripolyphosphate | Na₅P₃O₁₀ | STPP | Reinforcing-steel inhibition studies |

The sodium compounds and tripolyphosphate are discussed in the 2021 phosphate review [16]; ammonium phosphate is examined in the 2020 reinforcing-steel study [18], and boiler orthophosphates are documented in a 1991 patent [19].

**Solid examples broaden the family.** Zinc phosphate is widely used as an inhibitive pigment and conversion-coating constituent. Calcium phosphate supplies another important phosphate chemistry, while iron and manganese phosphates are central to industrial phosphating. Strontium aluminum polyphosphate and zinc aluminum polyphosphate illustrate modified pigment systems intended for protective coatings. [2][20]

| Compound or subcategory | Representative identity | Physical role | Typical application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zinc phosphate | Zn₃(PO₄)₂ | Inhibitive pigment or conversion film | Steel coatings and pretreatment |
| Hydrated zinc phosphate | Zn₃(PO₄)₂·4H₂O, hopeite | Conversion-coating constituent | Phosphated metal surfaces |
| Calcium phosphate | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ | Precipitate or inhibitive pigment | Coatings and cementitious systems |
| Iron phosphates | Fe₃(PO₄)₂ or FePO₄ | Surface reaction products | Steel passivation and pretreatment |
| Manganese phosphates | Manganese-containing phosphate films | Conversion coating | Gears, shafts, engine parts |
| Strontium aluminum polyphosphate | SAPP | Coating pigment | Galvanized-steel cut edges |
| Zinc aluminum polyphosphate | ZAPP | Coating pigment | Protective coating formulations |

Named phases and applications are supported by the phosphate review [16], the industrial phosphating guide [17], and Morsch et al., 2018 [20].

**Phosphate films can be chemically layered.** Studies of reinforcing steel describe an inner iron oxide/hydroxide region and an outer region containing iron-phosphate complexes. The inner layer limits dissolution and transport, while the outer precipitated products can cover vulnerable sites and hinder chloride attack. Consequently, observing an oxide-rich film does not exclude phosphate participation in its formation or stabilization. [16]

**Soluble sodium is usually a delivery ion.** When Na₃PO₄ is added to a steel-containing system, the final protective deposit need not be sodium phosphate. Phosphate can react with iron ions released from the substrate to form an iron-phosphate-containing surface layer. This distinction is essential when connecting the name of a treatment chemical to the film-forming equations in Part Two. [21]

**Phosphate speciation follows the local pH.** The 2021 review describes H₂PO₄⁻-rich conditions around pH 3–6, HPO₄²⁻-rich conditions around pH 8–12, and increasing importance of PO₄³⁻ above approximately pH 12. These are gradual acid–base transitions rather than rigid boundaries. The same phosphate inventory can therefore participate in differently written precipitation reactions in an acidic phosphating bath and an alkaline concrete pore solution. [16]

**Oxidation state changes the iron product.** Ferrous iron, Fe²⁺, can form Fe₃(PO₄)₂; ferric iron, Fe³⁺, can form FePO₄. A 1974 study found ferric phosphate precipitation in acidic phosphate solution and increasing replacement by ferric hydroxide as pH rose. Real corrosion films can contain mixed phases, so “iron phosphate” should not automatically be assigned the formula FePO₄ in every environment. [22]

**Electrochemical labels are condition-dependent.** Simple phosphates can promote anodic inhibition in oxygenated steel systems by supporting protective oxide formation. In the presence of calcium or zinc ions, insoluble phosphate complexes can also precipitate at cathodic regions and obstruct cathodic reactions. This provides a chemical basis for reports describing phosphate inhibition as anodic, cathodic, or mixed without requiring that one description apply universally. [20]

**A pore-solution study demonstrates dose response.** In 0.6 M chloride-contaminated simulated concrete pore solution at 25 °C, a 2022 carbon-steel study reported increasing inhibition efficiency with Na₃PO₄ concentration. Potentiodynamic polarization classified the response as predominantly anodic, while surface analyses supported a film containing iron oxides and insoluble phosphate products. The results are summarized below. [21]

| Na₃PO₄ concentration | Added phosphorus-to-chloride molar ratio | Reported inhibition efficiency |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 mol/L | 0.083 | 80.8% |
| 0.10 mol/L | 0.167 | 87.5% |
| 0.30 mol/L | 0.500 | 90.4% |
| 0.60 mol/L | 1.000 | 91.7% |

The ratios are calculated as added Na₃PO₄ concentration divided by 0.6 mol/L chloride, with one phosphorus atom per Na₃PO₄ formula unit. They describe total added phosphorus, not the concentration of free PO₄³⁻ after speciation and precipitation.

**The highest dose showed diminishing returns.** Increasing Na₃PO₄ from 0.05 to 0.60 mol/L raised the reported efficiency, but the improvement narrowed at the higher concentrations. This is consistent with a system approaching more complete surface protection rather than one in which protection increases proportionally with every additional mole. The finding supports dose optimization within the stated pore-solution experiment, not direct adoption of those concentrations as concrete admixture specifications. [21]

**Mortar adds a transport problem.** In a pore-solution test, the inhibitor already contacts the steel. In hardened mortar, a surface-applied inhibitor must move through the pore network while reacting with the surrounding cement phases. A compound that performs well at an exposed steel surface may consequently deliver a smaller useful quantity to embedded reinforcement. [16]

**The reviewed penetration profiles differed substantially.** For selected migrating-treatment profiles, the 2021 review reported penetration depths of 1114 μm for MFP, 380 μm for DHP, and 126 μm for TSP. It also estimated effective diffusion coefficients from those profiles. These results help explain the reported comparative treatment behavior, although penetration and corrosion inhibition remain different measured quantities. [16]

| Inhibitor | Reported profile penetration | Estimated effective diffusion coefficient | Reviewed efficiency order |
|---|---|---|---|
| MFP | 1114 μm | 1.8 × 10⁻⁸ cm²/s | First |
| DHP | 380 μm | 6.7 × 10⁻⁹ cm²/s | Second |
| TSP | 126 μm | 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ cm²/s | Third |

**Precipitation can help or hinder delivery.** Calcium-phosphate or apatite formation can fill pores and reduce the movement of aggressive species, but it can also consume phosphate before it reaches the reinforcing steel. The same general tendency to form insoluble solids is therefore beneficial at the steel interface and potentially detrimental along the delivery pathway. This is why a high phosphate content in the outer mortar does not by itself demonstrate an adequately protected reinforcement surface. [16]

**The mortar ranking is not universal.** The review’s overall efficiency order, MFP > DHP > TSP, applies to the treatment comparisons it discusses. Some individual conditions favored DHP, and the separate 2022 pore-solution experiment demonstrated substantial protection by TSP. The apparent contrast is explained by differences in exposure, delivery, matrix reactions, and assessment endpoint rather than by assigning each compound an immutable rank. [16][21]

**Conversion treatments deliberately consume surface metal.** Phosphating begins with controlled reaction between the substrate and an acidic phosphate-containing bath. Local changes in metal-ion concentration and acidity then allow insoluble phosphate products to deposit. The resulting layer is poorly conducting and can slow electrochemical reactions, while also providing a surface suitable for subsequent finishing. [17]

**Three industrial processes serve different objectives.** KIESOW’s November 2016 guide distinguishes iron, zinc, and manganese phosphating. Its terminology calls iron phosphating “non-layering” and zinc and manganese phosphating “layering”; “non-layering” is a process classification, not a statement that no protective surface layer forms. Their operating ranges and intended finishes differ materially. [17]

| Phosphating process | Main substrates | Typical bath pH | Typical temperature | Principal purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iron phosphate | Steel, zinc; selected aluminum applications | 4–6 | 25–65 °C | Indoor paint or powder-coating base |
| Zinc phosphate | Steel and zinc | 2.2–3.2 | 50–85 °C | More demanding painted exposure |
| Manganese phosphate | Steel | 2.2–2.4 | 90–95 °C | Lubrication and running-in of parts |

**Conversion layers usually complement another finish.** Phosphate coatings can be porous, particularly crystalline conversion layers. Their practical value often comes from combining surface conversion with paint, powder coating, oil, or another sealing system. The 2023 downhole review notes both this partial standalone protection and the use of phosphate conversion coatings to improve casing-connection sealing in oil production. [3]

**Manganese phosphate is not a paint substitute.** Its typical function is associated with wear, lubrication, and running-in of parts such as camshafts and gears. KIESOW specifically distinguishes this from painting and powder-coating pretreatment. Selecting it solely because it belongs to the same phosphate family as zinc phosphate would therefore overlook the actual manufacturing objective. [17]

**Pigments release protection gradually.** In a zinc-phosphate-pigmented coating, limited water entry can release phosphate-containing species that support passivation or precipitation at the metal interface. Low solubility helps retain the pigment but can delay protective-film development. Reclamation’s review explains that this activation lag can contribute to different impressions of performance in accelerated testing and slower field exposure. [2]

**Commercial impurities can alter the first film.** Morsch and colleagues examined SAPP at galvanized-steel cut edges and found that early cathodic film formation was associated with soluble strontium-carbonate impurities. These captured zinc as a zinc-carbonate layer before later polyphosphate deposition. The result shows why a rapidly protective commercial pigment cannot always be interpreted from its nominal phosphate composition alone. [20]

**Applications span both surfaces and liquids.** The strongest directly documented examples include concrete-reinforcement protection, steel phosphating, protective paints, and galvanized-steel cut-edge inhibition. Phosphates also participate in cooling-water formulations and coordinated boiler phosphate/pH programs. In these water systems, their usefulness depends on controlling bulk precipitation and operating chemistry as well as encouraging protective surface reactions. [16][23][19]

**Takeaway: precipitation must occur usefully.** Phosphates are effective when they reach the intended interface and support a persistent protective layer, rather than being consumed prematurely or producing detrimental deposits elsewhere.

### 4. Silicate-based inhibitors

**Silicates form environment-dependent protective films.** Soluble silicates can adsorb at metal surfaces and participate in hydrated silicate-, silica-, oxide-, and hydroxide-rich deposits. Their alkalinity can also favor steel passivation. In coating formulations, silicates may function as binders or inhibitive pigments, while zinc-rich silicate coatings additionally derive sacrificial protection from metallic zinc. [24][2]

| Specific example or subcategory | Physical role | Principal protective contribution | Documented context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium silicate hydrate, Na₂SiO₃·9H₂O | Dissolved additive | Silicate-rich surface film | Saline drilling-fluid research |
| Lithium silicates | Soluble coating binder | Inorganic film formation | Waterborne silicate coatings |
| Potassium silicates | Soluble coating binder | Inorganic film formation | Silicate and mineral coatings |
| Zinc silicate pigments | Coating pigment | Binder-dependent inhibition | Protective paint systems |
| Calcium phosphosilicate | Inhibitive pigment | Inhibitive products and alkalinity | Oleoresinous coatings |
| Borosilicate-derived systems | Pigment/binder reaction products | Release of inhibitive species | Protective coating systems |
| Zinc-rich silicate coatings | Metallic-zinc coating composite | Cathodic plus barrier protection | Steel and drill-pipe protection |

Examples are described in the 2016 saline-fluid study [24], the 2018 silicate-coating overview [25], the Reclamation pigment review [2], and the 2023 downhole review [3].

**Commercial water glass has variable composition.** “Sodium silicate” covers soluble silicate products with different silica-to-alkali ratios, not one universal molecular formulation in solution. Sodium metasilicate hydrate is a specific example, whereas commercial water-glass solutions can contain different distributions of dissolved and polymerized silicate species. Consequently, equal product concentrations do not necessarily deliver equal alkalinity or film-forming behavior. [24]

**Anodic sites provide iron for deposits.** The proposed mechanism in the 2016 saline-fluid study involves silicate transport toward anodically active regions and reaction with Fe²⁺ to produce an iron-silicate-containing film, represented as FeSiO₃. By covering reactive regions, the deposit restricts continued iron dissolution. This is a proposed surface mechanism rather than proof that every silicate-treated steel surface consists of a single crystalline FeSiO₃ phase. [24]

**Cathodic alkalinity enables additional deposits.** Calcium and magnesium in the surrounding water can participate in precipitates where local cathodic reactions increase hydroxide concentration. The same study proposed films containing calcium and magnesium silicates, silica, and calcium/magnesium hydroxides alongside iron silicate. Such mixed deposits can restrict both anodic and cathodic reactions, explaining why silicate protection may extend beyond a purely anodic description. [24]

**Surface deposition competes with bulk precipitation.** A thin adherent deposit at the metal can help inhibit corrosion, while precipitation in the circulating liquid can produce suspended solids, scale, or obstructive deposits. Calcium and magnesium are therefore not simply helpful or harmful: their effect depends on where and how their compounds form. Earlier cooling-water disclosures specifically recognized scaling risks when alkali-metal silicates were used in hard water. [26]

**A saline-fluid experiment provides concrete evidence.** The 2016 study tested high-purity Na₂SiO₃·9H₂O at 250–2000 mg/L on AISI 1020 carbon steel. The solution contained 3.5 mass% NaCl, 800 mg/L MgCl₂, and 600 mg/L CaCl₂, with continuous aeration and filtration during the gravimetric tests. Four coupons were used to assess reproducibility. [24]

**The reported optimum was study-specific.** A concentration of 1250 mg/L gave the best gravimetric performance among the tested concentrations. The reported results show a strong reduction in annualized corrosion rate during the short immersion periods, with little additional improvement above that concentration. They establish a useful laboratory example, not a general dosage for every drilling fluid or industrial water. [24]

**The methods did not give identical responses.** In the continuously filtered gravimetric setup, precipitates were removed from the circulating solution. The static electrochemical cell lacked that filtration, allowing calcium/magnesium-containing deposits and colloids to accumulate on the electrodes. The authors consequently found the electrochemical results less conclusive than the weight-loss results, showing how deposit formation can change both corrosion behavior and its measurement. [24]

**This difference has practical significance.** A silicate formulation may reduce metal loss while also generating solids that require operational management. Conversely, a deposit covering an electrochemical probe can alter the measured response without establishing that the entire system has acquired a durable protective film. The appropriate judgment connects corrosion reduction with deposit location and operating conditions, rather than selecting the most favorable isolated measurement. [24]

**Silicate pigments use another chemical route.** Reclamation describes silicate pigments containing metals such as calcium, strontium, and zinc. In compatible oleoresinous binders, these materials can form metal soaps whose subsequent products release inhibitive anions. Increased alkalinity near the substrate supplies an additional passivating contribution. [2]

**Zinc silicate is not metallic zinc.** A zinc-containing silicate pigment should not automatically be assigned sacrificial-anode behavior. Sacrificial protection requires metallic zinc that can oxidize and deliver electrons through an electrical pathway. A zinc-rich silicate coating contains such metallic particles within a silicate-based coating system; the silicate designation alone does not establish that mechanism. [2][3]

**Zinc-rich silicates combine family functions.** The 2023 downhole review describes their use in oil production since the 1970s, including drill-pipe protection. Their attraction includes cathodic protection from the zinc network, adhesion, hardness, and high-temperature capability, with thermal stability reported up to approximately 400 °C for the reviewed systems. The same review identifies a pH service range of approximately 5–10, making chemical compatibility decisive in acidic or strongly alkaline environments. [3]

**Alkali-silicate coatings cover several substrates.** Lithium, sodium, and potassium silicates are identified as soluble binder chemistries in waterborne silicate coatings. Silicate-emulsion formulations can be adapted to steel, galvanized steel, aluminum, concrete, masonry, and previously painted mineral surfaces. Where an organic emulsion is present, the complete formulation is a hybrid rather than an entirely inorganic film. [25]

**Concrete coating does not equal rebar inhibition.** Applying a silicate-containing coating to concrete can alter the exposed surface or its resistance to environmental access. That is distinct from demonstrating that dissolved silicate reaches embedded steel and inhibits its electrochemical reactions. This distinction keeps the broad substrate applications of silicate coatings separate from the direct phosphate-reinforcement evidence discussed earlier. [25][16]

**Applications follow water chemistry and film stability.** The family is relevant to cooling-water formulations, selected boiler-water formulations, saline water-based drilling-fluid research, and protective coatings for metals and mineral substrates. Performance changes with pH, silicate ratio, dissolved oxygen, calcium/magnesium concentration, temperature, chloride, and flow. These variables determine whether the treatment forms useful surface protection, undesirable deposits, or both. [24][23]

**Takeaway: control both films and deposits.** Silicate-based protection is most credible when the intended surface film forms under the actual water chemistry and bulk precipitation remains compatible with equipment operation.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Classification convention and evidence boundary**

This section uses the four categories requested by the user as the primary, chemistry-based taxonomy: metal-based, metal oxide-based, phosphate-based, and silicate-based inhibitors. An inorganic corrosion inhibitor is treated here as a mineral or inorganic chemical that changes interfacial electrochemical reactions or produces a protective layer, precipitate, or passivating film; an oxide or metal-containing material is not counted automatically merely because it is a corrosion product, filler, coating component, or adsorbent [31]. Electrochemical labels such as *anodic*, *cathodic*, and *mixed-type* cut across the four chemical categories rather than replacing them [130].

Some compounds occupy more than one descriptive boundary. Zinc phosphate, for example, is both a zinc-containing compound and a phosphate inhibitor; it is counted primarily under phosphate-based inhibitors, with its zinc-related cathodic contribution noted separately. Oxide nanoparticles dispersed in an epoxy are reported as oxide-based active phases in a coating formulation, not as free dissolved inhibitors. A bulk sacrificial anode is a galvanic-protection device and is treated separately from dissolved inhibitor chemistry in Part Two. The report-wide evidence boundary remains early 2023; later sources below are identified only as qualification or discovery evidence, not as final period support.

**Metal-based inhibitors**

**Metal-based inhibitors reduce corrosion mainly by passivating the corrosion-cell interface and by forming protective films at anodic or cathodic sites.** In the clearest documented water-treatment example, zinc acts primarily as a cathodic inhibitor, while orthophosphate supplies anodic inhibition; their combination reduces the corrosion rate by lowering the effective activity of both electrodes [130]. Zinc-containing programs can also be combined with phosphates, polyphosphates, or phosphonates; the phosphonate component helps control unwanted zinc deposits and mineral scale, while zinc supplies the cathodic contribution [130].

The mechanism is condition-dependent. Oxidizing passivators such as chromates, nitrites, and molybdates can promote a thin, adherent oxide film on steel, whereas non-oxidizing oxyanions such as phosphate, tungstate, and silicate generally depend more strongly on oxygen availability and the formation of an insoluble surface film [106]. If the film is penetrated or dissolves, or if the inhibitor concentration is insufficient, localized corrosion and pitting can accelerate rather than decline; passivating inhibitors are also not generally effective against hydrogen-sulfide-induced corrosion [106].


- **Zinc ions and zinc-containing formulations**, including zinc combined with phosphates, polyphosphates, or HEDP; zinc is identified specifically as a cathodic inhibitor [130].
- **Chromates**, including the historical pigment/inhibitor example **strontium chromate (SrCrO₄)** [92]. This is a classification example rather than an unrestricted modern recommendation: the cited evidence identifies hexavalent chromate as toxic, carcinogenic, and restricted under REACH [92].
- **Molybdates**, including sodium molybdate and molybdate-based passivating programs [130]. Molybdate can also appear in mixed formulations and therefore should not be treated as a mutually exclusive category from other oxyanion inhibitors.

Other inorganic candidates—including cerium compounds, tungstate, decavanadate, and additional multivalent-metal salts—are documented in the supplied evidence, but their placement depends on whether classification is based on the metal cation, the oxyanion, the film formed, or the final formulation [96][98]. Lanthanide salts likewise remain an adjacent inorganic category rather than an automatically validated member of the four requested types [89].

**Typical applications and limits**

The strongest directly supported application context here is **industrial cooling-water treatment**. Metal-based programs are documented for heat exchangers, recirculating-water piping, process-cooling equipment, and open recirculating cooling systems [130]. Zinc/HEDP formulations are described for aggressive waters with low hardness, alkalinity, and total dissolved solids, particularly where chromate is prohibited but low zinc concentrations remain acceptable [130]. The cited water-treatment source gives an example of an allowable zinc concentration in an outfall as low as **1.0 ppm**; the period and jurisdiction are not stated, so this value should not be generalized as a universal regulatory limit [130].

Where zinc is unacceptable, inorganic phosphate treatment may replace or supplement it in open recirculating systems. This illustrates an important practical limitation: the metal-based mechanism is often delivered through a mixed program rather than through a single isolated compound. Environmental discharge limits, heavy-metal concerns, water hardness, alkalinity, dissolved oxygen, pH, and scale-forming ions can determine whether a zinc-containing treatment is technically and legally appropriate [130].

The supplied evidence also documents applications of metal-containing or passivating systems in conversion coatings, primer formulations, zinc or galvanized-steel passivation, marine protection, concrete-related treatments, and aerospace or acidic-steel studies. However, several of those associations appear only in a later review published after the report cutoff; they should therefore be treated as leads requiring verification against the underlying pre-2023 studies rather than as final cutoff evidence [123].

**Metal oxide-based inhibitors**

**Metal oxide-based inhibitors protect a substrate through a combination of barrier formation, adsorption, electrochemical inhibition, passivation, and—in some formulations—release or self-healing.** Oxide nanoparticles can block transport of oxygen, water, and chloride ions; adsorb on active sites and reduce anodic metal dissolution; impede cathodic oxygen reduction or hydrogen evolution; and promote a dense passive oxide layer [124]. Because both anodic and cathodic reactions can be affected, oxide nanoparticles are often described as mixed-type inhibitors [124].

Particle morphology controls the interfacial mechanism. Smaller particles provide a greater surface-area-to-volume ratio, adsorb more efficiently, and can enter microcracks and pores associated with pitting or crevice corrosion. Larger particles may form a dense, stable layer but can leave small defects uncovered. Spherical particles favor relatively uniform films, rod-like or anisotropic particles can form interlocking structures, and platelet or flake-shaped particles can create overlapping diffusion barriers [124]. The same high surface energy that improves adsorption also promotes agglomeration; poor dispersion reduces active surface area, causes sedimentation or phase separation, and can leave parts of the substrate unprotected [124].

Oxides can also act through chemical or physical adsorption. Surface hydroxyl groups, electrostatic interactions, and chemisorption can anchor the particles to the metal. Some systems release metal ions in response to pH or electrochemical changes; for example, the supplied review describes ZnO-derived Zn²⁺ reacting with hydroxide to produce zinc hydroxide that can seal defects. Cerium- and manganese-oxide systems may additionally provide redox or reactive-oxygen-species control, while combinations with polymers or other inhibitors can provide synergistic barrier and self-healing effects [124].

**Typical applications and operating environments**

Metal oxides are reported in **protective coatings, polymer nanocomposites, paints, and liquid additives** for industrial and marine service [124]. Documented substrates and environments include:

- copper exposed to boiler-feed-water-related aqueous conditions [124].

For concrete reinforcement, a ZnO experiment on embedded mild-steel rebar tested preparations equivalent to **25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%**; the reported effectiveness was limited to the 25–50% preparations, with the maximum result at 25%, under the stated **0.2 M H₂SO₄** test condition. The concentration and immersion period should not be transferred to ordinary concrete service because the test medium was acidic and the date is not stated in the supplied span [124]. In another application, ZnO–NiO nanocomposites were assessed on steel in **3.5 wt% NaCl**, while NiO and TiO₂ films were evaluated on stainless steel in salt-water environments [124].

The main limitations are formulation- and environment-dependent. Low oxide solubility can make uniform aqueous dispersion difficult; pH, ionic strength, temperature, competing ions, adhesion, substrate compatibility, and particle size all affect performance. Highly acidic or alkaline media can dissolve or alter some oxides, and high temperatures can cause phase changes or degradation [124]. Nanoparticle safety also differs from bulk-oxide safety: the supplied evidence flags inhalation and aquatic-toxicity concerns for nano-ZnO, possible inhalation hazards for nano-TiO₂, possible Cr(VI) contamination associated with chromium oxides, and greater toxicological concerns for copper and lead oxides, whereas aluminum and iron oxides are described as comparatively lower-risk materials [124]. These distinctions are material when selecting an oxide for an open water system rather than for a sealed coating.

**Boundary notes for phosphate and silicate categories**

The source group also supplies evidence clarifying the neighboring phosphate and silicate categories. A later review describes **orthophosphates, polyphosphates, and organophosphates** as phosphate-inhibitor subcategories and attributes protection primarily to insoluble phosphate films that form a crystalline or amorphous barrier; appropriate pH and phosphate concentration are required for uniform, adherent films [123]. It lists zinc phosphate, **Zn₃(PO₄)₂**, for coatings and automotive primers at a source-stated pH range of **5.0–7.5** and dosage of **5–25 ppm**; because the page is from 2025, these values are not adopted as final early-2023 operating limits [123]. The same later source associates phosphate programs with power-plant cooling-water circuits, cooling towers, closed-loop systems, metal finishing, water-based processes, and carbon steel in saline water from oil-source wells; it also describes imidazole plus polyphosphonate as a mixed adsorption–precipitation system used in oil and gas [123].

For **silicate-based inhibitors**, the later source describes sodium silicate and related compounds forming silicate or gel-like films that impede corrosive-species access. It identifies alkaline conditions as generally favorable and warns that high concentrations or temperatures can produce deposits and scale [123]. The cited application contexts include cooling water, automotive systems, municipal and industrial water systems, pipelines, cooling circuits, carbon steel in soft water, and passivation of zinc plates or zinc coatings [123]. The same source lists **lithium silicate (Li₂SiO₃)** as reacting with calcium to form a protective layer at a source-stated commercial dosage of **10–30 ppm** and pH **7.0–9.0**; because this listing is in a 2025 publication, lithium silicate remains provisional for the early-2023 report rather than a fully validated cutoff-period example [123].

Several visible records must not be treated as early-2023 final evidence:

- A market page reports an anodic-inhibitor share of **44.6% in 2025** and an oil-and-gas segment share of **61.4% in 2025**, and describes zinc technology introduced in **August 2024**; these observations are outside the report horizon and do not establish two pre-2023 metal-based examples [32].
- A 2025 review supplies useful mechanism and application leads, but its later publication date prevents it from independently satisfying the cutoff. Its oil-and-gas example of imidazoline derivatives is organic and should not be recast as metal-based inorganic inhibition [123].
- A page dated **July 20, 2026** reports boiler-water pH of **9.5–11.5**, dissolved oxygen below **0.02 mg/L** for medium- and high-pressure systems and below **0.005 mg/L** for critical applications, and notes that phosphates or phosphonates can decompose above roughly **40 bar**; these are later operational claims, not early-2023 evidence [132].
- The 2022 metal-based-inhibitor record concerns **urease**, not corrosion; its transition-metal ions and copper(II) complexes cannot be transferred to corrosion inhibition [43]. A related record published **1 December 2023** reports copper-complex urease IC₅₀ values of **0.46–41.1 μM**, but those values are biochemical urease data and are outside the requested cutoff [44].


Metal oxide-based inhibitors are classified by an oxide active phase—often used as particles, pigments, thin films, or coating fillers—rather than by the surrounding binder. Documented examples include zinc oxide (ZnO), titanium dioxide (TiO₂), aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃), cerium oxide (CeO₂), nickel oxide (NiO), manganese oxides, copper oxide, zirconium oxide (ZrO₂), chromium oxide (Cr₂O₃), and iron oxides such as Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄. Mixed systems, including ZnO–NiO and RGO–TiO₂, are also reported, but they should be identified as oxide-containing composites rather than assumed to be standalone dissolved inhibitors [133].

Candidate qualification is not uniform. **ZnO** is a supported specific metal-oxide inhibitor example, including the 2020 study of steel in acidic solution and studies of reinforced concrete [133]. **Al₂O₃** is named among common corrosion-inhibiting oxides, but another evidence span describes alumina mainly as a nanofiller in an anticorrosive composite coating and does not establish it as a standalone inhibitor; it should therefore be counted as a qualified example, not an unqualified dissolved inhibitor [133][93]. **Fe₂O₃/Fe₃O₄** are documented as oxide examples, and pre-2023 studies report iron-oxide or iron-nickel-oxide corrosion-protection systems, but the exact active species and whether the oxide acts independently or through a composite film remain material qualifications [133][103]. Nano-ZnO and nano-CuO also require exposure and environmental controls, whereas bulk Al₂O₃ and iron oxides are described as comparatively inert; Cr₂O₃, CuO nanoparticles, and lead oxides carry more substantial toxicity or contamination concerns [133].

**Phosphate-based inhibitors**

Applications include mild steel in simulated cooling water, where a cited sodium-phosphate entry reports 1 × 10⁻² M and 74.0% inhibition efficiency under the stated test conditions [122]. Other pre-2023 references concern phosphate inhibitors in cooling-water systems, cement pastes with chloride binding, soluble phosphates for steel reinforcement in mortar, and phosphate ions for reinforcement steel in chloride-rich environments [122]. Zinc phosphates are also used in coating systems with different binders and resins because their low solubility supports pigment and film applications [47]. Additional cited contexts include phosphate-containing automotive coolants for AA6060 aluminum and phosphate-ester mixtures for carbon steel in CO₂ brine .

**Qualification of silicate examples**


**Examples and classification.** Documented examples include zinc phosphate, zinc orthophosphate, sodium monofluorophosphate (Na2PO3F; MFP), disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4; DHP), trisodium phosphate (Na3PO4; TSP), aluminum tripolyphosphate, bimetallic polyphosphates, blended ortho-polyphosphates, calcium strontium phosphosilicate, and strontium aluminum polyphosphate [33][37][134]. Orthophosphates are generally classified as nonoxidizing anodic inhibitors whose protection normally requires oxygen, whereas polyphosphates may function principally as sequestrants or as part of ortho/polyphosphate blends [135][90]. Zinc phosphating can produce hopeite, Zn3(PO4)2·4H2O, and phosphophyllite, Zn,Fe(PO4)2·4H2O, on steel; hopeite is also reported on aluminum [78].

Typical applications include anticorrosive paints and primers for carbon steel, galvanized steel, aluminum, bridges, automotive and marine structures, pipelines, storage tanks, and oil-and-gas equipment [134][135]. In potable and industrial water treatment, dissolved orthophosphate passivates iron, copper, lead, and galvanized pipe by forming a less soluble metallic-phosphate scale; only dissolved orthophosphate reacts with the pipe wall. A documented operating practice uses 2–4 mg/L as PO4 during a 3–12-month start-up period, followed by 0.5–2 mg/L as PO4 for maintenance [90]. Polyphosphates instead sequester dissolved iron or manganese, should be added before chlorine, are not intended to control lead or copper release, and gradually hydrolyze to orthophosphate [90]. Other applications include reinforced concrete, where soluble phosphates may be admixed into cement paste (ACI) or applied to hardened concrete as migrating inhibitors (MCI). Reaction with portlandite, Ca(OH)2, can form apatite and restrict penetration; reported MFP, DHP, and TSP penetration depths were 1114, 380, and 126 μm, respectively [33]. Steam boilers use phosphate, caustic, sulfite, and sometimes dispersant; typical maintained conditions are pH 10–11, phosphate 20–60 ppm as PO4, TDS 2500–3500 micromhos/cm, sulfite 30–50 ppm as SO3, and OH alkalinity 150–300 ppm as CaCO3. In that treatment, scale is removed by blow-down and corrosion protection is attributed mainly to the sulfite oxygen scavenger rather than a phosphate film [40].

Performance depends strongly on water chemistry, flow, substrate, and dosage. Zinc orthophosphate was associated with good results below pH 8 and alkalinity 20 mg/L, with a recommended pH range of 6.5–8.5 and an optimum near 7.5 [37]. In one copper-water condition—pH 8, alkalinity 20 mg/L, chloramine 2.5 mg/L, and 0.37 mg/L each as Zn and P—relative corrosivity was 0.25 times raw water [37]. Conversely, cast-iron results were inconsistent: some treated loops were 4–7% worse than raw water, and natural scale formation and pH sometimes explained more protection than inhibitor addition [37]. Phosphate treatment can also soften existing scale, cause transient red water, encourage bacterial regrowth in cast-iron systems, or increase dissolved-metal concerns; these limitations require field verification [37].

The candidate **sodium orthophosphate** is only partially qualified. Allowed evidence explicitly supports sodium dihydrogen orthophosphate at 2 mg/L in the King’s Park, Scotland water system, where household lead levels were reported below 25% of pretreatment levels, and separately identifies Na3PO4 in concrete and alkaline-chloride studies. Other source slices do not establish “sodium orthophosphate” as the exact formulation, so NaH2PO4, Na2HPO4, and Na3PO4 should be named separately rather than treated as one fully validated alias [37][33][38].

**Silicate-based inhibitors**

Silicate-based inhibitors primarily act through passivation and protective-scale formation. Negatively charged silicate species are attracted to anodically generated positive metal ions and combine with corrosion products to form a film; continued feed can extend the film toward cathodic areas [37]. Sodium silicate can hydrolyze into negatively charged colloidal particles that migrate to anodic sites and form a film with solid corrosion products [40]. Coating formulations may also produce a silica-rich barrier, adsorbed silicate–metal complexes, or calcium-modified silica-gel barriers with an alkaline reserve of about pH 9–10 [134].

Important limits are slow film development, possible hot-water silica deposits, higher dosage and cost than phosphate treatment, and sensitivity to pH and natural scale. A 12-month cast-iron test reported a 26% improvement over raw water for a sodium-silicate loop, but also concluded that pH and naturally formed scale could dominate the result and that treatments gave no significant additional protection in some comparisons [37]. In a household-plumbing pilot, sodium silicate appeared beneficial for copper, but high standing-water metal levels led to a recommendation against immediate distribution-system use; mild-steel probe data were inadequate for firm conclusions [37]. **Sodium silicate** is nevertheless a supported specific inhibitor example: it is documented as an aqueous water-glass formulation and as a closed-system inhibitor; one pilot preparation used a 28.7% SiO2 concentrate diluted 100:1 to 2870 mg/L [37][40].


Silicate-based inhibitors protect mainly by forming an interfacial film. In water systems, silica adsorbs onto existing corrosion products; no complete protective film forms until some corrosion product is present. The reported film has a lower layer of corrosion products and an upper silica–metal-hydroxide adsorption layer [41]. Documented examples or subcategories are sodium silicate (water glass, approximately Na₂O:3.3SiO₂), crystalloid silica, and stable or unstable hydrated colloidal silica [41].

Typical applications include steel potable-water pipes, open or recirculating cooling-water systems, industrial cooling waters, and bimetallic systems. Silicate inhibition has also been reported for lead, brass, copper, zinc, aluminium, and iron. In comparative cooling-tower tests, a 100–150 ppm silicate residual produced a very hard, fairly dense coating; the same tests found phosphate deposits softer and bulkier, whereas chromate produced a thin film [41]. Silicate use in cooling-water systems was reported as less extensive than chromate or phosphate treatment, and a 2013 reference specifically associates sodium silicate with mild steel in simulated cooling water [48].

**Phosphate examples and qualification**

The pre-2023 water-treatment record lists polyphosphates, sodium-phosphate glasses, orthophosphates, sodium hexametaphosphate, and phosphonates as phosphate-related inhibitor examples or subcategories [41]. Sodium orthophosphate is independently supported in tap-water studies: at low concentration it minimized copper dissolution and reduced susceptibility to localized corrosion [71]. In artificial tap water at pH 7.6 and 20 ± 2 °C, tested orthophosphate doses were 5, 10, and 20 mg L⁻¹ as phosphorus, equivalent to 0.16, 0.32, and 0.64 mmol L⁻¹ NaH₂PO₄·H₂O. The optimum tested dose for brass was 10 mg L⁻¹ as P; phosphate made the film less porous, thinner, and denser and delayed pit initiation, but did not prevent pit growth after initiation. The presence of phosphate in the passive film still required confirmation by techniques such as Raman spectroscopy or X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy [71].

**Qualification of metal-oxide examples**

- **Zinc oxide (ZnO)—supported, but formulation- and environment-dependent.** A concrete-reinforcement study used ZnO as an inhibitor; after more than four years, the ZnO repair was the only tested inhibitor system showing clear passivation, with \(E_{\mathrm{corr}}<-200\) mV and \(i_{\mathrm{corr}}<0.2\ \mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}\). A related long-term study reported good performance for 1% ZnO concrete and identified a 2% ZnO/calcium-nitrite mixture as the best mixture tested [67][84]. ZnO/polymer nanocomposites are also supported in 5% HCl for mild steel: ZnO/PEG, ZnO/PVP, and ZnO/PAN behaved as mixed-type inhibitors and adsorbed through combined physisorption and chemisorption [68]. At 1000 ppm, their reported PDP efficiencies were 69.43%, 74.50%, and 83.42%, respectively, while EIS efficiencies were 70.69%, 90.81%, and 81.72% [68]. A 2020 article specifically investigated ZnO nanoparticles as an inhibitor for steel in acidic solution [69]. However, other records use ZnO merely as a Zn²⁺ source in cooling-water treatment, a phosphating-bath additive, a coating component, or part of a composite anode [81][82][137]. Those uses do not prove that neat ZnO is active as a dissolved inhibitor. Zinc oxide can also be a naturally formed corrosion product: its passivating effect depends on pH, and a zinc–iron model system found no cathodic inhibition or self-healing from poorly adherent ZnO/Zn(OH)₂ under its test conditions [53][76][77]. Thus, ZnO is a defensible metal-oxide example when its nanoparticle, concrete-admixture, or formulated use is stated explicitly.

- **Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃, alumina)—supported mainly as a protective oxide or coating, not as an independently verified dissolved inhibitor.** Pre-2023 studies describe Al₂O₃ in deposited corrosion-protection films, including approximately 10-nm films on aluminium and chromium steel and a 5-nm layer protecting an aluminium nanoaperture [50]. Sol–gel alumina coatings restricted electrolyte ingress and reduced mild-steel corrosion current by about five orders of magnitude relative to bare steel [54]. These results validate a metal-oxide protective-film role; they should not be recast as proof of conventional dissolved inhibition. Al₂O₃ is amphoteric and dissolves in both acidic and basic aqueous media, which limits its use alone in water [52].

Zinc oxide (ZnO) is a context-specific supported example: a cathodic rebar inhibitor, yet also a passive product or polyaniline–ZnO mixture for 57S Al in 2 M NaOH—not a standalone dissolved inhibitor [131][112][111]. A patent instead names sodium titanium oxide [107].

Aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) is qualification-limited: a 2022 chapter title calls it an inhibitor, but retained evidence is passive/coating evidence [65]. Pre-cutoff ALD films (10–50 nm) protected 100Cr6/Al2024-T3; 50 nm had <0.5% porosity on 100Cr6, while hydrophobic anodic Al₂O₃ reached up to 99% for 7 days in 3.5 wt% NaCl [66][64]. These are barrier results, not dissolved-inhibitor results; anodizing is distinct [113].

Sodium orthophosphate is unvalidated: sodium phosphate is an inorganic concrete inhibitor; sodium dihydrogen/pyrophosphate are coating components; Na₃PO₄ is only a ferric-phosphate precursor [131][107][114]. Sodium silicate/water glass is validated: Si₂O₅²⁻ gave ~90% inhibition in aerated 0.5 M NaCl, suppressing anodic/cathodic processes without pitting; it is also a precipitation inhibitor in water softeners [117][51]. Potassium silicate (2014/2020) and lithium silicate (2018) have coating-only evidence [110].

**Candidate qualification and boundary evidence**

**Iron oxides**

**Iron oxides are only conditionally validated as metal-oxide corrosion-control examples.** Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) is the strongest retained example: oxygen-free alkaline boiler water forms it from iron and water, producing a 0.2–0.7 mil protective film with a compact inner layer and porous outer layer; concentrated caustic can dissolve this layer.[119] In high-pH concrete, hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) and magnetite passivate reinforcing steel, but chlorides, carbonation, and sulfonation can destabilize the film.[115][116] Other evidence identifies iron oxides as corrosion products or film constituents, including Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄, FeOOH, goethite, and lepidocrocite; DEHA-mediated conversion of hematite to magnetite and inhibitor modification of magnetite coatings do not establish these oxides as standalone dissolved inhibitors.[34][85][87][94] Accordingly, magnetite may be listed as a conditional protective-oxide subcategory, whereas hematite and the other iron oxides should not be counted as independently validated inhibitor compounds. Pre-2023 bibliography leads associate Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄ with inhibitor studies, but one cited 2015 magnetite study is retracted and the later 2023 source is outside the cutoff.[118] A contents excerpt listing zinc addition in BWRs and batch or continuous oilfield inhibitors supplies no additional iron-oxide chemistry.[139]

**Sodium orthophosphate**

**Sodium orthophosphate is a supported specific phosphate-inhibitor example.** A 1951 study reports that decimolar sodium orthophosphate passivates iron above pH 7.25 in air; under deaeration, phosphate forms Fe₃(PO₄)₂·8H₂O, while dissolved oxygen contributes to formation of γ-Fe₂O₃.[70] Independent ellipsometry in 0.1 N sodium orthophosphate found multiple phosphate films at open circuit in aerated solutions at pH 7.0 and 9.1, with reduced iron corrosion; film behavior depends on pH, potential, and oxygen.[80] Orthophosphate is therefore an anodic inhibitor, but its action is oxygen-dependent: in glycol systems, the cited feed range is 1,000–5,000 ppm, and inhibition is ineffective without oxygen.[94]

Related evidence names Na₃PO₄ in concrete-pore solutions, disodium/trisodium phosphate in iron-passivation studies, dibasic sodium phosphate as an anticorrosive material, and mono-, di-, and trisodium phosphate in boiler treatment; these corroborate phosphate inhibition but do not erase the species-level distinction surrounding the name “sodium orthophosphate.”[55][57][79][83][86] Boiler phosphate programs buffer pH, but orthophosphate should not be fed through the feedwater line because calcium-phosphate precipitation can plug it; phosphate precipitation in low- and medium-pressure boilers forms hydroxyapatite, while polyphosphates such as sodium tripolyphosphate and sodium hexametaphosphate revert rapidly to orthophosphate at boiler temperatures.[86] Older aluminum fresh-water tests likewise identify phosphate as a buffer/inhibitor, generally finding the most effective formulations above pH 9.5.[121]

**Silicate examples**

**Sodium silicate is a validated silicate-based inhibitor.** In aerated 3.5% NaCl drilling fluid, sodium silicate hydrate (Na₂SiO₃·9H₂O) was tested at 250–2,000 mg/L; concentrations above 1,250 mg/L produced reported carbon-steel protection above 92%.[60] The proposed mechanism is adsorption followed by a gelatinous film containing iron, calcium, and magnesium silicates, silica, and associated hydroxides.[60] Earlier EPA testing identified sodium metasilicate and glassy silicate in drinking-water systems; 30 mg/L as SiO₂ was most effective in gravimetric cast-iron tests, whereas electrochemical testing up to 50 mg/L showed no measurable effect, demonstrating method and substrate dependence.[56] Other pre-cutoff evidence covers carbon-steel water pipelines, where sodium silicate/HEDP produced a compact film and over 90% co-inhibition under specified flow conditions,[58] cooling-water systems using 50 ppm as Si with sodium molybdate, and ordinary potable or cooling water in which silicate plus zinc forms a protective zinc-silicate film.[59][75] Polysilicates are described as adsorption inhibitors; continuous feeding can extend protection toward cathodic areas, with a common sodium-silicate SiO₂:Na₂O ratio of 3:22.[36] Sodium silicate is also documented in water piping, aerated hot-water, domestic softening, boiler-hardness, and cooling-water formulations.[61][63][120][140] Anodizing and conversion-coating evidence is retained as a boundary case, not as proof of dissolved-inhibitor use.[49][62]

**Potassium silicate is also supported as a specific example.** K₂SiO₃ is reported as beneficial in ammonia–water absorption cycles,[72] and Kasil-6 is identified in an aqueous corrosion-inhibitor composition as potassium silicate with a 1:2.1 K₂O:SiO₂ ratio and 38% solution concentration.[109] FDA interchangeability with sodium silicate provides only indirect support, while agricultural foliar-spray and magnesium-anodizing evidence concerns non-corrosion applications.[73][49] A July 2023 coating report is post-cutoff and is not counted.[74]

**Lithium silicate remains partially supported rather than a validated standalone inhibitor.** A patent describes Li₂SiO₃ as a believed-beneficial additive in ammonia–water cycles,[72] while pre-cutoff studies mainly concern zinc-rich lithium-silicate coatings in which zinc supplies sacrificial-anode protection; lithium silicate functions as the inorganic binder.[99][101][129] Pre-2023 concrete studies similarly describe lithium silicate as a densifier or sealer: one field study reported a 42.4% average chloride reduction from lithium silicate alone and 79.9% from lithium silicate plus silane, but required continued monitoring for permanence.[128] A surface-improvement agent penetrated about 40 mm into concrete and reduced chloride diffusion by 20–60%, yet it was not classified as a corrosion inhibitor.[102] Sources published in March or April 2023 are outside the operational early-2023 evidence set; one also labels a section “lithium silicate” while reporting sodium silicate solution.[95][100] Thus sodium and potassium silicate satisfy the validated example minimum; lithium silicate should remain explicitly qualified.

Use the requested principal taxonomy—Metal-based inhibitors, Metal oxide-based inhibitors, Phosphate-based inhibitors, and Silicate-based inhibitors—while recognizing that phosphate and silicate also appear within the broader inorganic anodic/passivation classification [122][123]. The supported profiles distinguish metal oxides by barrier, adsorption, electrochemical inhibition, passivation and self-healing [124], and phosphates by migratory adsorption, ion replacement, precipitation and scale formation [45][38][134].

Metal-based inhibitors, including low-level zinc programs, are associated with cooling systems, boilers, pipelines, industrial water circuits and oil-and-gas facilities, including automotive coolants and surface pretreatment [130][32][123]; metal-oxide inhibitors extend to reinforced concrete, marine and salt-water systems, acidic or alkaline media, biomedical implants and polymer or epoxy coatings [124][133]. Phosphate-based inhibitors serve concrete, boiler, potable and cooling water, primers and marine or bridge protection [136][134], while silicate-based inhibitors serve potable-water pipes, drinking-water distribution and recirculating cooling water [41][126].

Metal-based inhibitors are treated as a passivation family when anodic members form protective oxide layers that suppress metal dissolution; chromates, nitrites and molybdates are documented members [32][123]. Their effectiveness therefore depends on formation and persistence of that surface film under the operating condition, rather than on a single universal interaction [32].

Phosphate inhibitors combine migratory adsorption, chloride replacement, precipitation and passivation: ions adsorb on rebar, moisture-activated pigments release ions into insoluble layers, and calcium or iron reactions build protective films [45][134][38][33]. These modes can be anodic, cathodic or mixed and depend on oxygen, chloride, pH, concentration and film or scale conditions; applications include chloride-contaminated concrete and rebar, drinking-water lead and copper control and cooling-water systems, while poor carbon-steel protection was reported in a low-dose cooling test [33][126][42][136].

Silicate-based inhibitors are documented for steel water-corrosion control in potable-water pipes and recirculating cooling-water systems; sodium silicate was recommended for recirculating water but used less extensively than chromates and phosphates [41].

Within the chemical-structure classification, metal-oxide-based inhibitors form an oxide or nanoparticle profile: examples include ZnO, TiO₂, Fe₂O₃, Al₂O₃, CeO₂ and NiO, using barrier protection, adsorption, electrochemical inhibition, passivation and self-healing [123][124][133]. Phosphate-based inhibitors form the phosphate subclass, with sodium monofluorophosphate, disodium hydrogen phosphate, trisodium phosphate, zinc phosphate and aluminum tripolyphosphate as examples; their supported mechanisms are migratory adsorption, precipitation and duplex passivation, with pH, chloride, oxygen and scale composition controlling performance [33][45][134][38][136][126].

Zinc oxide (ZnO) is retained as a qualified metal-oxide example: a 2020 study documents it for steel in acidic solution, it is explicitly discussed as a corrosion inhibitor, and it is reported as cathodic [133][124][131]. Because other evidence places ZnO in composite or additive systems and raises dispersion or standalone-qualification concerns [133][88], the substrate and formulation should be stated rather than generalized; Al₂O₃ is likewise qualified because its nanoparticles promote passive oxide layers on aluminum and the material is described as common, non-toxic and environmentally inert [124][133].

Al₂O₃ is classified as a metal oxide associated with aluminum protection; its nanoparticle mechanism is passive-layer promotion and its reported profile is non-toxic and environmentally inert [124][133]. Iron oxides (Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄) are retained only as qualified examples: nanoparticles are studied on mild steel and iron-nickel oxide is used as an anticorrosive pigment [133], while Fe₂O₃ can also be rust or a corrosion-product/passive-film species, so the exact oxide, substrate and formulation must be stated [124][138][115].

Retain sodium orthophosphate, especially trisodium phosphate (Na₃PO₄) and disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na₂HPO₄), as phosphate examples explicitly identified among soluble inhibitors [33]; boiler programs use phosphate precipitation to remove calcium hardness as hydroxyapatite [86], but the protective result remains formulation- and pH-dependent [33]. Retain sodium silicate as a qualified silicate candidate because it is documented in corrosion studies and for carbon steel in saline or salt-water environments, with a treatment level reported [35][37][59].

Retain sodium silicate as a silicate member: sodium metasilicate and glassy silicate are documented examples [56], and sodium silicate is supported in carbon-steel saline service and corrosion studies [35][37]; direct film-forming evidence is later than the reporting cutoff and should not be used to generalize the cutoff qualification [125]. Potassium silicate should not be counted as a validated member because the specific-example evidence names sodium silicate, while potassium-specific material does not establish a pre-cutoff inhibitor mechanism, formula or application [75][110][46].

Lithium silicate is a qualified but not fully validated silicate candidate: pre-cutoff literature links it to anticorrosive coatings [99], while other evidence places it in multicomponent systems with phosphates, aluminates or metal salts and in concrete-densifier use without establishing standalone inhibitor performance [108][127]. Use the requested principal taxonomy—Metal-based inhibitors, Metal oxide-based inhibitors, Phosphate-based inhibitors and Silicate-based inhibitors—as the top-level framework, but separate dominant chemistry from overlapping phosphate, silicate and metal formulations [108].

Organize Metal-based inhibitors and Metal oxide-based inhibitors in the same sequence—class, examples, interface mechanism, applications and limitations. Metal-based inhibitors form protective oxide films that suppress dissolution and include zinc, chromate and molybdate examples in cooling, boiler, pipeline and oil-and-gas contexts [32][130], whereas metal-oxide nanoparticles add barrier, adsorption, electrochemical inhibition, passivation, self-healing and synergy; size, shape, surface chemistry and structural properties should remain attached to each application claim [124][133].

Silicate-based inhibitors should be presented as a passivation or film-forming subclass: silicate ions adsorb to metal or corrosion products and combine with by-products into a protective scale or diffusion barrier, while pH, silicate level and pre-existing corrosion products govern formation [37][126][41]. Documented fields include potable-water pipes, recirculating or cooling water, drinking-water distribution and detergent or antifreeze formulations; use remains limited and results are mixed because effectiveness depends on existing scale and water chemistry [41][126][37][39].

## Part Two: Key Chemical Reactions

**Five reaction cases explain different processes.** The zinc and aluminum cases describe formation of protective products on those metals; the magnesium case describes sacrificial electron supply to steel; and the phosphate cases describe precipitation of insoluble surface products. Combining them under “protective-layer formation” is useful, provided sacrificial protection is not mistaken for deposition of a compulsory film on the protected steel.

**Notation identifies species and phases.** In the equations below, **(s)** denotes a solid, **(l)** liquid water, **(aq)** a dissolved species, and **(g)** a gas. Electrons are written as e⁻. A “derived overall balance” is a stoichiometric sum of the stated component reactions; it is not an independently measured reaction rate or a claim that every service environment follows only that pathway.

### 1. Zinc reacting with oxygen and water

**The requested hydroxide-forming balance is:**

$$2\mathrm{Zn(s)}+\mathrm{O_2(aq)}+2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\rightarrow2\mathrm{Zn(OH)_2(s)}\tag{1}$$

**This balance combines three surface steps.** A 2019 study of pure zinc in saline solution describes zinc oxidation, oxygen reduction generating hydroxide, and precipitation of zinc hydroxide. Equation (1) is the derived overall balance of those source-described steps. Zinc hydroxide can subsequently contribute to an oxide/hydroxide product layer that restricts further dissolution. [9]

The anodic oxidation is:

$$\mathrm{Zn(s)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{e^-}$$

The oxygen-reduction half-reaction is:

$$\mathrm{O_2(aq)}+2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}+4\mathrm{e^-}\rightarrow4\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}$$

The precipitation step is:

$$\mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn(OH)_2(s)}$$

**Electron cancellation produces the overall equation.** Multiplying zinc oxidation and hydroxide precipitation by two supplies and consumes the four electrons in oxygen reduction. Dissolved Zn²⁺ and OH⁻ then cancel from the sum, leaving Equation (1). Water remains a reactant because the selected protective product is zinc hydroxide.

**Zinc oxide provides a related endpoint.** The saline-zinc study describes partial dehydration of zinc hydroxide to zinc oxide:

$$\mathrm{Zn(OH)_2(s)}\rightarrow\mathrm{ZnO(s)}+\mathrm{H_2O(l)}$$

Adding this step for both zinc atoms to Equation (1) gives the derived oxide-forming net reaction:

$$2\mathrm{Zn(s)}+\mathrm{O_2(aq)}\rightarrow2\mathrm{ZnO(s)}$$

**Water cancels only in the oxide balance.** This does not mean the electrochemical process occurs without water. Water participates in oxygen reduction and the formation of hydroxyl-containing species, but does not remain in the net stoichiometry when ZnO is selected as the final product. The hydroxide-forming and oxide-forming equations describe related endpoints of the same aqueous reaction sequence. [9]

**Alkaline calcium solution changes the final film.** Doi and Hiromoto directly report the following zinc-oxide-forming anodic expression for their saturated-Ca(OH)₂ system:

$$\mathrm{Zn(s)}+2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{ZnO(s)}+\mathrm{H_2O(l)}+2\mathrm{e^-}$$

They also describe zincate formation and its subsequent reaction with calcium:

$$\mathrm{ZnO(s)}+\mathrm{H_2O(l)}+2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn(OH)_4^{2-}(aq)}$$

$$2\mathrm{Zn(OH)_4^{2-}(aq)}+\mathrm{Ca^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Ca[Zn(OH)_3]_2\cdot2H_2O(s)}+2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}$$

**The environment selects the protective product.** The last equation forms CHZ, the calcium hydroxyzincate layer discussed in Part One. In chloride-containing saline water, the products instead included zinc oxide, zinc hydroxide, and zinc hydroxychloride. Thus Equation (1) is an appropriate core oxygen-and-water balance, while the actual surface film must be interpreted from the solution composition. [5][9]

### 2. Aluminum forming an aluminum hydroxide layer

**The oxygen-assisted overall balance is:**

$$4\mathrm{Al(s)}+3\mathrm{O_2(aq)}+6\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\rightarrow4\mathrm{Al(OH)_3(s)}\tag{2}$$

**Equation (2) is a derived balance.** It represents aluminum oxidation coupled to oxygen reduction and precipitation of Al(OH)₃. The 2020 LDH review describes aluminum substrates supplying aluminum species and participating in surface aluminum-hydroxide formation. The equation expresses that hydrated surface-product chemistry in a balanced overall form. [14]

The formal aluminum-oxidation step is:

$$\mathrm{Al(s)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Al^{3+}(aq)}+3\mathrm{e^-}$$

The hydroxide-precipitation balance is:

$$\mathrm{Al^{3+}(aq)}+3\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Al(OH)_3(s)}$$

**Twelve electrons complete the balance.** Four aluminum atoms release twelve electrons; three oxygen-reduction reactions consume the same twelve electrons and generate twelve hydroxide ions. Four precipitation reactions then consume those hydroxide ions. Adding the three contributions gives Equation (2).

**Hydrated aluminum products restrict further reaction.** An adherent oxide/hydroxide-rich surface can reduce transport between the aluminum and the surrounding electrolyte. Actual aluminum passive films may contain oxide and oxyhydroxide components as well as hydroxide, so Al(OH)₃ is the requested representative hydrated product rather than a universal description of the entire passive layer. [14]

**Strong alkalinity can undermine that film.** Aluminum oxide/hydroxide chemistry is sensitive to pH, and strongly alkaline interfacial conditions can favor dissolution rather than persistent protection. The cerium-coating study uses this behavior to explain why blocking cathodic reactions can protect aluminum: reducing local alkalization helps avoid conditions in which its own surface oxide becomes soluble. [13]

**Self-passivation differs from sacrificial service.** Formation of an aluminum hydroxide-rich layer can benefit an aluminum substrate, yet excessive passivation of an aluminum sacrificial anode can reduce useful current output. Equation (2) therefore describes protective surface chemistry on aluminum; it should not be interpreted as the principal reason an aluminum-alloy anode protects steel. [3]

### 3. Magnesium as a sacrificial anode protecting steel

**Magnesium oxidation supplies the protective electrons:**

$$\mathrm{Mg(s)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Mg^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{e^-}\tag{3}$$

**The steel receives those electrons.** Equation (3), the core sacrificial-anode half-reaction, is reported in the 2022 natural-gas-pipeline study. Electrical connection allows the released electrons to reach the steel, where they sustain cathodic reduction while suppressing the competing dissolution of iron. [27][6]

For an oxygenated neutral or alkaline electrolyte, the steel cathode can support:

$$\mathrm{O_2(aq)}+2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}+4\mathrm{e^-}\rightarrow4\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}$$

If magnesium hydroxide precipitates, the accompanying balance is:

$$\mathrm{Mg^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2(s)}$$

**The oxygen-coupled net balance is derived.** Combining two magnesium-oxidation reactions, one oxygen-reduction reaction, and two hydroxide-precipitation reactions gives:

$$2\mathrm{Mg(s)}+\mathrm{O_2(aq)}+2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\rightarrow2\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2(s)}$$

**Iron is absent for a reason.** The protected steel is the cathodic reaction surface, not a required consumed reactant in this net equation. The main protective action is magnesium oxidation and external electron delivery. Magnesium hydroxide may precipitate at suitable locations in the coupled system, but a continuous Mg(OH)₂ layer on the steel is not a prerequisite for protection.

**Water reduction provides another cathodic pathway.** Especially where hydrogen evolution is significant, the cathodic reaction can instead be written:

$$2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}+2\mathrm{e^-}\rightarrow\mathrm{H_2(g)}+2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}$$

Combining this with magnesium oxidation and hydroxide precipitation gives the overall reaction reported in the 2021 seawater-anode study:

$$\mathrm{Mg(s)}+2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2(s)}+\mathrm{H_2(g)}$$

**Hydrogen can represent an efficiency penalty.** Magnesium corrosion and hydrogen evolution can also occur locally on the anode, consuming magnesium without sending all corresponding charge through the external protective circuit. Consequently, magnesium mass loss is not automatically equivalent to useful protective current. Excessive polarization and hydrogen generation can also compromise coatings or protected structures. [8][7]

**Protection requires sustained anodic operation.** The bicarbonate-induced polarity reversal discussed earlier is the decisive counterexample: if magnesium passivates sufficiently and the couple reverses, the intended sacrificial mechanism no longer applies. Equation (3) identifies the desired anodic process; service chemistry determines whether that process continues usefully. [10]

### 4. Formation of insoluble zinc phosphate

**The source-reported film-forming reaction is:**

$$3\mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn_3(PO_4)_2(s)}+4\mathrm{H^+(aq)}\tag{4}$$

**This is precipitation, not metal oxidation.** Zinc remains in oxidation state +2 and phosphorus remains in its phosphate oxidation state. The equation describes conversion of dissolved zinc and phosphate species into an insoluble surface product. Sato, Ueno, and Okamoto explicitly reported this reaction for crystalline zinc-phosphate deposition on mild steel in an acidic zinc-phosphate-saturated solution. [28]

**Charge and atoms are conserved.** The left side has net charge +4: three Zn²⁺ ions contribute +6 and two H₂PO₄⁻ ions contribute −2. The four H⁺ ions on the right provide the same charge. The phosphate groups form the neutral solid, and their original hydrogen appears as released acid.

**Local acid removal favors deposition.** Because Equation (4) releases hydrogen ions, continuing precipitation requires conditions that prevent excessive local acid accumulation. Cathodic processes and bath chemistry can help establish the necessary interfacial conditions. The film-forming reaction is therefore linked to the electrochemical behavior of the substrate, even though the precipitation step itself contains no electrons. [28]

**Oxidant strength changed the observed outcome.** In the 1966 bath, dissolved oxygen produced phosphate deposition relatively slowly, and sodium nitrate was judged a suitable moderate oxidant. Strong oxidants such as potassium permanganate and hydrogen peroxide passivated the iron and prevented phosphate deposition under those conditions. This demonstrates that accelerating oxidation is not synonymous with accelerating a useful conversion coating. [28]

**Hydrated and mixed phosphates also occur.** Practical zinc-phosphate coatings can contain hydrated hopeite, Zn₃(PO₄)₂·4H₂O, and mixed iron–zinc phosphate phases such as phosphophyllite, Zn₂Fe(PO₄)₂·4H₂O. Equation (4) deliberately gives the requested simplified zinc-phosphate precipitation balance. The deposited crystalline layer can reduce electrochemical access and improve the base for a subsequent coating, although porosity often makes sealing or topcoating important. [3]

### 5. Overall iron-phosphate protective-layer formation

**The supported ferrous-film precipitation balance is:**

$$3\mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-(aq)}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{Fe_3(PO_4)_2(s)}+4\mathrm{H^+(aq)}\tag{5}$$

**The product is iron(II) phosphate.** The 1974 study reports this equilibrium stoichiometry and observed an anodic Fe₃(PO₄)₂ film on iron in neutral phosphate solution at an active-dissolution potential. The rightward direction represents precipitation and film growth; the reversible arrow emphasizes that solution chemistry can also favor dissolution. [22]

**Equation (5) is net ionic overall chemistry.** It contains no electrons because the iron has already entered solution as Fe²⁺. The preceding anodic step is:

$$\mathrm{Fe(s)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)}+2\mathrm{e^-}$$

The phosphate then captures suitable dissolved iron species as a surface precipitate, potentially restricting further dissolution and transport.

**A metal-plus-acid balance can also be derived.** If iron dissolves as Fe²⁺ and proton reduction supplies the cathodic reaction, the overall ferrous-phosphate expression is:

$$3\mathrm{Fe(s)}+2\mathrm{H_3PO_4(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe_3(PO_4)_2(s)}+3\mathrm{H_2(g)}$$

**This expression requires its stated assumptions.** It combines iron dissolution in acid with phosphate deprotonation and precipitation, assuming the selected final iron product is ferrous phosphate. Avdeev’s 2019 review documents iron dissolution in phosphoric-acid media and the association of residual iron phosphates with protective surface films. The derived equation is useful stoichiometric bookkeeping, not a claim that hot phosphoric acid necessarily passivates steel completely. [29]

**Ferric phosphate requires different iron chemistry.** Where Fe³⁺ is the relevant dissolved species, its simple precipitation balance is:

$$\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq)}+\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{FePO_4(s)}$$

An equivalent expression using dihydrogen phosphate is:

$$\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq)}+\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{FePO_4(s)}+2\mathrm{H^+(aq)}$$

**FePO₄ is a distinct protective product.** Ferric phosphate was identified together with iron oxides/oxyhydroxides in the 2020 reinforcing-steel study. It is therefore a valid component of phosphate-based protective films, but should not replace Fe₃(PO₄)₂ indiscriminately in the ferrous reaction. The deciding conditions include oxidation state, local potential, pH, and the competing formation of hydroxides and oxides. [18][22]

**A balanced reaction does not establish coverage.** The 1974 work also investigated colloidal phosphate formation, illustrating that an insoluble solid may form in the liquid rather than as a useful adherent layer. Protection requires deposition at the right location with sufficient continuity and stability. That distinction links the equations back to the practical differences among soluble inhibitors, pigments, and conversion coatings. [22]

**Additional documented evidence**

**Reaction conventions and scope**

The equations below distinguish anodic and cathodic half-reactions, net-ionic precipitation, and idealized overall reactions. A dissolved inorganic inhibitor acts in the electrolyte or at the metal–solution interface; a conversion coating, zinc-rich primer, or sacrificial anode is a related but distinct protection method. The permitted 2019 chapter describes inhibitor action as barrier formation or retardation of anodic/cathodic processes, and places oxide/hydroxide barrier films in neutral or sometimes alkaline oxygen-reduction environments [51]. Equations marked “idealized” identify their assumed medium and product rather than claiming that one film phase forms universally.

**Zinc-based metal case**


\text{Anodic:}\quad &\mathrm{Zn(s)\rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq)+2e^-}\\
\text{Cathodic:}\quad &\mathrm{O_2(g)+2H_2O(l)+4e^-\rightarrow4OH^-(aq)}\\
\text{Precipitation:}\quad &\mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)+2OH^-(aq)\rightarrow Zn(OH)_2(s)}\\
\text{Overall:}\quad &\mathrm{2Zn(s)+O_2(g)+2H_2O(l)\rightarrow2Zn(OH)_2(s)}

The anodic step consumes zinc, the oxygen-reduction step occurs at cathodic sites, and the local hydroxide promotes deposition of a sparingly soluble zinc hydroxide film. The general neutral/alkaline oxide–hydroxide and oxygen-reduction framework is supported by the pre-cutoff source [51]. A later source explicitly prints the corresponding zinc–hydroxide and zinc–oxide half-reactions, but it is not treated as final early-2023 evidence [145].


\mathrm{Zn(OH)_2(s)\rightarrow ZnO(s)+H_2O(l)}


\mathrm{2Zn(s)+O_2(g)\rightarrow2ZnO(s)}

is the idealized oxide-forming overall equation. Thus, **Zn(OH)₂** is the most direct hydrated product for the stated aqueous route, whereas **ZnO** becomes a plausible alternative when the interfacial film dehydrates or the local alkalinity changes. Later material reports Zn(OH)₂ as the principal product near physiological pH and ZnO when local pH exceeds approximately 8.3; it also notes that dissolved oxygen affects the cathodic reaction and corrosion potential [145]. Hydrogen, bicarbonate, phosphate, and chloride ions can alter the stability and composition of the zinc corrosion layer [145]. Carbon dioxide or phosphate can further replace the simple hydroxide route with carbonate, hydrozincite, or zinc-phosphate products; these alternatives should not be inserted into the oxygen–water-only equation [2, 253].

Zinc salts are therefore classified as cathodic, film-forming or precipitating inhibitors: the insoluble deposit blocks active sites and restricts oxygen or ion transport rather than necessarily forming a uniform passive oxide. The film-forming species may be a zinc hydroxide, carbonate, phosphate, or another sparingly soluble phase, and its effectiveness depends on pH, oxygen availability, temperature, electrolyte composition, and surface coverage [65, 253]. A later review reports an observed case of **Zn²⁺ at 6 ppm** in carbon-steel seawater, classified as cathodic, with **83.3% inhibition efficiency**; because the review is post-cutoff and gives no test date in the cited span, this is not used as an early-2023 benchmark [122].

Zinc-rich primers and galvanized zinc must be kept separate from the dissolved-zinc equation. In a zinc-rich primer, zinc particles are electrically connected to steel, zinc is the sacrificial anode, and steel is the cathode; the initial protection is galvanic and later becomes more barrier-like as zinc corrosion products fill pores [146]. A later grounding-grid study likewise describes a dense zinc oxide film and preferential zinc oxidation, but reports that protection declines when the zinc layer is consumed or damaged [165]. Those are coating or sacrificial-metal systems, not evidence that every zinc-containing product is a dissolved inhibitor.

A separate surface-treatment route illustrates the same need for condition labels. A later study reports zinc treatment with 2-ethylhexanoic-acid vapour for one hour at **60, 80, or 100 °C**, followed by **24 hours in air**. A zinc basic salt forms during vapour exposure, and a second zinc-organic compound forms after aeration; the latter was identified as the protective species. Under **100% humidity**, the reported corrosion-time increases were factors of **700, 2,850, and 3,500**, respectively; the passive state persisted for up to **70 days**, salt-fog protection reached **72 hours**, and the adsorption film was no thicker than **100 nm** while the oxide thickness fell from **5.5 to 1 nm** [212]. This is a vapour adsorption/dehydration mechanism, not the requested zinc–oxygen–water reaction, and the later source is not final cutoff evidence.

**Magnesium sacrificial anode and steel**


\text{Magnesium anode:}\quad &\mathrm{Mg(s)\rightarrow Mg^{2+}(aq)+2e^-}\\
\text{Steel cathode:}\quad &\mathrm{O_2(g)+2H_2O(l)+4e^-\rightarrow4OH^-(aq)}\\
\text{Local precipitation:}\quad &\mathrm{Mg^{2+}(aq)+2OH^-(aq)\rightarrow Mg(OH)_2(s)}\\
\text{Overall cell reaction:}\quad &\mathrm{2Mg(s)+O_2(g)+2H_2O(l)\rightarrow2Mg(OH)_2(s)}

Magnesium supplies electrons through the metallic connection, making the steel surface the cathode and suppressing anodic dissolution of steel. The electrolyte carries ionic current; electrical continuity between magnesium and steel is essential. The protective function is therefore the electron supply and cathodic polarization, not merely the formation of magnesium hydroxide. Galvanic corrosion requires a potential difference between dissimilar metals in a conducting corrosive solution, which drives electron flow between them [51].


\mathrm{2H^+(aq)+2e^-\rightarrow H_2(g)}

\mathrm{Mg(s)+2H^+(aq)\rightarrow Mg^{2+}(aq)+H_2(g)}

In neutral water, hydrogen evolution can instead be written as \(\mathrm{2H_2O+2e^-\rightarrow H_2+2OH^-}\), giving \(\mathrm{Mg+2H_2O\rightarrow Mg(OH)_2+H_2}\). These alternatives are medium-dependent; the oxygen-reduction equation is the appropriate first representation for an oxygenated aqueous steel system.

The classification distinction is decisive: a dissolved inhibitor is added continuously or intermittently to a fluid and changes interfacial electrochemistry or deposits a film, whereas a magnesium sacrificial anode is a separate, consumable metal electrically coupled to the protected structure. Cathodic protection is reported for submerged seawater structures, soil-exposed structures, and concrete such as well casings; the anode has a finite life because it is consumed, and passivation can reduce its capacity [141]. A related source notes that magnesium-containing compounds may also precipitate as oxides forming a surface barrier, but that precipitation mechanism must not be confused with magnesium acting as a galvanic anode [51].

Later sources add condition-specific, non-final observations. Magnesium protection is electrochemical galvanic coupling, not dissolved inhibition: Mg dissolves anodically, while hydrogen evolution or oxygen reduction is shifted mainly to coupled steel, and a metallic connection plus common electrolyte is required [171][213]. Steel remains protected while the Mg/steel galvanic arrangement keeps steel cathodic and supplies current through the metallic circuit and electrolyte [206][213]. Zinc phosphate protects through phosphate-film formation on the metal surface, whereas iron phosphate is condition-dependent: below pH 7.2 its film is unprotective, while an iron-oxide-forming potential can produce a mixed oxide/phosphate film [205][146][80].

### Cooling water systems: combine inhibition with deposit control

**A documented formulation combines phosphate and silicate.** U.S. Patent 5,232,629, published in 1993, describes a carbon-steel corrosion-inhibiting composition with approximately three parts by weight orthophosphate to one part sodium silicate, preferring trisodium phosphate as the phosphate source. It explicitly includes cooling-water systems and cooling towers among its applications. [23]

**The comparison favored the combined treatment.** In a continuously aerated 42-liter bath at 25 °C and pH 7.5, two SAE-1010 steel coupons per condition were exposed for seven days. The treatment containing 2.5 ppm as SiO₂ plus 7.5 ppm as PO₄ produced an average corrosion rate of 1.64 mils/year, compared with 3.83 mils/year for 10 ppm as PO₄ alone. [23]

**The calculated improvement was approximately 57%.** Using the reported means, the estimated relative reduction was (3.83 − 1.64) ÷ 3.83 × 100 = 57.2%. This compares the combined treatment with phosphate alone under the patent’s test conditions; it is not a claim of a 57.2% improvement in every cooling tower. The test concentrations are reported on PO₄ and SiO₂ bases, which should not be confused with equal masses of the corresponding commercial sodium salts.

**The mechanism supports complementary action.** Phosphate can assist oxide passivation and metal-phosphate precipitation, while silicate can contribute another surface-film chemistry. Their combined effectiveness is plausible where each supplies a useful interfacial function. The result does not establish that any arbitrary mixture is synergistic, because composition, water chemistry, and deposition behavior still determine the final film. [20][24]

**Cooling service adds a heat-transfer constraint.** A treatment that reduces coupon corrosion but produces excessive scale can still be unsuitable for heat exchangers and circulating equipment. Earlier cooling-water patent literature identifies both phosphate-related sludge/scale and calcium/magnesium-related silicate scaling as concerns. The practical judgment therefore combines metal-loss reduction with acceptable deposit behavior under the system’s flow and concentration conditions. [26]

### Oil and gas production: distinguish fluids from hardware

**The sodium-silicate example concerns saline drilling fluids.** The 2016 AISI 1020 study demonstrated corrosion reduction in an aerated saline laboratory medium and proposed application to saltwater-based drilling fluids. Its named inhibitor was Na₂SiO₃·9H₂O. This is a specific, relevant oilfield application pathway, rather than evidence that sodium silicate is the general-purpose inhibitor for all produced fluids. [24]

**Production chemistry can change the dominant attack.** Oil-and-gas equipment may encounter chloride-rich brine, dissolved CO₂, H₂S, acidic stimulation fluids, and substantial temperature and pressure changes. The 2023 downhole review distinguishes these environments and their corrosion mechanisms. An aerated saline-water result therefore transfers most directly to a comparable water-based service, not automatically to acidic or sour production conditions. [3]

**Coatings protect a different part of the system.** Zinc-rich silicate coatings are documented for steel and drill-pipe protection, while phosphate conversion layers can contribute to casing-connection performance. These are manufactured surface systems, not substitutes for a dissolved inhibitor delivered through a production stream. Their suitability depends on coating integrity, adhesion, mechanical exposure, and compatibility with the contacted fluid. [3]

**External anodes address another corrosion circuit.** Magnesium or other galvanic anodes can protect a pipeline’s external surface through soil or water acting as the electrolyte. They do not thereby establish protection of an electrically or hydraulically separate internal corrosion environment. The distinction between external cathodic protection and internal fluid treatment is therefore central to applying the metal-based family correctly. [27]

**The application judgment is environment-specific.** Silicate treatment is supported as a saline water-based fluid option, zinc-rich silicates as protective coatings, and active-metal anodes as electrochemical protection for suitable external structures. A change toward strongly acidic chemistry, an unstable coating environment, or inadequate current delivery changes which of these mechanisms remains useful.

### Concrete reinforcement: delivery determines useful chemistry

**Phosphate is directly supported for reinforcing steel.** The Na₃PO₄ example in the application table is backed by both simulated-pore-solution research and mortar literature. Its intended action is to support a protective iron oxide/phosphate interface and impede chloride-driven dissolution. It is therefore chemically connected to the iron-phosphate equations in Part Two, even though the treatment is introduced as a sodium salt. [21][16]

**An ammonium phosphate study confirms mixed films.** A 2020 study tested 1–3 vol.% additions of a 0.5 M NH₄H₂PO₄ stock solution in simulated concrete pore solution containing 3.5 wt.% NaCl. The 2 vol.% addition achieved a reported 90% inhibition efficiency after one hour. After 120 hours, XPS and Raman analyses identified goethite, maghemite, and FePO₄ in the protective film. [18]

**The dosage basis matters chemically.** In that example, “2%” refers to the volume addition of the stated stock solution, not a 2 wt.% concentration of pure ammonium phosphate in concrete. Keeping the stock concentration, addition basis, chloride concentration, and exposure period together prevents a laboratory result from becoming a misleading field dosage. The identified mixed film also supports interpreting phosphate inhibition as more than deposition of one pure phosphate phase.

**Existing concrete presents the harder delivery case.** A surface-applied treatment must reach reinforcement through the cover, while an admixture starts distributed within the fresh cementitious material. The penetration and precipitation behavior described in Part One explains why these approaches can yield different results with the same nominal compound. Increasing surface-treatment concentration does not necessarily overcome consumption by the concrete matrix. [16]

**Other families remain distinct alternatives.** Galvanized reinforcement uses a metallic zinc coating and its associated corrosion products, whereas a silicate-containing concrete coating principally modifies the exposed concrete surface. Neither should be reported as the same mechanism as a dissolved phosphate inhibitor acting at bare reinforcing steel. The appropriate comparison is between complete protection systems and their delivery routes, not simply between chemical-family names. [5][25]

### Metal surface treatment: conversion layers support finishing

**Zinc phosphate is a clear conversion example.** The steel surface participates in establishing the local conditions for insoluble zinc-phosphate deposition. The resulting layer changes the interfacial chemistry and provides a useful foundation for subsequent coating. This is a deliberate manufacturing process, unlike relying on an uncontrolled corrosion deposit to become protective during service. [28]

**The final finish determines practical performance.** Iron phosphating is commonly associated with indoor painted or powder-coated articles, while zinc phosphating is used for more demanding outdoor exposure. A phosphate layer’s porosity and its interaction with the subsequent coating influence the final resistance. Consequently, a statement about the performance of a phosphated-and-painted part should not be attributed entirely to the phosphate layer alone. [17]

**The manufacturing objective changes the selection.** Where running-in lubrication and wear behavior are central, manganese phosphating offers a different function from zinc-phosphate paint pretreatment. Where aluminum corrosion control is required, cerium oxide/hydroxide treatment provides another surface-chemistry route. These alternatives belong in the same broad corrosion-control discussion but are not interchangeable processing recipes. [17][13]

**Controlled reaction is the practical criterion.** A successful conversion process balances initial surface reaction with formation of an adherent protective layer and compatibility with finishing. Excessive passivation before phosphate deposition, inappropriate bath chemistry, or an unsuitable subsequent finish can defeat that objective even when the selected chemical belongs to a well-established inhibitor family.

### Boiler water treatment: preserve the protective oxide

**Phosphates mainly control operating chemistry here.** Coordinated phosphate/pH treatment uses orthophosphates to buffer boiler water and prevent excessive free caustic from concentrating beneath deposits or in crevices. The named examples are trisodium phosphate and disodium hydrogen phosphate, with monosodium dihydrogen phosphate also participating in the broader control chemistry. This established treatment approach is documented in the 1991 boiler-water patent. [19]

**Disodium phosphate consumes excess caustic.** The Water Technologies handbook gives the following reaction:

$$\mathrm{Na_2HPO_4(aq)}+\mathrm{NaOH(aq)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Na_3PO_4(aq)}+\mathrm{H_2O(l)}$$

By controlling free caustic, the program reduces conditions that can dissolve the protective magnetite layer and cause caustic gouging or related damage. [30]

**The preserved film is commonly magnetite.** In the handbook’s oxygen-free boiler-water description, steel forms a protective Fe₃O₄ layer. Phosphate treatment helps maintain chemistry compatible with that protection; it should not automatically be described as forming a zinc- or iron-phosphate conversion coating on every boiler tube. The mechanism is environmental control supporting an oxide-protected metal surface. [30]

**More trisodium phosphate is not always better.** Trisodium phosphate can raise alkalinity, while other phosphate forms consume caustic as the distribution shifts. Coordinated control therefore manages phosphate concentration and pH together. High local temperature and deposits can also cause phosphate “hideout,” with phosphate leaving the water during increased load and returning as the load decreases. [30]

**Boiler service reverses some water-treatment priorities.** Oxygen can assist protective-film development in some ambient inhibitor systems, yet oxygen ingress is a major cause of hot-water pitting in boilers and feedwater equipment. Boiler phosphate control consequently operates alongside appropriate deaeration, oxygen control, cleanliness, and thermal management. Transferring an oxygen-dependent cooling-water mechanism directly into boiler service would overlook this fundamental environmental difference. [30]

**The resulting selection is functional.** Phosphate is appropriate in the table because coordinated phosphate/pH control is an established boiler-water application. Its role is to maintain protective operating chemistry, not to provide a universal standalone film-forming cure for oxygen pitting, poor circulation, deposits, and mechanical stress.

**Read the evidence on its stated basis.** This report covers information through early 2023. Surface products may be hydrated or mixed, and their persistence depends on pH and electrochemical conditions. Explicitly labeled overall balances are derived from the cited species and component reactions; calculated percentage comparisons are estimates, not additional experiments. Laboratory efficiencies retain their stated substrates, media, methods, and exposure periods, and annualized corrosion rates from short tests are not service-life forecasts.

**Final judgment: match mechanism to service.** The decisive criterion is whether a treatment maintains the intended chemical or electrochemical protection under actual operating conditions without introducing anode passivation, damaging deposits, or localized attack.
