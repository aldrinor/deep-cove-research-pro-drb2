# National Resources Commission Factories and Site Selection in Hunan and Chongqing, 1937–1945

**The main change was strategic.** The National Resources Commission (NRC) initially sought a connected heavy-industrial base around Xiangtan, combining railway access, the Xiang River, power generation, and proximity to an established commercial center. Wartime development placed greater emphasis on preserving machinery and personnel, rebuilding farther inland, and maintaining production through Chongqing-centered river and resource networks. Transport and urban access remained essential; security increasingly constrained how those advantages could be used. [1][2]

**The factory list needs institutional distinctions.** Central Electrical Equipment, the Changsha radio and electrical-porcelain projects, and Central Copper Smelting are principal Hunan cases. Dadukou, Electrochemical Smelting, and Ziyu are principal Chongqing cases—but represent joint reconstruction, merger, and establishment incorporating an existing foundry, respectively. Hunan’s wartime coal developments and reorganized electricity company also belong in the account, with their establishment status identified explicitly. [3][4]

**The report proceeds from identification to explanation.** The four regional-period tables first distinguish factory origins from later construction, relocation, merger, and operating milestones. The subsequent sections explain the site-selection factors and compare the two periods on railway dependence, waterway dependence, urban relationships, and defense security.

## Scope, chronology, and the meaning of establishment

### The requested periods and geographic boundaries

**August 1938 is the inventory boundary.** “Pre-war” below means before 1 August 1938, while “wartime” means 1 August 1938 through 31 August 1945, following the requested division. Historically, full-scale war began on 7 July 1937, so the first category includes more than a year of actual wartime preparation and relocation. [2]

**Hunan includes its western industrial centers.** The inventory covers the province’s relevant sites at Xiangtan, Changsha, Chaling, Shuikoushan, Yuanling, Chenxi, Hengyang, and the southern Hunan coalfields. Historical locality names are retained because a county, minefield, generating station, and company office describe different kinds of industrial location. Liu Guowu’s provincial history identifies both the Changsha–Xiangtan projects and the later industrial centers around Yuanling–Chenxi and Hengyang–Qiyang. [3]

**Chongqing means a connected industrial region.** It includes Chongqing proper and the closely connected Dadukou–Ba County–Qijiang industrial belt. Contemporary references to Ba County or Qijiang as being in Sichuan are preserved rather than replaced silently with present-day municipal boundaries. Nantong’s coal supply extended toward the Qijiang–Nanchuan–Tongzi border area; that cross-border resource network is distinguished from the location of the Chongqing steelworks it supplied. [5][6]

### What the NRC did—and what counts here

**The NRC built industrial foundations.** Its predecessor, the National Defense Design Commission, was established on 1 November 1932. Reorganization in April 1935 created the NRC, whose work developed from resource investigation and defense planning into industrial construction. Its 1938 reorganization under the Ministry of Economic Affairs gave it responsibility for establishing, operating, and managing industrial, mining, and electric-power enterprises in support of prolonged resistance. [7]

**Industrial development crossed institutional boundaries.** The NRC founded enterprises directly, invested jointly with provincial or commercial partners, and participated in major reconstruction projects with the Ordnance Bureau. It also helped initiate industrial evacuation, but private-factory relocation subsequently became the responsibility of the Industrial and Mining Adjustment Committee and its successor office. Government-assisted relocation therefore does not, by itself, make a private factory an NRC establishment. [2]

**“Major” denotes documented strategic importance.** Inclusion rests on a named connection to the NRC’s principal construction program or early enterprise list, a documented role in supplying essential industrial inputs or power, or an explicit description of substantial regional importance. The early program encompassed metallurgy, machinery, electrical equipment, mining, and power; Dadukou was subsequently described as the largest integrated iron-and-steel enterprise in the rear areas. List-based mining projects are retained as such, without treating program membership as proof of completed large-scale production. [8][5]

The NRC's 21-enterprise list for 1936 through the first half of 1937 and its major-project program provide inclusion anchors alongside strategic industrial function, large integrated capacity, and descriptions such as the largest rear-area integrated steel enterprise; no universal quantitative NRC threshold is stated.

**Establishment is separated from later milestones.** A new enterprise, a new physical plant, a relocated branch, and an acquired mine are different historical events. Accordingly, the tables retain the requested five columns but place a short institutional-status description beside each factory name and identify the kind of date being reported. Supporting mines and utilities appear because the question includes mining and electric industries and because these facilities made factory operation possible.

**Factory numbers are catalog identifiers.** H-PW, C-PW, H-W, and C-W identify Hunan or Chongqing and the requested period. They are editorial serials, not official NRC administrative numbers. When an enterprise appears in both periods, its second entry records a later event in the same enterprise’s history, not another independently founded factory.

### Establishment and classification notes

**Central Electrical has distinct founding milestones.** The factory’s Chinese name was 中央电工器材厂. Its institutional lineage dates to 1936; Liu Guowu places preparation in July, while the site survey occurred in August. Construction began in March 1937, and its second manufacturing unit began producing bulbs, vacuum tubes, and batteries in July 1938. These are successive stages of one project, rather than competing dates for the same event. [9][3][11]

**The four units were internal divisions.** The Xiangtan design included wire, tube-and-bulb, telephone, and motor units, supported by offices, warehouses, accommodation, a dock, and generating infrastructure. By 1938, the account records more than 13,000 square meters of buildings. These units demonstrate the intended breadth of electrical production; they should not automatically be counted as four independently established enterprises. [1]

**Radio names are treated together.** Liu’s project list uses 中央无线电器材厂, while its dated narrative uses 中央无线电机制造厂. The April 1937 Changsha establishment is therefore presented as one provisional identification, preserving both names. Its subsequent move to Guilin in October 1938 belongs to its relocation history, not to an additional Hunan founding. [3]

**Electrical porcelain was modern industrial production.** 中央电瓷制造厂 made electrical porcelain, rather than simply traditional household ceramics. Its place in the early NRC enterprise list supplies a 1936–June 1937 organizational interval, while Liu’s more specific account dates production in Changsha to spring 1938. Classification under the electric industry reflects the product’s industrial function. [8][3]

**Copper founding preceded later smelting.** The Central Copper Smelting Factory, 中央炼铜厂, is explicitly described as NRC-founded at Huangtuling. Its appearance in the early enterprise list supplies the organizational interval used in the table. The often-mentioned 1939 date concerns smelting after relocation to Kunming, where the account reports refined-copper output of 2 metric tons per day; it is not a Hunan founding date. [4]

**Xiangxi crosses the period boundary.** In February 1938, the NRC preparatory office planned a Yuanling main plant and a Chenxi branch to serve relocated military, industrial, and educational establishments. At Xiaolukou, Chenxi, construction began in March using a transferred 500-kilowatt Curtis turbine generator; installation finished in October, and formal supply began on 1 January 1939. Thus the project belongs to the first period by preparation and construction, and to the second by commissioning. [10]

**Mining-list dates identify NRC projects.** Chaling Iron Mine and Xiangtan Coal Mine appear in the history’s group of enterprises established during 1936–June 1937. That interval is retained as an organizational date rather than converted into an individual mine-opening year. Shuikoushan requires an additional distinction: Liu dates its origins to the late Qing, so its appearance in the NRC program represents development of an existing mining enterprise, not creation of the mine itself. [8][3]

**Xiang River power supported the cluster.** The Lingjidu plant was an associated power facility, distinct from Xiangxi Power Plant in western Hunan. The Xiangtan construction account reports 4,000 kilowatts of installed capacity by 1938, alongside a riverside dock and railway-spur roadbed. Its significance lies in making the planned industrial concentration workable, rather than in establishing another electrical-equipment manufacturer. [1]

**Hunan’s early pattern was connected clustering.** The strongest interpretation is a coordinated industrial base around existing towns and transport corridors, with associated resource and power projects—not a collection of completed, isolated defense factories.

Dadukou therefore has both a pre-August origin and a much larger subsequent construction history. [8][5]

### Institutional and chronological distinctions

**Temporary copper is an early-list identification.** 重庆临时炼铜厂 is directly named among the NRC’s 1936–June 1937 enterprises. It establishes an early Chongqing nonferrous-metallurgical project. It is kept distinct from the later Hualongqiao Chongqing Copper Smelter because a common city and industrial function do not establish that two differently named facilities were identical. [8][4]

**Dadukou was jointly created through reconstruction.** The Steel Plant Relocation Committee, 钢铁厂迁建委员会, was organized by the NRC and Ordnance Bureau in March 1938 to dismantle machinery from Hanyang and other Wuhan-area steelworks and rebuild production in the rear. The resulting works combined transferred equipment, new buildings, and newly organized supporting infrastructure. Both institutions must therefore remain visible in its attribution. [12]

**March and July describe different beginnings.** The metallurgical history gives both 1 March and 4 March 1938 for committee formation; March is consequently the appropriate month-level entry. The local Dadukou account dates construction of Qingchuan Yard—the temporary office and single employees’ quarters—to July 1938 and identifies it as the plant’s first building. Major furnace construction followed in September and November. [4][5]

**The mines were integral industrial projects.** Nantong and Qijiang were organized to provide fuel and ore for the reconstructed steel system. The sector history dates their directly affiliated preparatory offices to early April 1938; Wu Guangda’s account places Qijiang preparation in March and records its formal reorganization as Qijiang Iron Mine on 1 March 1940. The table’s March–April interval preserves those organizational stages. [4][6]

**The early Chongqing pattern was already wartime.** Under the requested terminology these projects fall in the “pre-war” table, but their immediate purpose was emergency relocation and reconstruction, demonstrating that August 1938 was not the historical starting point of the strategic shift.

### New organizations versus relocated operations

**Qiling is a dated wartime development.** In November 1938, the NRC established a preparatory office to develop the Qiyang–Lingling coalfields, investigated Yijiaqiao, and acquired land and Maosheng Company mining rights. Formal establishment of 祁零煤矿局 followed in April 1940. This was substantive NRC development, but its incorporation of existing rights makes “new mining organization with acquired assets” more accurate than “an entirely new mine on untouched land.” [13]

**Xiangnan exposes the difficulty of takeover.** The NRC attempted in August 1939 to take over the Xiangnan Coal Mine Bureau associated with the Military Commission chairman’s Guangzhou headquarters. The predecessor resisted transfer, and the NRC’s reorganized Xiangnan Mining Bureau pursued mineral development and support for private mining across several Hunan counties. Its structure included four mining works and a machine shop, with a sales office at Hengyang. [13]

**Administrative creation did not equal inheritance.** Xiangnan is especially important because it prevents an institutional date from being mistaken for the transfer of a complete functioning enterprise. Its subsequent arrangements included managing Dingxin Company’s mine in 1940 and converting that relationship into a joint operation, designated the Third Mine, in 1941. The later name Xiangyong Coal Mine Company belongs to a 1946 reorganization, so it is not used here as a wartime company-founding date. [13]

**Xiangjiang links investment to coal quality.** The provincial archival history explicitly states that the NRC invested jointly in the Xiangjiang Coal Mine Company at Yangjiaqiao in 1943 because its coal was of particularly good quality. The reorganized enterprise became 湘江矿业特种股份有限公司. This is unusually direct evidence connecting a resource characteristic to a specific NRC investment location. [13]

### Electricity and the persistence of urban connections

**Hunan Electrical was a partnership.** 湖南电气股份有限公司 was organized with Hunan Electric Light Company, whose assets and history preceded NRC involvement. The provincial archive dates preparation to January 1941, a founding meeting in Guilin to July, office opening in Changsha to August, and office removal to Hengyang to September. These distinguish corporate formation from the movement of management and from the construction or operation of individual generating stations. [14]

**The NRC supplied majority capital.** Liu reports total capital of 6 million yuan, comprising 3.5 million yuan from the NRC and 2.5 million yuan from Hunan Electric Light Company. The corresponding calculated capital shares are approximately 58.3% and 41.7%, respectively—3.5 ÷ 6 and 2.5 ÷ 6. Majority participation supports substantial NRC influence, but not exclusive NRC creation of every generating asset. [3]

**Zhongliupu identifies the inherited generating site.** The Changsha power history places the predecessor generating plant at Zhongliupu Street beside the Xiang River. That address describes the physical lineage of the utility’s Changsha installation, not a newly selected 1941 factory site. The archival institutional history separately identifies Changsha Power Plant, Hengyang Power Plant, and Xia She Si Generating Station as subordinate units. [15][14]

**Xiangxi supplied an industrial service center.** Its 1939 aggregate output is reported as 1,024,104 kilowatt-hours, serving factories, schools, and government offices at Yuanling and Chenxi. The transferred 500-kilowatt generator describes the Chenxi installation, while the annual output figure covers the Xiangxi enterprise described in the provincial account. Its importance was its enabling role for a relocated community of production and administration. [3][10]

### Period-crossing entries

**Porcelain relocation preserved an existing enterprise.** Liu places the move from Changsha to Yuanling after Wuhan’s fall in October 1938. The estimated October 1938–August 1945 interval uses that event as its lower bound and the study’s wartime endpoint as its upper bound. It dates the Hunan relocation episode, while the earlier organizational and production dates remain attached to the Changsha project. [3]

**Chenxi’s range dates the partnership.** The company is identified as jointly operated by the NRC and Hubei Daye Yuanhua Coal Mine Company. The estimated outer interval, April 1935–August 1945, is bounded by the creation of the NRC under that name and the enterprise’s wartime identification; it is not a point estimate of mine opening. Because the interval crosses August 1938, this row represents a wartime-associated joint enterprise rather than an asserted post-cutoff founding. [3][7]

**Hunan’s wartime development was selective continuity.** Security encouraged departures from exposed sites, while local industrial demand, valuable coal, and existing utility assets justified continued NRC participation.

### Dadukou: establishing a new works from existing machinery

**Dadukou’s achievement was industrial recombination.** Machinery from Hanyang, Daye, Liuhegou, and Shanghai was brought together with new power, ironmaking, steelmaking, rolling, refractory, repair, and transportation facilities. The local history identifies the resulting complex as the largest integrated steel enterprise in the rear areas. Its major status therefore rests on industrial function and integration, not merely on the number of buildings erected. [5]

**Commissioning occurred unit by unit.** The 100-ton furnace project began construction in September 1938 and the 20-ton furnace in November. Dadukou’s local account dates the 20-ton furnace’s operation to 2 March 1940; Wu’s report gives 1 March for its opening, making March 1940 the secure shared milestone. The rail-and-plate shop was built in 1940 and commissioned at the end of 1941, while all eight manufacturing shops were operating in 1942. [4][5][6]

**The later arsenal number is separate.** The designation Ordnance Factory No. 29 is associated with Dadukou’s later institutional history. The detailed metallurgical account dates that renaming to 1 March 1949, outside this inventory’s endpoint. C-W-01 therefore remains an editorial catalog number, while the wartime attribution is the joint NRC–Ordnance Steel Plant Relocation Committee. [4]

### Electrochemical Smelting and Ziyu: two forms of enterprise creation

**Electrochemical formation was a merger.** 电化冶炼厂 was created in June 1941 from the Chongqing Copper Smelter at Hualongqiao and the pure-iron and zinc-smelting operations at Sanxi, Qijiang. Its headquarters were at Sanxi, with four subordinate plants covering electrolytic copper and zinc, pure iron, alloy steel, and steelmaking and rolling. June 1941 consequently dates the combined organization, rather than the physical origin of all its components. [4]

**Its location was a multi-site system.** The enterprise name should not be converted into a single imaginary factory compound. Hualongqiao and Sanxi represented distinct installations brought under common management. The historical significance lies partly in coordinating complementary processes and equipment across those locations, demonstrating that wartime industrial concentration could be organizational as well as spatial. [18]

**Ziyu has a formal establishment date.** 资渝钢铁厂 entered preparation in October 1941, took over Dahua Foundry in April 1942, and formally became a factory on 6 July 1943. Its reported production capacity was 400 metric tons of steel per month. That is capacity, not a record that every month achieved that output. [4]

**Ziyu combined acquisition with construction.** Its history describes locally designed and installed small converters, cupolas, rolling equipment, power generation, repair, and transportation facilities. Incorporating Dahua did not make the entire Ziyu undertaking a simple inheritance; nor does the 1943 founding date erase the 1942 takeover and production episode. The correct description is a newly organized NRC-sponsored works incorporating an existing foundry. [18]

**The address has historical variants.** The metallurgical history gives Ganjiabei, 甘家碑, in Jingkou Township, Ba County. Ziyu’s land-acquisition file uses Ganjiapi, 甘家埤, and records the county government’s reply that the relevant places had already been incorporated into Chongqing’s urban area. Both forms are retained in the interpretation because they expose the changing administrative geography around the plant. [19]

### Branches, mines, and further construction

**The electrical branch extended an enterprise.** Central Electrical’s institutional history places the move from Xiangtan toward branches in Kunming, Guilin, and Chongqing within the relocation program beginning in October 1938. The Chongqing entry therefore records branch formation through relocation, rather than an independently founded electrical company. Its industrial role remained the manufacture of electrical goods needed by the war economy. [16]

**Mining formalization supported continuing production.** Qijiang’s 1940 formal organization and Nantong’s continuing shaft and coke developments belong to the same supply system as Dadukou. Their presence in both tables makes the transition visible: preparatory organizations created before August 1938 became producing, transporting, and administratively consolidated operations afterward. [6]

**Dajian remained a construction-stage entry.** A 1942–1943 file records a proposal to establish a future plant near Xiannü Cave at Puhe, close to Nantong coal, specifically to facilitate large-scale production and reduce raw-material transportation difficulties. Engineering personnel, a surveying team, land acquisition, and construction arrangements followed. The row records that development project, while its explicit rationale supplies strong evidence about site selection. [17]

**Chongqing’s pattern was integrated reconstruction.** The wartime industrial base grew through the combination of transferred machinery, new facilities, mines, branches, and mergers, making “NRC involvement” broader than a count of entirely new factory sites.

## The factors governing factory-site selection

**The decisive question was operational feasibility.** A secure site still needed deliverable raw materials, usable machinery, utilities, workers, and connections to customers and supervising agencies. The evidence supports eight related categories, summarized below and then developed through factory-level examples.

| Factor category | Principal consideration | Named evidence | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resources | Quantity, quality, and delivered cost | Xiangjiang coal; Nantong–Qijiang supply system | Locate extraction at deposits and processing within a workable supply network |
| Railway transport | Main-line links and local haulage | Xiangtan corridor; Nantong light railway | Separate regional access from mine-to-river movement |

### Resources: usable inputs mattered more than deposits alone

**Xiangjiang offers a direct resource rationale.** The NRC’s 1943 investment at Yangjiaqiao was explicitly attributed to the quality of its coal. This is stronger evidence of an investment-location mechanism than a general statement that Hunan possessed coal. Quality affected the value of the existing mine and helps explain why the NRC entered a partnership there instead of treating every coalfield as interchangeable. [13]

**Steel required a connected resource bundle.** The rear-area steel account identifies southwestern coal and iron distribution, reserves, and transportation conditions as the basis for inland construction. Dadukou’s reconstruction was therefore accompanied by Nantong coal development and Qijiang iron mining. The relevant geography was a system of complementary inputs, not the shortest distance to a single mineral deposit. [4]

**Material quality constrained effective capacity.** Wu Guangda’s engineering account attributed the 20-ton furnace’s failure to reach expected output to insufficient blast, excessive sulfur and ash in coke, impurities in ore, and low hot-blast temperature. A large coal reserve was consequently not equivalent to an adequate supply of suitable metallurgical fuel. Site assessment had to connect geology with the actual requirements of the production process. [6]

**The Puhe proposal shifted toward coal.** The proposed new plant near Xiannü Cave was expressly intended to reduce raw-material transportation difficulty through proximity to Nantong. This provides a revealing contrast with Dadukou’s advantages as an equipment-receiving and urban-connected location. Once machinery had been rescued and reconstructed, the repeated cost and difficulty of moving fuel could justify another location within the industrial network. [17]

**The resource judgment is conditional.** Mine proximity was particularly compelling for bulk-consuming metallurgy, but less decisive for products whose operation depended more strongly on skilled personnel, utilities, imported components, and customer access. The evidence therefore supports resource-sensitive sectoral choices, not one universal “build beside the mine” rule.

### Railway transportation: regional access and industrial haulage

**Xiangtan was planned around rail–river access.** The 1936 corridor account names the Yuehan, Xiangqian, and Zhejiang–Jiangxi railways in describing the intended heavy-industrial zone along the Xiang River from Bantangpu and Xia She Si toward Zhuzhou. It also identifies the Ban–She railway spur, whose roadbed had been completed by 1938. Railway integration was thus an explicit feature of the planned NRC industrial base. [1]

**Planning should remain distinct from service.** The railway names establish the planners’ intended network advantages; the spur’s recorded roadbed stage identifies a specific level of physical progress. Neither fact requires treating every proposed connection in the industrial design as a fully operating railway. The important comparison is that Xiangtan’s selected setting offered a railway-oriented development strategy.

**Dadukou valued a future trunk connection.** Wu listed the proposed Chengdu–Chongqing railway alignment outside the works as an advantage if a connection could eventually be made. Britannica dates completion of that railway to 1952, after the war. The wartime site could therefore be selected partly for anticipated railway access while depending on other modes for its immediate operation. [6][24]

**Wartime industrial railways nevertheless remained important.** Wu describes local lines carrying Qijiang ore and Nantong coal to river-transfer points. Nantong’s first unit had a 17-kilometer light railway to Puhe, with one-ton vehicles pushed by workers; the account reports daily transport of about 130 metric tons. These were practical industrial-haulage facilities, not substitutes for a national trunk railway. [6]

**The railway trade-off was scale-specific.** Main-line access could connect a factory to wider markets and distant supplies, while mine railways solved shorter collection and transfer problems. The wartime change was reduced immediate reliance on a completed trunk connection at the principal Chongqing reconstruction site—not abandonment of rails within its supply chain.

### Waterway transportation: access, capacity, and bottlenecks

**Dadukou’s stated advantages were strongly river-based.** Wu’s site appraisal emphasized that heavy machinery could come directly from Yichang and that coal could arrive from the Jialing, Qijiang, and upper Yangtze routes. Its Yangtze frontage simultaneously supported construction, fuel procurement, and movement of products and materials. This was an explicit factory-level rationale, rather than merely a description of Chongqing as a river city. [6]

**The relocation fleet shows operational dependence.** The Steel Plant Relocation Committee’s report to early March 1939 listed 11 sea-going ships, four iron barges, 17 tugboats, 218 wooden barges, and 7,000 Sichuan River sailing boats. The movement began on 5 June 1938, crossing the requested period boundary. These counts document the breadth of waterborne mobilization, while the report separately records dismantled railway tracks, vehicles, and locomotive-repair equipment. [25]

**River access contained several separate problems.** Wu distinguished the comparatively manageable main-river movement from difficult Qijiang tributary transport and from unloading within the factory. A site could be readily reached by machinery from downstream yet remain constrained in its daily supply of ore and coke from upstream mines. The location of the factory, the capacity of its feeder routes, and its landing equipment must therefore be assessed together. [6]

| Part of the Dadukou supply system | Reported quantity or planning basis | Operational meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Main-river transport | About 800 metric tons/day estimated practical capacity | Large-river access offered substantial movement capacity |
| Qijiang water transport | About 250 metric tons/day targeted average | Feeder transport constrained resource delivery |
| Factory unloading | Below 200 metric tons/day reported average | Materials could accumulate beside the works |
| Full-works input requirement | About 800 metric tons/day; 1,000-ton planning allowance | Nominal production required much more than river frontage |

**Handling could undermine transport advantages.** At the report’s approximate full-works requirement of 800 metric tons per day, unloading below 200 metric tons per day would cover less than 25% of that requirement if the material passed through the reported landing system—200 ÷ 800. This is a conditional comparison between planned demand and reported handling, not a measured production-loss percentage. It explains why a well-positioned riverside factory could still suffer shortages while material waited nearby. [6]

**Navigation was also an engineered resource.** A historical account of Qijiang river improvement describes an initial navigable course of 133 kilometers with 127 dangerous shoals. Boats carrying roughly 4–5 metric tons required seven or eight days; subsequent channelization and locks permitted 10–30-ton wooden boats and reduced the journey to about two days. The works were therefore supported by infrastructure construction that changed the usefulness of their location. [26]

**Waterway dependence persisted after evacuation.** The same account reports more than 420,000 metric tons of raw materials delivered to Dadukou during 1939–1945. This distinguishes a continuing production network from the one-time rescue of machines. The site-selection implication is that the NRC and its partners needed reliable repeated movements, not simply an accessible route for the first shipment. [26]

### Electric power and water: making a site productive

**Power could be built with the factory.** Xiangtan’s 4,000-kilowatt supporting plant shows that planners did not merely choose a site with existing electricity and begin manufacturing. They developed power as part of the industrial base. The close relationship between generation and electrical manufacturing also gave the cluster a supporting infrastructure beyond the individual workshops. [1]

**Chenxi illustrates demand-led power siting.** Its generating plant was planned because relocated arsenals, the naval torpedo establishment, textile and cement works, and Hunan University required electricity. The NRC transferred an existing generator and built a local installation to serve that concentration. In this case, the movement of consumers helped determine the location of the utility, reversing a simple assumption that factories always followed an established electricity supply. [10]

**Dadukou required water engineering too.** Wu’s account describes water supply, drainage, river intake and pumping installations, a water tower, and cooling-water arrangements as parts of reconstruction. Water was necessary not only as a transport route but also as a production input for steam-power and cooling systems. River frontage therefore had multiple uses, although pumping and distribution infrastructure still had to connect the river to machinery. [6]

**Flood safety complicated river proximity.** Wu also listed the site’s elevation more than roughly 10 meters above flood level as an advantage. The desirable combination was access to the river without exposing expensive fixed installations to inundation. This environmental-security consideration was distinct from protection against Japanese attack, even though both affected whether a factory could operate continuously. [6]

**Utilities made security operationally meaningful.** Moving inland protected equipment from immediate capture only if power, water, fuel, and maintenance systems could sustain it. An isolated site with inadequate utilities could preserve machinery physically while leaving it unable to contribute production.

### Labor and technical expertise: capability moved with people

**Central Electrical planned specialized personnel early.** Its 1936 preparations treated personnel selection, investigation, and training as important tasks and included emergency mobilization of specialists. This makes technical capability part of the project’s original organization, rather than a secondary matter considered only after construction. [27]

**Relocation transferred an industrial workforce.** The Dadukou program involved engineers supervising dismantling and reconstruction, while its associated mines and transport systems required large numbers of manual workers. Sichuan’s support measures facilitated labor recruitment alongside electricity supply and raw-material procurement. The success of a site thus depended partly on bringing skilled people with the machinery and obtaining additional local labor. [4][2]

**Ziyu demonstrates the value of engineering capacity.** Its works used its own technical personnel to design, manufacture, install, and commission equipment, combining locally produced machinery with metallurgical processes suited to the circumstances. The reported construction sequence shows how an enterprise could compensate for restricted external equipment supply through technical organization. That capacity was not supplied automatically by mineral deposits or available land. [18]

**Workforce retention required supporting facilities.** Ziyu’s engineering file includes worker housing, roads, and a power-house expansion, while the Dadukou history describes technical training and employee facilities. These investments connected industrial location with everyday life and the reproduction of technical skills. The inference is operational: a rear-area industrial site had to accommodate a workforce, not merely contain machines. [19][28]

**Technical planning differs from labor abundance.** The evidence establishes recruitment, training, transferred expertise, and accommodation as important conditions. It does not justify assuming that every chosen locality already possessed an ample skilled workforce; in several cases, building that workforce was part of establishing the industrial base.

### Markets and urban access: the benefits of connected peripheries

**Dadukou was selected partly for Chongqing.** Wu explicitly identified proximity to Sichuan’s commercial and industrial center as an advantage for obtaining construction materials and selling products. He also noted convenience for supervision by the responsible authorities. Its location approximately 20 kilometers upstream therefore combined physical separation with economic and administrative access. [6]

**Xiangtan likewise retained an urban relationship.** Xia She Si lay across the Xiang River from the county town, rather than in an entirely detached mountain location. Its selected setting linked large industrial land requirements to an established settlement and commercial corridor. The original plan sought a new industrial concentration beside existing economic activity, not industrial self-sufficiency through geographic isolation. [11]

**The machine-factory debate exposes the cost.** In February 1938, two engineers opposing Central Machine’s proposed move to Yunnan argued that inconvenient transport would complicate raw-material procurement and dispatching staff to install customers’ machines. They also anticipated higher operating costs and poorer information exchange. Their objections reveal why a defensible rearward location could be commercially and technically less attractive than a more connected Hunan site. [21]

**Electricity businesses followed concentrated demand.** Xiangxi served factories, schools, and offices, while Hunan Electrical organized operations around Changsha, Hengyang, Xiangtan, and neighboring areas. These were urban-service relationships as well as industrial ones. Their geography shows that wartime demand and coordination could sustain connections to towns even when parts of the manufacturing system moved farther inland. [3][14]

**Urban access remained a positive consideration.** The relevant choice was usually how to preserve access while reducing exposure and obtaining suitable land. A general rule of maximizing distance from cities would misdescribe both Dadukou’s stated advantages and Hunan’s utility geography.

### Land, construction, and administration: buildable sites

**Xiangtan required coordinated land acquisition.** The three-factory plan covered nearly 10,000 mu, according to the commemorative history, with acquisition handled by Xiangtan County and Xiangjiatang Township authorities over three months. Such a project required more than identifying a favorable point on a map: it needed a large, administratively obtainable landholding suitable for coordinated construction. [27]

**Large plans still faced construction constraints.** Central Electrical’s institutional history describes slow progress because of funding, environmental, and transportation difficulties. By the end of 1937, only part of the offices, workshops, warehouses, and employee facilities had been completed. The distinction between acquiring a site and creating a productive works was therefore substantial even before the major evacuation crisis. [16]

**Dadukou accepted costly terrain.** Wu described uneven ground broken by hills and streams, anticipating higher building expenditure. This disadvantage existed alongside direct heavy-equipment delivery, urban supply access, and flood safety. The selected site was a compromise among competing benefits and construction costs, rather than a location ideal on every dimension. [6]

**Ziyu encountered jurisdictional friction.** Its preparatory office sought assistance from Sichuan and Ba County officials to negotiate land prices and obtain early use of plots. Ba County replied that the relevant areas had already entered Chongqing’s urban jurisdiction and declined to handle the acquisition on that basis. Administrative geography thus affected the practical ability to secure land, even when the industrial locality had been chosen. [19]

**Provincial assistance could improve feasibility.** Sichuan’s government instructed officials to choose transport-convenient industrial areas, help relocated enterprises purchase land and build factories, and prevent obstruction by landowners. These measures reduced the administrative costs of reconstruction. They were general relocation policies, but they help explain why the institutional environment around a site mattered alongside its physical geography. [2]

### Defense, concealment, and vulnerability

**Strategic depth changed the regional choice.** Liu’s Hunan account states that military-related key construction increasingly favored Yunnan, Guizhou, and Sichuan because Hunan faced substantial risk of Japanese advance. Central Electrical’s movement from Xiangtan after the October 1938 crisis gives this regional argument a concrete enterprise-level expression. Defense considerations could override the benefits that had originally favored a connected Hunan base. [3][11]

**Inland sites still required local protection.** Dadukou had a Half-Bishan air-raid shelter under construction in 1938, initially excavated for 28 meters. Ziyu’s engineering file includes stone lining for an air-raid shelter. These are direct examples associated with NRC-involved enterprises and show that being in the rear did not eliminate the need for protection. [5][19]

**Camouflage was also used at Dadukou.** The 2025 China Steel News account describes disguising workshops and chimneys to reduce exposure to Japanese bombing. This establishes concealment as part of wartime factory protection at the joint works. It is evidence of adaptation at an operating site, rather than proof that concealment was the original reason for choosing Dadukou. [28]

**Chenxi protected equipment after construction.** From 1939, frequent raids prompted the power plant to build steel-frame, iron-plate, and sand-filled protective works above its generating equipment. The account reports 30 metric tons of steel and an approximately 120-ton load-bearing design. This was investment in keeping an already selected installation functioning under attack. [10]

**Terrain descriptions need their stated rationale.** Chenxi’s Xiaolukou site was backed by mountains and bordered by rivers, but the account does not make that description an explicit original concealment argument. Similarly, the proposed plant near Xiannü Cave was justified expressly by proximity to coal and reduced transport difficulty. A place-name containing “cave” should not be converted into evidence that the factory was planned underground. [10][17]

**Ordnance caves provide a useful contrast.** The extensive production tunnels at Chongqing’s First and Fiftieth Ordnance factories demonstrate a more pronounced form of underground industrial protection. They clarify what strong evidence for tunnel-based manufacture looks like, while their Ordnance affiliation keeps them outside the NRC-established catalog. [29]

**The overall siting judgment is integrated.** Resources, transport, utilities, people, markets, land, and security operated together; the factory cases support different balances among them, not a single ranked NRC formula applied everywhere.

## Pre-war versus wartime site-selection strategies

**The shift was survival plus connectivity.** Before the requested cutoff, Xiangtan planning sought a coordinated rail–river industrial base. Subsequent development increasingly combined inland reconstruction, river logistics, resource-linked outlying sites, and local dispersal. The retained importance of Chongqing’s markets, suppliers, and supervising agencies shows that survival did not mean abandoning industrial concentration.

| Comparison axis | Before August 1938 | August 1938–August 1945 | Resulting judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Railway reliance | Xiangtan corridor and dedicated-spur planning | Dadukou prospective trunk connection; working mine railways | Less immediate trunk-rail dependence in the principal reconstruction case |
| Waterway reliance | Xiang River frontage and dock | Yangtze evacuation plus continuing Qijiang and tributary supply | Greater operational centrality of river networks |
| Urban relationship | Xia She Si across from Xiangtan town; Changsha projects | Dadukou about 20 km upstream; Ziyu within an expanding urban jurisdiction | Peripheral connection, not universal urban withdrawal |
| Defense security | Inland defense planning already present | Urgent evacuation, dispersal, shelters, camouflage, and hardening | Security became more immediate and operational |
| Industrial organization | Planned neighboring factories and supporting utilities | Reassembled works, mergers, branches, mines, and transport agencies | Concentration became more network-based |
| Implementation sequence | Survey, acquire land, construct a coordinated base | Salvage, transport, reconstruct, commission units, and correct bottlenecks | Wartime construction was more incremental and adaptive |

### Railway reliance: a qualified but substantive difference

**Xiangtan integrated railways into the original design.** The corridor’s railway setting and dedicated-spur project were part of the reason to concentrate major industrial construction there. They promised connections among the industrial base, resource areas, and wider markets. Even though construction progress varied, railway integration formed a positive original attraction. [1]

**Dadukou proceeded without its desired trunk connection.** Its appraisal treated a future Chengdu–Chongqing link as beneficial, while immediate heavy-equipment delivery and fuel transport were organized around rivers. The contrast is meaningful: the steel reconstruction could not wait for the transport network imagined for its eventual development. The availability of a usable river route made an otherwise imperfect rear-area site practicable. [6]

**Local railways qualify the simple contrast.** Nantong’s light railway and Qijiang’s ore-haulage lines show continued reliance on rail technology within the resource network. The wartime strategy combined modes at different scales: workers and small vehicles moved material toward river landings, and boats carried it farther through the industrial region. The distinction is between trunk-network dependence and local industrial haulage, not between using and rejecting railways. [6]

**Geography helps explain the change.** The comparison involves both a change in war conditions and movement from Hunan into a different transport environment. It therefore supports a case-based judgment about the infrastructure available to reconstruction, rather than attributing every difference to a new NRC preference. A stronger claim of deliberate rejection of rail would require a documented choice against an equally feasible operating railway alternative.

### Waterway reliance: from an advantage to a production requirement

**Water mattered in both periods.** Xiangtan’s river frontage and dock were built into the original industrial design, so wartime river use was not an entirely new principle. What changed most clearly was the range of tasks for which rivers became essential: rescuing heavy machinery, establishing the new works, supplying bulk inputs, and connecting production units. [1][6]

**Equipment evacuation intensified the dependence.** The joint steel committee’s 1939 fleet demonstrates a movement system organized across numerous river craft and transfer points. Once heavy equipment had been brought to Dadukou, its riverside position also reduced the need for another difficult overland movement to the factory site. The relationship between the evacuation route and the receiving location helped lock the reconstructed enterprise into the river network. [25]

**Recurring inputs reinforced the initial choice.** Wu’s furnace schedule listed 160 metric tons of ore, 100 of coke, 80 of limestone, and five of manganese material per day—345 metric tons for that itemized furnace-input schedule alone. His estimate for the broader works was approximately 800 metric tons daily, with provision planned for 1,000 because routes and river levels varied. Such repeated bulk movements made feeder navigation and unloading central to the viability of the chosen site. [6]

**Infrastructure investment changed effective accessibility.** Qijiang improvements show that planners did not simply accept the existing river network as fixed. Locks, channelization, landing arrangements, and dedicated transport administration increased the usefulness of the selected industrial geography. The wartime strategy consequently included constructing accessibility, not merely choosing accessible places. [26]

**The waterway judgment concerns dependence, not ease.** Wartime reconstruction relied more centrally on rivers, while the difficult tributary routes and landing bottlenecks demonstrate the costs of that reliance. The conclusion would weaken only if equally substantial factory-level evidence showed another mode carrying the principal evacuation and recurring bulk-supply functions in these cases.

### Distance from urban centers: peripheral but connected

**Dadukou supplies a concrete distance anchor.** Wu located the works approximately 20 kilometers upstream from Chongqing, on the Yangtze near Dadukou in historical Ba County. He nevertheless described Chongqing as near enough to facilitate construction-material supply, product sales, and administrative supervision. Distance was therefore evaluated through access and function, not as an end in itself. [6]

**Xia She Si was also peripheral.** Its position across the river from Xiangtan town already combined separation with proximity. The comparison is not a straightforward transition from an urban-center factory to a remote rural factory. Rather, Dadukou extended the connected-periphery principle within a much deeper rear-area region and a different transport setting. [11]

**Ziyu counters universal remoteness.** Its acquisition file shows that the intended site had entered Chongqing’s expanding municipal jurisdiction. Hunan Electrical’s offices and service territory likewise maintained urban links during wartime reorganization. Together, these cases show that the NRC’s industrial system could move farther from the front while retaining, or becoming incorporated into, an urban economy. [19][14]

**Strategic depth differs from urban distance.** A factory could be far from Japanese ground forces but close to a rear-area commercial center. Conversely, being outside a town did not itself provide protection from aircraft. The best-supported conclusion is selective peripheral siting within connected industrial regions, rather than a uniform increase in factory-to-city distance.

### Defense and concealment: distinguish choice from adaptation

**Defense was present before the cutoff.** The early NRC program explicitly sought to strengthen national defense, and its projects were located inland partly for that purpose. The later shift therefore should not be described as the first discovery of security considerations. It was a reassessment of how much strategic depth was required as Japanese advances overturned earlier expectations. [8]

**The Hunan base became too exposed.** Central Electrical’s July 1938 production was followed by evacuation after Wuhan’s fall and bombing at Xia She Si. The same place that offered transport and industrial-clustering advantages now carried an unacceptable risk to machinery and personnel. Its move into branches illustrates security overriding the original concentration without discarding the enterprise’s technical capability. [11][16]

**Chongqing offered depth but not immunity.** Dadukou remained subject to bombing and adopted camouflage and shelters. At Chenxi, mountains and rivers did not remove the perceived need for heavy protective works over generating equipment. Inland relocation reduced particular risks, while local engineering addressed the continuing vulnerability of fixed industrial installations. [28][10]

**Dispersal occurred within concentrated systems.** Wu records that the steel-rail and steel-plate shops were originally intended near the steel-bar shop but were assigned another site because of dispersal requirements. This is direct evidence that air-defense considerations affected layout inside the joint steel enterprise. It fits the broader policy of “large concentration, small dispersion”: retain a connected industrial region while separating vulnerable installations locally. [6][2]

**Original concealment needs explicit evidence.** Shelters, camouflage, protective roofs, and later separation of workshops establish wartime defensive practice. They should be distinguished from an original decision to choose one geographic site over another because it was concealed. For Dadukou, Wu’s explicit advantages emphasize transport, markets, flood safety, prospective rail access, and supervision; those statements support a more balanced explanation than concealment alone.

**Security’s role became more operational.** The strongest comparison is from anticipatory national-defense industrialization toward urgent preservation, repeated relocation, protected operation, and local dispersal. Concealment became one important tool within that strategy, but the factory evidence does not make it the sole or universal determinant.

### Continuity and change in industrial concentration

**The industrial-system principle remained constant.** Xiangtan’s planned factories required neighboring power and transport infrastructure; Dadukou required mines, coke, power, refractories, repair facilities, and river administration. Both approaches sought mutually supporting industrial activities. The continuity was the need to assemble a workable production system rather than establish an isolated manufacturing building. [1][6]

**Wartime concentration became more distributed.** Electrochemical Smelting united installations at Hualongqiao and Sanxi, while Dadukou drew resources from outlying mines and considered additional production closer to coal. These arrangements combined managerial coordination with spatial separation. They preserved some benefits of concentration while accommodating resource geography, inherited equipment, transport limitations, and defense needs. [4][17]

**Reconstruction also became more incremental.** Dadukou commissioned facilities as they became ready rather than waiting for the whole complex. Ziyu incorporated a working foundry before formal establishment, and Hunan’s coal organizations combined acquisition, partnership, and development. These sequences reflect the need to obtain useful production quickly under uncertainty, even when the final industrial arrangement remained incomplete. [5][4][13]

**The cutoff separates records, not mechanisms.** Xiangxi construction, Dadukou organization, and associated mining preparation all began before August 1938 but continued through the later period. The historical transition developed across those projects as military conditions changed. Treating August as an abrupt replacement of one siting doctrine by another would obscure the very enterprises that best explain the change.

**Evidence and measurement are bounded.** This is a source-supported catalog of major identified projects and associated developments, rather than a regional enterprise census; its serials are editorial, locality descriptions retain their historical geographic scale, and dates distinguish organization, construction, production, relocation, and acquisition. The explicitly labeled Chenxi and porcelain intervals are estimated outer bounds; other calculations use stated source inputs, metric tons, kilowatts, kilowatt-hours, or nominal historical yuan. Transport capacities are separated from actual throughput, and the urban comparison uses historical settlement relationships and Dadukou’s reported approximately 20-kilometer upstream distance rather than a matched statistical average.

**The final historical judgment is clear.** The NRC’s development moved from a planned, highly connected Hunan industrial concentration toward a deeper rear-area system combining reconstruction, river logistics, resource-linked sites, and defensive adaptation. Its central achievement was not simply finding remote places: it was preserving and reconnecting the materials, machinery, people, utilities, and institutions required to keep industry producing under war conditions.

**Additional documented evidence**

The allowed evidence supports a qualified contrast: before **1 August 1938**, NRC planning in central Hunan combined resource access, river geography, planned rail or dock infrastructure, and a regional defense-industrial objective; from **1 August 1938 through 31 August 1945**, war-related disruption made relocation, multimodal contingency planning, incomplete transport construction, and—where directly documented—concealment more prominent. This is directional rather than statistical because the evidence does not provide a common transport scale, comparable kilometre distances, or a complete period denominator.[163][173]

**Urban distance and defense evidence**

Pre-war security evidence is explicit at the regional-policy level: central Hunan was considered strategically important and was planned as a national-defense industrial center between Xiangtan, Liling, and Hengyang. The sources do not, however, state that Central Steel Plant itself was selected for concealment or dispersion.[163] Hilly terrain at Xiasheshi was described as offering protection from ground forces and air strikes, but the evidence concerns the associated industrial complex and cannot automatically be assigned to Central Steel Plant alone.[101] After Wuhan fell, the NRC used the same general planning procedure in moving the Xiasheshi industries to Kunming; later dispersion of electrical facilities was explicitly intended to reduce the risk of one strike destroying the whole industry.[191]

Direct concealment evidence comes from a non-target Kunming comparator, not Hunan or Chongqing. After bombing damage in **July 1941**, a safe and concealed temporary power facility was built in a mine cave and commissioned in **June 1943**; the original Kunhu site was **9 km** from Kunming, but no comparable distance was supplied for the cave facility.[174][51] This demonstrates a wartime security mechanism without proving that remoteness alone represented NRC concealment strategy in the target sample.

The Hunan Electric Company is also a qualified case: NRC planning began in **January 1941**, its founding meeting occurred in Guilin in July, and it began operating in Changsha in August; its office moved to Hengyang in September because Japanese forces entered Hunan. The source describes a cooperative enterprise and attributes the move to invasion, not concealment.[180] Finally, the Central Machine Plant’s 1939 Kunming site and its later temporary Longling facility show how railway closure, seized equipment, road constraints, and a **10-ton** bridge limit altered wartime production planning from **3,600** to **300 vehicles per year**; this is an external comparator, not a target-geography observation.[170]

**Counts and evidence limits**

No defensible pre-war/wartime factory count is calculated from this source group. The available catalogue merely lists a 1939 relocated-factory register, a 1943 Chongqing enterprise-location table, and 1942–1945 Jialing River coal-transport records without reproducing their entries, denominators, distances, or reliance measures.[167] Wartime transport-system collapse, resource scarcity, and administrative competition are alternative explanations that must be separated from deliberate NRC strategy.[117] General policy evidence did require planners to consider transport geography and secure rear areas, but Northwest examples cannot be substituted for the Hunan–Chongqing sample.[168] Pages about the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission and nuclear-site security concern a different institution and are not evidence for this historical comparison.[164][80]

**Additional case evidence**

**Central Machine Factory (中央机器厂).** Planned for Xiangtan, it was moved after the war began; Ciziba, northern Kunming, was selected in April 1938 because Xiangtan was near the front and vulnerable to air attack. More than 5,000 tons of equipment departed Zhuzhou in May, partly via Guangzhou–Hong Kong–Haiphong and the Yunnan–Vietnam Railway. Ciziba was explicitly described as concealed and surrounded by mountains on three sides. The buildings were completed in June 1939; the NRC characterized it as China’s first large state-run machine factory. A 1941 bombing led to continued production in caves and dispersed workshops. These March–April 1938 decisions fall before the report’s 1 August 1938 cutoff, although they occurred during the war. [141][64]

**Xiangtan Coal Mine (湘潭煤矿).** The company was formally established on 1 December 1937, with NRC holding 51% of its 2 million-yuan capital; it later had 10 shafts and output of approximately 400 tons per day, mostly sent to Changsha and Wuhan. This is evidence of pre-cutoff resource, demand, and supply logic, but not of a measured urban distance or documented defense motive. By contrast, the 1940 Qiling Coal Mine was developed to supply locomotive coal for the Xiang–Gui Railway, and in 1944 the NRC ordered equipment, documents, and personnel prepared for evacuation. [157]

**Central Steel Plant and Chongqing Dadukou.** Central Steel Plant is listed as an NRC enterprise; the Steel Plant Relocation Committee was formed on 1 March 1938 and selected Dadukou for reconstruction. Dadukou had a railway connection and access to the Yangtze, Jialing, and Qijiang systems; 56,000 tons of equipment moved by the Yangtze during 1938–1939, and more than 420,000 tons of raw materials reached Dadukou during 1939–1945. Qijiang navigation was constrained by 127 shoals and four- to five-ton boats before improvement, but later supported 10–30-ton boats. Concealment and air-defense camouflage are documented for 1938–1943, as wartime responses rather than proof of the original site motive. [189][60][134]

Across the evidence, the NRC’s 1936 focus on the two Hu provinces and Jiangxi shifted toward inland southwest priorities as the war expanded; the reasons stated include security, resources, geography, agriculture, and wartime transport. Zhuzhou planning also combined seven proposed factories with mineral resources, Xiang River shipping, and intersecting railways. [132][55] Transport evidence supports access and actual use, but not a complete comparable none/low/medium/high coding. A non-NRC relocation comparator reports Beibei as 140 li by land or 100 li by water from Chongqing and rejected partly for remoteness and market access; the unit and method are not convertible into the required common kilometre series. [122]

Aggregate counts should not be treated as NRC denominators: the 687 factories and mines moved to the rear by 1940—647 private and 40 state-owned—include many non-NRC enterprises; the 1942–1945 enterprise count is a proxy, and identified 1942 and 1945 Chongqing tables provide no denominator or missing-data rule. The official NRC archive nevertheless records engineering, factory-site, land-acquisition, and transport series, supporting further verification rather than a complete count. [79][116][56] The 31-km 1893 Daye railway and modern U.S. nuclear-siting guidance are out-of-scope comparators, not evidence for Chinese NRC strategy. [166][146]

**Hunan–Chongqing comparison evidence**

Hunan’s NRC coal fonds lists state/joint bureaus, companies and plants but is incomplete (935 volumes, 1932–1950) [61]. Chongqing catalogues list NRC electrical, steel, smelting, hydropower and chemical establishments [176]. NRC-wide preparation rose from 21 enterprises in 1936–37 to 98 by 1945 (57 independent, 41 joint), not a Hunan/Chongqing denominator [112]; six NRC-invested enterprises supplied 46% of rear-area pig iron and 56% of steel by 1945 [71].

In Hunan, NRC designated Xiangtan Xia She Si an industrial base in 1936, establishing Central Steel, Central Electrical, Central Machinery and Xiangjiang Power [162]. Central Electrical selected the site in August 1936 and began construction in March 1937; four plants operated by 1939 [181]. Its stated trade-off was an inland, safer site on the Xiangjiang, near the Yuehan and Zhejiang–Jiangxi railways and major roads [109]. Wartime branches moved to Kunming and Guilin; a separately reported Hunan Electrical Manufacturing Plant moved from Changsha in September 1936 to Guilin in 1938 and then Kunming because Guilin became unsafe [109][145]. Central Porcelain moved from Changsha through Yuanling, Hengyang and Guiyang to Yibin (1939; production 1942); transport and material shortages constrained output, with Hunan branch works recorded from January 1942 to July 1944 [78][108]. The February 1938 Xiangxi Power decision placed the main plant at Yuanling and a branch at Chenxi, backed by mountains and bordered by rivers, to supply relocated institutions [139].

In Chongqing, NRC and the Ordnance Bureau formed the Steel Factory Relocation Committee in March 1938 to move Wuhan equipment to Sichuan [118][114]. Dadukou, about 20 km upstream, used Yangtze access, Yichang machinery, coal from the Jialing–Qijiang–upper-Yangtze system and an anticipated Chengdu–Chongqing rail link; elevation above 10 m reduced flood risk, while rough terrain and Qijiang transport raised costs [82]. A wartime hydropower decision selected Xiannü Cave, about 5 km from the Yangtze mouth, over Modaoxi because transmission was shorter and transport easier [175]. South Tong combined high-quality coking coal with a remote site: 1,018 hectares and 1 million yuan were invested in October 1938, output rose from about 5,000 tons in 1939 to 120,000 tons in 1942, and it entered the NRC/Steel Committee system on 1 March 1940 [137].

**Comparison evidence**

NRC-linked evidence supports Chongqing as a wartime rear-industrial base that shifted from pre-war water-oriented access to wartime multimodal logistics [130][40]. Before the cutoff, water transport was developed while road and aviation remained nascent: Chongqing had 56 steamships (22,344 tonnes) in 1936, 288 (64,033 tonnes) in 1941, and 16,369 wooden boats (256,600 tonnes) in 1944. From 1938–1941, 56 navigation stations were built—18 on the Yangtze and 26 on the Jialing—and rear supplies mainly used the Sichuan River [123]. Hanyang equipment moved Wuhan–Yichang–Three Gorges–Dadukou [113]; one Dadukou steel site lay about 18 km upstream beside the river, hills, and the Chengdu–Chongqing Railway [151]. Railway-use degree remains unknown: 1936 and 1943 maps show networks, not factory use [153][184].

No comparable factory-to-city distance or historical reference-center measurement in kilometres is supported. A later study found 73.57% of Chongqing industrial enterprises within 10 km of major rivers and 64.83% within 10 km of central towns, but those figures are not evidence for 1937–1945 [119]. Government relocation was declared on 20 November 1937 and completed by the end of 1938 [40]; by 1945, Chongqing held 17 of 27 rear-area armament factories and 77% of employees [121]. Mountains, rivers, bombing in 1938–1944, caves, and dispersed suburban zones support wartime security context, but do not consistently prove NRC-specific concealment intent [150][183][42][87].

Central Steel Plant (中央钢铁厂) was a documented pre-war NRC project: its May 1936 preparatory committee selected Xiangtan’s Xia She Si (下摄司), a high, flat, flood-safe site on the Xiang River corridor, about 6 km from the Xiangqian Railway, with a branch and shared utilities planned [74][140][190]. Sources conflict on outcome: one says the plan was never implemented [105], while others record land leveling, a railway spur, and dormitories by 1938, followed by conversion to a military airport in July 1938 [192][190]. Pre-war integrated transport and safety planning is therefore clear; the interruption predates the requested wartime band, and wartime urban-distance or rail/water/security ratings remain unknown.

Central Electrical Equipment Plant (中央电工器材厂) was planned in the 1936 Xiangtan cluster but relocated to Huangjueya (黄桷垭) in 1938; highway access, tree concealment, night production, walls, and guards document wartime adaptation [190][155]. Central Radio Manufacturing Plant (中央无线电机制造厂) was established in Changsha in April 1937 and moved to Guilin in October 1938; its NRC link is not explicit, although 1943–1944 Chongqing records exist [105][172]. Central Electrical Porcelain Plant (中央电瓷制造厂) began production in Changsha in spring 1938, moved to Yuanling after Wuhan fell and later to Yibin; its 1942–1946 Chongqing record supplies no comparable distance or strategy evidence [105][129].

**Comparative cases (continued)**

**Overall result.** The evidence supports a shift from pre-war fixed sites chosen for integrated rail–river access toward wartime relocation, inland dispersion, and security-driven adaptation. Hunan’s pre-war industrial concentration in Changsha, Yueyang, and Hengyang contrasted with wartime movement toward Yuanling, Chenxi, and western Hunan; relocation routes used the Yangtze, Dongting Lake, Xiangjiang, and Yuanjiang. [199] NRC’s broader sequence was preparation and investigation, economic transfer in 1937–38, and rear-area construction after 1938. [138]

- **Central Electrical Equipment Plant (中央电工器材厂).** The pre-war Xiangtan site was selected beside the Xiangjiang, near intersecting railways, a port, and a planned railway siding; associated works included a 4,000-kW power plant, and construction began in March 1937. [73][188] After Wuhan fell and Hunan became exposed in October 1938, bombing stopped construction and machinery and personnel moved to Guilin, Kunming, and elsewhere; other accounts record branches at Chongqing and Lanzhou. [73][136] The Kunming replacement site was assessed for safety, water, transport, fuel, materials, public security, air defense, terrain, minerals, and proximity to the city; it was reported as about 9 km from Kunming’s urban area, although the reference and method are undefined. [142] Bombing later moved the Fourth Factory from dense Majie to Qinjiashan valley, where four temporary workshops were erected. [144] A separate account records wartime truck movement from Changsha and Guilin to Huangjueya, but this documents evacuation, not the original siting rationale. [135]

- **Central Electrical Porcelain Plant (中央电瓷制造厂).** It was officially established in Changsha’s Huangtuling in September 1936, began production in spring 1938, moved to Yuanling after Wuhan fell, and later maintained a Yibin branch during 1939–41. [225][224][178] Wartime output reportedly reached 2.07 million insulators supporting communications and power networks. [225] The sources do not provide a common-distance calculation or an explicit transport or concealment rationale.

**Comparison limits.** Chongqing logistics show that rail and water remained complementary: the Nantong coal railway began only in January 1939 and opened in June 1940 as a 17.3-km, 0.9-m narrow-gauge line, while wartime river transport carried tens of thousands of tons and sometimes involved 300 boats. [165] Chongqing’s Yangtze–Jialing position and wartime bombing and cave production are documented, but they are not automatically NRC site-selection evidence. [160][161] No defensible aggregate count or urban-distance distribution should therefore be calculated: the denominator must be confirmed, eligible, major NRC physical sites, with unresolved geography, proposals, and relocation-only cases separated.

The National Resources Commission (NRC) defines the institutional universe because it was a National Government institution responsible for industrial construction and, during the Anti-Japanese War, developed mining and industrial enterprises in the rear [131]. Its wartime mobilization of private factories means the roster should distinguish NRC-established or formally affiliated establishments from private plants mobilized for relocation [145][122].

Before wartime disruption, NRC planning followed major Yangtze tributaries and paired inland security with transport access [101][198]. After Japanese control of coastal areas, major river ports, and railway-line cities disrupted transport, the strategy shifted toward deeper inland concealment and dispersion; bombing moved documented Kunming units two kilometers west and twenty kilometers southwest, but no NRC-wide rail-versus-waterway share or standardized urban-distance difference is supplied [185][84][101].

Hunan cases show a shift from pre-war balancing of inland security with water, rail, and road access to wartime dispersal into mountainous terrain for concealment, while transport difficulty and route security remained constraints; a Hunan site faced 22 raids and 209 sorties [109][115][120]. The Zi River preserved water transport, and the Central Electrical Porcelain Plant’s Yuanling branch and Changsha factory show a multi-location network, but no quantitative rail-versus-waterway or urban-distance difference is supplied [152][128].

Chongqing contributes a wartime model of concentrating relocated industry in a secure inland transport hub: its industrial zone followed the Yangtze and Jialing, and the cited wartime waterway-reliance figure is 73.57% [147][40][119]. Other reported figures—64.83%, 93.50%, and 93.48%—have no specified context or denominator, and no comparable pre-war percentage or urban-distance measure is supplied, so the direction and size of Chongqing’s period change cannot be established [119].

Central Steel Plant represents a pre-war strategy of placing heavy industry inland but connected: Xiashesi’s soil supported heavy machinery, its hilly terrain protected against ground forces and air strikes, and the Hunan plan emphasized water-and-land transport [101][74]. Wartime made Xiangtan a war front, stopped construction, and converted the leveled land to military use, showing a shift from planned fixed infrastructure to threat-driven interruption rather than a completed alternative site; no quantified rail/waterway or urban-distance change is available [192][105].

Chaling Iron Mine supplies pre-war evidence of resource-led, defense-related heavy-industry planning: the 1935 plan targeted 30万吨 annual capacity [210][57]. No wartime Chaling site, transport, urban-distance, or concealment evidence is supplied, so this case cannot establish a direction or size of pre-war-to-wartime strategy change.

Xiangtan Coal Mine provides a pre-war baseline: it is dated to 1936 and documented as under NRC involvement from 1936 onward [214][215]. The supplied evidence gives no wartime site-selection factor, transport mode, urban distance, or security comparison, so it cannot show whether strategy changed or by how much.

Tianhe Coal Mine links a pre-war development plan to an NRC activity span: the 1935 plan set annual capacity at 150万吨, while NRC-related involvement is dated 1937–1940 [210][107]. No site-selection rationale, wartime relocation, urban-distance, or security comparator is supplied, so the case shows continuity of administrative attention rather than a measurable strategy shift.

Shuikoushan Lead-Zinc Mine is a pre-war, resource-anchored case: it dates to 1934 and is classified as nonferrous-metal mining and smelting, with abundant metal resources and river/boat access [204][223][203][206]. During the war, invaders damaged its shafts and forced production to stop [221], but the evidence does not establish that it was an NRC-established plant or provide a quantified rail, waterway, urban-distance, or concealment comparison [105].

Chongqing Temporary Copper Smelter supplies pre-war metallurgical evidence: the NRC’s 1935 plan assigned it 3600 tons per year, and it was listed among the smelting enterprises already established and operating before the war [210][219]. No wartime siting change or rail, waterway, urban-distance, or concealment comparison is supplied, so its contribution is a baseline rather than a measurable strategy difference.

Central Machine Manufacturing Plant anchors the pre-war side of the comparison: it is dated to 1936 and is described as part of the NRC’s large-scale pre-war industrial construction [214][209]. The evidence supplies no wartime relocation or site-selection factors, railway/waterway reliance, urban distance, or security measure, so no direction or size of a period change can be inferred.

Central Electrical Equipment Plant shows a logistics shift: the pre-war Xiangtan plan combined inland security with intersecting railways, a railway special line, river/wharf infrastructure, and convenient water transport [109][143][188]. During wartime relocation, the plant moved by GMC trucks and the Kunming site was approximately 9 kilometres from Kunming’s urban area, indicating a shift toward flexible road movement and rear-area security; no comparable pre-war urban distance or rail-versus-waterway share is supplied [135][142][198].

Central Radio Manufacturing Plant contributes a pre-war chronology: it is dated to September 1936, while the surrounding NRC strategy is described as deliberately placing major heavy industries in Southwest China before the war [96][199]. No plant-specific wartime relocation, transport, urban-distance, or concealment evidence is supplied, so the direction and size of a strategy change remain undetermined.

Central Electrical Porcelain Plant shows continuity from pre-war establishment into wartime operation: it was established in 1936 and produced 207万件 during the war [225]. No site-specific evidence compares its railway/waterway reliance, distance from an urban center, or concealment before and during the war, so it cannot quantify a strategy shift.

Central Copper Smelter, established in 1937, shows a security-oriented shift: pre-war factors included proximity to electricity and mountain terrain for air defense, whereas wartime conditions relocated it from Hunan to Kunming and renamed it Kunming Copper Smelter [226][99][133]. This indicates movement toward a deeper rear-area position, but the evidence gives no numerical rail/waterway or urban-distance difference.

Longxihe Hydropower Plant supplies a pre-war NRC baseline: it is dated to 1936 and documented as an NRC project from 1936 onward [138][215]. No wartime counterpart, railway/waterway reliance, urban distance, or security/concealment factor is supplied, so it cannot establish a direction or size of period change.

Sichuan Oil Mine shows a shift from a pre-war exploration phase to wartime administrative constraint: it is dated to September 1936, with Baxian and Daxian sections and equipment staged in Chongqing, but in August 1938 Daxian drilling was ordered to stop [193][182][186]. Its importance may instead reflect resource availability and wartime fuel demand, and no kilometre distance to an urban center or rail/waterway/concealment comparison is supplied; this limits any inference that its site choice was security-driven [159].
