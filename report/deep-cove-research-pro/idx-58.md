# Online Learning in Higher Education During COVID-19: Effectiveness and Influencing Factors, January 2020–August 2023

**Online learning was conditionally effective.** Across empirical higher-education studies published from January 2020 through August 2023, it maintained or improved academic achievement in several settings, but did not consistently outperform face-to-face teaching. A randomized comparison involving 725 statistics students found no overall delivery-mode advantage, while a blended nursing intervention produced higher assessment scores; other studies identified weaker achievement in particular cohorts or disciplines. [1][2] Effectiveness depended especially on infrastructure, instructional design, social interaction, emotional burden, and whether flexibility translated into productive study time.

**The review separates three questions.** First, an 18-study catalog identifies what was studied and which outcomes supported each effectiveness classification. Second, the synthesis distinguishes achievement from perceived learning and explains why surveys, comparative studies, longitudinal research, and randomized trials sometimes reached different conclusions. Third, the factor analysis traces how the five required influences helped or hindered learning, including circumstances in which apparently beneficial features produced trade-offs.

## Summary Table of Reviewed Studies

**The scope is higher education.** This review concerns empirical research on university students, instructors, courses, and institutions experiencing online learning during COVID-19. The publication window is **January 1, 2020–August 31, 2023**, without geographic restriction. Online exposure includes synchronous teaching, asynchronous learning, blended instruction, and emergency remote teaching—the rapid transfer of campus-based education to remote delivery during disruption. Earlier academic records appear only as comparison baselines within eligible pandemic-era studies.

**Classifications follow the measured outcome.** “Effective” identifies favorable evidence for the stated outcome or intervention; “ineffective” identifies an unfavorable result; and “neutral” covers null, mixed, or opposing findings. Consequently, an ineffective belonging or infrastructure result is not automatically evidence of lower examination achievement. The tables distinguish academic outcomes from perceptions and the conditions supporting learning.

## Effectiveness Analysis

### Overall judgment: achievement often held up, but broader effectiveness was uneven

**The catalog favors a conditional judgment.** Counting the classifications above gives five effective, ten neutral, and three ineffective results across 18 distinct studies. This is a descriptive count of outcome-specific classifications, not a pooled success rate: the positive category includes academic improvements and favorable perceived-quality relationships, while the negative category concerns infrastructure, belonging, and social or psychological conditions. The central question is therefore not which label wins numerically, but which educational functions online provision successfully performed.

**Meaningful academic gains were possible.** Ke and colleagues’ blended nursing group achieved a mean score of 82.18, compared with 77.95 in the traditional face-to-face group—a calculated difference of 4.23 score points, using 82.18 − 77.95. The corresponding standard deviations were 8.97 and 11.76, and the between-group comparison had p = 0.001. Improvements also appeared in most assessed domains of self-directed learning, problem-solving, and critical thinking. [2]

**Preservation also represented educational success.** Zheng, Bender, and Lyon found that the online cohort was equally or more likely to receive an A grade in 16 of 17 comparable dental courses: four favored online teaching, twelve showed similar grade distributions, and one favored face-to-face teaching. Thus, 94.1% of course comparisons were equal or favorable, calculated as 16 ÷ 17 × 100. That result supports the feasibility of maintaining didactic education during campus closure, rather than proving that every course benefited from moving online. [8]

**Neutral averages were also consequential.** Torda and Shulruf found similar objective knowledge outcomes between online and face-to-face medical scenario groups and across historical cohorts. Geng and colleagues found no significant aggregate score difference in the immediate online semester. Cheung and colleagues’ randomized statistics experiment likewise found no significant overall delivery-mode effect across five assessment measures. Together, these findings contradict a blanket assertion that university learning necessarily deteriorated when teaching moved online. [5][3][1]

**Selected negative findings remain important.** Zhang and colleagues’ 2023 study of Fundamentals of Nursing I at Yangzhou University reported significantly lower final written-examination grades among students receiving distance teaching during March–July 2020. Selected engagement outcomes—including vitality and focus—were also lower, although other self-efficacy measures did not differ. This demonstrates a substantive counterexample to the positive blended nursing result: nursing education itself did not determine the direction of effectiveness. [21]

**Academic success did not ensure community.** Zhou and colleagues’ Dutch medical-school study found smaller friendship, collaboration, and support networks alongside higher written-test performance. Torda and Shulruf similarly found better social outcomes for face-to-face groups without a corresponding knowledge advantage. Online learning could therefore succeed at transmitting and assessing knowledge while performing less well at supporting the wider university experience. [19][5]

**Broader reviews support this distinction.** A 2022 health-sciences review identified seven academic-performance studies: five reported increases, one a decrease, and one no effect. Separately, 24 of 36 satisfaction studies favored online learning and twelve opposed it. These are study counts within a discipline-focused review, not estimates that five-sevenths of all university students learned more or that two-thirds preferred online education. [22]

**Emergency continuity differs from superiority.** Maintaining learning during campus closure was an important achievement, but it answers a different question from whether the same online course would outperform a well-supported face-to-face alternative. The pandemic simultaneously changed teaching, assessment, living conditions, employment, and social contact. The evidence supports online learning as a viable educational route under suitable conditions, with a stronger case for targeted and blended uses than for treating fully remote provision as a universally better replacement.

**Additional documented evidence**

These are discovery leads, not causal estimates. Koh/Daniel (2022) screened 547 records to 36 peer-reviewed articles (six databases; Jan 2020–Dec 2021) [40]. Effectiveness leads include Kapasia (2020, West Bengal), Abou-Khalil, Ahmed–Opoku, Ali and Chan [10,86]. Eight strategies (skills/assessment/replication/access/engagement/participation/coping) maintained scores and attendance; outcomes were success/challenge/mixed, but access, self-direction, assessment/workload and experience problems remained; active learning helped, while poor recordings, fatigue and weak interaction hurt [40]. Electricity/broadband/devices/space, self-regulation and social presence mattered; Conklin–Dikkers and Ezra (2021) are social/equity leads [10,86,257].

Pedraja-Rejas’s Chile review (WoS/Scopus/SciELO) found 153 records; 14 duplicates left 139, then 54 full texts and 28 retained (2020–14 June 2023) [42]. Mixed performance, task-completion and satisfaction gains coexisted with effort/quality costs; Armijos reported N=625, 60.6% connectivity problems, 96% no tool problems, 40.2% learning a lot and 49.6% valuing flexibility [259,260]. Ozdamli–Karagozlu (2022) reviewed 94 articles: flexibility, confidence and continuity coexisted with infrastructure, practice, pedagogy, interaction, digital-literacy, emotional and self-regulation challenges [45]. An umbrella review of 576 articles reports poorer engagement and well-being but helpful peer mentoring in Wilcha’s 31 studies, and positive tele-education performance in Rahayuwati’s 22 studies [41].

**Evidence by design**

The same U.S. pharmacy report is linked to slots 01/12: a retrospective cross-sectional comparison of 118 on-campus students (Fall 2019) and 125 online students (Fall 2020). Overall examinations, final score and experience were similar; quiz means favored on-campus (88% vs 84.4%, p=.02), while exam 1/2/3, overall exam and final score were p=.02/.11/.95/.77/.89, respectively [73]. The finding is mixed/neutral, not causal evidence of online harm. The flipped design retained preparation, peer discussion and faculty interaction, but replaced self-selected peers with new random 3–4-person breakouts, possibly reducing comfort and engagement [73].

Separate, non-randomized cohorts left covariates unmeasured. Pandemic stress, illness, bereavement, children at home, unequal preparation, proctoring anxiety, delivery differences and multiple-choice-only assessment could affect scores and understanding [73]. Retrospective past-in-person comparisons therefore confound selection, pandemic period, comparator and measurement with modality, unlike RCTs; the authors call for same-cohort randomization or multi-course evidence [73]. This 2019–2020 contrast is not longitudinal.

A West Bengal, India, descriptive survey (January 2021) found 42% of undergraduate/postgraduate respondents found online learning boring; sudden unprepared transition, poor infrastructure/design, absent interaction, feedback and doubt clarification were linked to cognitive load, isolation, boredom and frustration [75]—perceptual, not achievement evidence. Athens’s 2023 mixed-method HyFlex study (876 students; Fall 2020–Spring 2021) found 70% flexed attendance; pass/withdrawal rates were 88%/2% in both terms versus 88%/3% and 89%/3% in face-to-face comparison classes [77]. Flexibility was valued by 48%; satisfaction was 3.83/5 and peer connection 2.72/5; isolation/disjointed modes affected 21%, technology 20%, and motivation/procrastination 17%; 82% (n=381) agreed tutorials/resources were provided [77]. These observational results support conditional equivalence under supported HyFlex, with access benefits but greater self-regulation demands—not causality.

Study slot 14’s Serbian art study used two-wave interviews (seven professors; nominal 25-student groups; May–June 2020 and October 2023), measuring channels rather than achievement. Initially untrained staff mostly used e-mail; later Google Meet/Discord were preferred, yet online teaching did not fully meet art needs [70]. A detailed passage reports 15 students in 2020, leaving a 25/15 denominator conflict; the supplied 2024 chapter is contextual, not an eligible row [70].

Screening qualifications: a 2024 Messina, Italy, comparison found online-pandemic pass .65 (n=94,736), pandemic in-person .58 (n=47,022), pre-pandemic in-person .60 (n=104,658), and overall pandemic .63 versus .60; it lacked pre-pandemic online data and raised cheating/assessor-bias concerns [74]. Pavia difference-in-differences evidence, received October 2023 and accepted June 2024, found no marked short-term grade effect and slightly positive estimates, but possible grade inflation and no dropout analysis [76]. Martin, Sun and Westine reviewed 619 articles in 12 journals from 2009–2018, not intervention effects, so cannot support a pandemic conclusion [78]. ### How the four research methodologies shaped the evidence

**Methodology changes what “effective” means.** A survey can show whether students felt supported or believed they learned; a comparative assessment study can show whether observed scores differed; a longitudinal study can reveal adaptation or deterioration; and a randomized trial can estimate the effect of an assigned intervention. These questions overlap, but their answers are not interchangeable.

| Methodology | Principal examples | Main reported pattern | What the result principally establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional perceptual surveys | Keržič; Yin; Hong Kong CoOL | Positive associations, mixed experiences | Perceived quality, engagement, satisfaction |
| Cross-sectional comparative studies | Rodríguez-Hernández and Rincón-Flores; Zheng | Positive, null, and opposing subgroup findings | Differences between observed groups or cohorts |
| Longitudinal studies | Geng; Elmer; Gillis and Krull | Delayed gains, evolving barriers, social deterioration | Change over time |
| Randomized controlled trials | Cheung; Hardt; reported Hamadan trial | Overall nulls and intervention-specific benefits | Effects of particular assigned contrasts |

**Early research emphasized student perceptions.** Bond and colleagues’ mapping review of 282 early-pandemic empirical studies classified 260, or 92.2%, as cross-sectional; fifteen, or 5.3%, were longitudinal; and seven, or 2.5%, used experimental or quasi-experimental designs. Online surveys were used in 234 studies, whereas student grades featured in a much smaller share. This methodological imbalance helps explain why the literature often spoke more confidently about experiences than about learning gains. [23]

**Design categories require contextual interpretation.** Some studies cross category boundaries. Chisadza combined a one-time survey with repeated assessment records; Torda combined contemporary groups, survey responses, and historical results; Ke evaluated a nonrandomized teaching intervention with pre/post measures. These combinations improve understanding, but calling every such study simply “comparative” would conceal important differences in what was observed and what was controlled.

### Cross-sectional perceptual surveys: informative experiences, weaker modality comparisons

**Favorable associations were common, not universal.** Keržič and colleagues found that service quality, the teacher’s active role, and system quality contributed to perceived e-learning quality. Satisfaction, in turn, strongly mediated the relationship between quality and perceived academic performance. The result identifies a coherent pattern: students experiencing better-supported courses also tended to report more satisfaction and stronger performance. [11]

**Structural models organize relationships, not experiments.** Structural equation modeling estimates relationships among several measured concepts simultaneously, such as quality, satisfaction, and perceived performance. In a cross-sectional questionnaire, however, the ordering of these concepts is partly theoretical. A satisfied student may evaluate both teaching quality and personal learning favorably, while a student who believes they performed well may become more satisfied; the model alone does not establish which process occurred first.

**Explained variance is not learning gain.** A Chinese university study of 457 students reported that its model explained 32.3% of variance in perceived learning outcomes and 51.6% in satisfaction. These figures describe how well the measured predictors accounted for differences in questionnaire responses. They do not mean that online instruction increased learning by 32.3% or satisfaction by 51.6%. [24]

**Mixed experiences challenge simple averages.** Purwadi and colleagues’ interviews with 22 Indonesian students identified ineffective, unpleasant, and limiting experiences alongside greater learning independence and enjoyment. Yin’s much larger survey identified four distinct motivation-and-engagement profiles rather than a uniform response. The qualitative and quantitative studies converge on heterogeneity: the same delivery transition could empower some students while leaving others disengaged. [10][14]

**Satisfaction and achievement are related selectively.** Taşkın and colleagues found moderate satisfaction among 2,663 Turkish students, with achievement and behavioral engagement differing across satisfaction levels. Their 494 free-text responses also revealed concerns about assessment, technical problems, instructor preparedness, and longing for face-to-face education. This combination is more informative than a single satisfaction average because it connects student evaluations to specific course conditions. [12]

**Preferences can conceal competing benefits.** At the University of West Attica, 60% of 336 engineering students preferred face-to-face teaching. Students favored it over remote teaching for understanding, concentration, engagement, and communication with tutors, yet 77% felt positively about avoiding the commute. A student could therefore value online convenience while judging its learning experience less favorably. [25]

**Online sampling can miss struggling learners.** A 2022 study at a predominantly Black American university linked survey responses with institutional records. Among 1,250 eligible students, 666 responded; their mean GPA change was −0.043, compared with −0.344 among nonrespondents. This concrete difference shows why survey participants should not automatically be treated as representative of all students experiencing emergency remote instruction. [26]

**The methodological judgment is specific.** Perceptual surveys are valuable evidence about usability, engagement, belonging, and perceived barriers. They provide substantially weaker evidence that online delivery caused superior—or inferior—academic learning, especially when the comparison is a remembered campus experience rather than a concurrently observed alternative.

### Cross-sectional comparative studies: real score differences, context-dependent explanations

**The Mexican comparison demonstrates opposing directions.** Rodríguez-Hernández and Rincón-Flores assessed students during February–July 2022 across six schools at Tecnológico de Monterrey. Their analyses adjusted post-test scores for pre-test performance and gender. Significant modality differences appeared in three schools, but the favored modality differed by school. [7]

The comparative study was published on 20 April 2023.

**These are school-specific test scores.** The assessments were 16-item multiple-choice tests scored from 0 to 100 and designed around each school’s learning objectives. The Architecture contrast was 14.23 points in favor of face-to-face teaching, calculated as 84.94 − 70.71; Medicine showed a 17.75-point remote advantage, calculated as 87.96 − 70.21. These contrasts demonstrate heterogeneity within one university, but they are not a common cross-discipline effect-size range.

**Business also illustrates the hybrid alternative.** Adjusted means were 90.62 for face-to-face, 87.71 for hybrid, and 75.38 for remote delivery. Remote students performed below both alternatives, while the overall study did not establish that a single modality was best across schools. The hybrid format here meant simultaneous attention to on-campus and remotely connected students, rather than simply an online component added before classroom teaching. [7]

**Positive historical comparisons need explanation.** Zheng and colleagues compared the same dental courses across 2019 and 2020, retaining seventeen courses with comparable content, goals, instructors, assessment methods, and grading policies. This offers a more focused comparison than an unrestricted student-preference survey. Nevertheless, the cohorts experienced different testing environments and pandemic circumstances, and greater time for didactic study could have contributed to the observed grade preservation. [8]

**Another medical comparison favored online scores.** At Arabian Gulf University, a 2023 retrospective study compared 202 online first-year medical students from 2020–2021 with 200 face-to-face students from 2018–2019. Online performance was higher in four of five basic-science courses, with reported Cohen’s d values between 0.267 and 0.494; Biostatistics showed no statistically significant difference. Cohen’s d expresses a mean difference relative to score variability, making it different from a raw percentage-point difference. [27]

**Recorded access provides one plausible explanation.** The Arabian Gulf University authors suggested that replaying lectures and redirecting commuting time toward revision could support memorization and recall. Those explanations are consistent with online learning’s practical advantages. They remain interpretations of an observed cohort difference, rather than separately estimated effects of replay or saved travel time.

**Negative nursing findings offer a counterexample.** Zhang and colleagues reported lower final grades and selected engagement measures under asynchronous distance teaching despite using the same teachers and course materials. Preserving materials and instructors therefore did not guarantee that the learning process remained equivalent. The authors proposed first-time online delivery, reduced communication, and pandemic-related psychological burden as possible explanations; these were hypotheses, not experimentally established mediators. [21]

**Learner level could reverse the association.** Diong, Lee, and Reed compared University of Sydney anatomy cohorts taught face-to-face in 2019 and online in 2020. They reported an 8.3% examination-mark difference favoring face-to-face undergraduates, with a 95% confidence interval of 3.3%–13.4%. For postgraduates, the direction reversed: face-to-face students achieved 8.1% fewer marks than online students, with a 95% confidence interval of 3.6%–12.6%. [28]

**Sophisticated adjustment still depends on assumptions.** The Sydney study explicitly represented its proposed causal structure using a directed acyclic graph—a diagram specifying which variables influence others. That transparency is valuable because it makes the interpretation conditional on stated assumptions, including how pandemic-related motivation and teacher engagement affected performance. Its opposing undergraduate and postgraduate findings support attention to learner needs, rather than a universal ranking of delivery modes.

**The comparative-study judgment is contextual.** Observed academic differences were substantial in some settings, but direction varied across courses, learner levels, and institutions. These studies establish that implementation and context matter; their strongest contribution is identifying where particular arrangements worked well or poorly, rather than isolating an invariant effect of “being online.”

### Longitudinal studies: adaptation and deterioration unfolded on different timescales

**Immediate and delayed results differed.** Geng and colleagues’ panel covered nine semesters from 2017 to 2021, drawing on 123,208 retained score observations across 434 courses and eight undergraduate specialties. The detailed semester comparison found no significant aggregate difference in the immediate online semester, followed by significant improvement in the seventh and eighth semesters. The resulting pattern is more nuanced than either “online teaching raised grades” or “the transition had no effect.” [3]

**Later improvement has several possible origins.** Students and teachers may have adapted, acquired digital skills, or developed more effective study routines. Later semesters also introduced other educational and contextual changes, including renewed face-to-face learning. The panel therefore shows that outcomes evolved after the emergency transition, without assigning every later gain exclusively to the original online exposure.

**Within-student comparisons improve one dimension.** Chisadza and colleagues compared the same students’ assessment performance before and during lockdown, with similar lecturer, content, and assessment format. This reduces the problem that students choosing online education may differ systematically from those choosing campus education. It does not remove changes occurring at the same time as lockdown, such as household disruption or altered study opportunities. [4]

**Social conditions deteriorated despite academic resilience elsewhere.** Elmer, Mepham, and Stadtfeld followed Swiss students using measures extending from 2018 to April 2020 and compared the crisis cohort with an earlier cohort. Interaction and co-studying networks became sparser, more students studied alone, and stress, anxiety, loneliness, and depressive symptoms worsened. Those findings identify a temporal deterioration in the environment surrounding learning, rather than contradicting studies showing stable examination performance. [18]

**Repeated experiences revealed emerging barriers.** Gillis and Krull found that technology and internet problems were common even among students who had not expected them. A single early survey of anticipated readiness would therefore have portrayed a different situation from subsequent surveys of actual participation. Longitudinal measurement matters partly because students discover practical constraints only after attempting to learn remotely. [13]

**Administrative trajectories also differed by program.** Bacci and colleagues used difference-in-differences analysis across five University of Florence degree programs. This approach compares the change in an exposed group with the change in a comparison group, rather than treating a post-transition difference alone as the effect. Estimated acquired credits increased by approximately 6% in Industrial Design, decreased by approximately 6% in Psychology, and changed little in the other programs. [29]

**Timing alone does not resolve causation.** Repeated records reveal whether outcomes persist, recover, or worsen, but interpreting the changes still depends on comparable assessments and an appropriate counterfactual. Acquired credits, for example, measure successful academic progression rather than every dimension of knowledge or competence. The longitudinal evidence supports separating initial disruption, subsequent adaptation, and broader social consequences instead of compressing them into one pandemic-wide verdict.

### Randomized controlled trials: specific interventions, not a universal direction

**Randomized findings were not uniformly positive.** The strongest direct modality experiment in this evidence set found no significant overall difference. The German mentoring experiment improved effort-related outcomes but not average academic achievement, while the Hamadan report favored an interactive online vaccination package. These studies tested different educational contrasts.

| Cheung et al. (2023) | Face-to-face versus synchronous online statistics | 725 | No overall significant assessment difference | Direct modality comparison |
| Hardt et al. (2020) | Online peer mentoring offer versus usual support | 691 | Motivation and registrations improved; GPA unchanged | Additional support within online education |
| Shirahmadi et al. (2023) | Interactive online vaccination package versus workshop | 120 reported | Knowledge, self-efficacy, performance favored online | Package benefit with nonconcurrent comparator |

**The statistics trial gives the clearest average comparison.** Cheung and colleagues compared 282 students assigned to face-to-face teaching with 443 assigned to synchronous online teaching across seven university statistics courses. Baseline pre-test means were very similar—66.62 and 66.52, respectively, with p = 0.955. After adjustment for course, grading option, gender, and pre-test score, delivery mode was not significant for final weighted scores, final-examination scores, coursework, tests, or assignments. [1]

**A null result is not exact equivalence.** Failure to reject a difference does not establish that two teaching modes have identical effects, or that every educationally meaningful disadvantage has been excluded. The appropriate conclusion is that this experiment did not demonstrate an overall difference on its measured assessments. Its relevance is strongest for the statistics courses and teaching arrangements actually tested.

**Small classes produced a different pattern.** When face-to-face lectures and tutorials involved no more than 25 students, face-to-face assignment was associated with a 5.99-point advantage in final weighted scores, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.64–11.34. The final-examination advantage was 6.70 points, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.26–13.14. Thus, the overall neutral result coexisted with a potentially important class-size boundary. [1]

**Mentoring tested support, not delivery mode.** Hardt, Nagler, and Rincke offered five one-to-one online peer-mentoring meetings during an entirely online German summer term. The intention-to-treat estimate—comparing students according to assignment, whether or not they participated—showed approximately 1.4 additional registered credits. Motivation and study behavior improved, but average passed credits and GPA did not show corresponding significant gains. [15]

**Take-up and prior achievement mattered.** Approximately 40% of students offered mentoring participated, helping explain why estimates for actual participation differed from estimates for the offer. Benefits were concentrated among higher-performing students: the upper prior-credit tercile gained approximately nine percentage points in the probability of reaching the designated 60-credit goal after two terms. The result shows that a helpful online support service can increase effort without producing an equal academic benefit for every learner. [15]

**Hamadan provides positive practical-training evidence.** Shirahmadi and colleagues reported knowledge-score increases of 43.1% in the online group versus 26.8% in the control group, and self-efficacy increases of 30.8% versus 9%. Reported post-intervention knowledge means were 19.17 versus 16.03 on a 21-point measure, while performance-checklist means were 27.92 versus 22.38. These findings concern both knowledge and observed performance, rather than satisfaction alone. [9]

**Its allocation description needs careful interpretation.** The report describes random allocation of classes, but also states that the in-person control had received training in an earlier semester or year. It additionally gives conflicting January and June 2020 trial dates. Accordingly, its positive result is most persuasive as evidence that the interactive online package supported vaccination learning, rather than as a clean, concurrent randomized estimate of delivery mode alone.

**Randomization does not standardize interventions.** Cheung tested synchronous teaching mode; Hardt tested an additional relationship-based support service; Hamadan combined video demonstration, practice, feedback, and home rehearsal. Divergent results are therefore not surprising. They indicate that online interventions can work, while giving no basis for assuming that all online instruction has the same effect.

### Why retrospective comparisons and randomized trials often disagreed

**The comparisons frequently asked different questions.** A student asked whether online learning was better than previous campus learning evaluates an entire remembered experience: teaching, friendship, classroom atmosphere, travel, routine, and study environment. A randomized assessment comparison asks whether assigning students to a defined instructional arrangement changes specified outcomes. Dissatisfaction with the first experience can coexist with stable scores in the second.

**Retrospective judgments combine mode and crisis.** During lockdown, students were not simply exchanging a classroom for a screen. Some lost quiet study space, employment, access to libraries, informal support, or confidence about the future. A negative comparison with the pre-pandemic university experience can therefore accurately describe hardship while attributing more of the difference to online teaching than a narrowly defined modality experiment would.

**Remembered baselines are uneven.** In the West Attica survey, 39% of respondents were first-year students without previous experience of in-person university lectures. Their comparison could not have the same experiential foundation as that of senior students. More generally, recollection emphasizes salient benefits and frustrations, whereas an examination-based trial directly observes current performance under its assigned conditions. [25]

**Concurrent randomization reduces competing explanations.** When students are assigned within the same period, differences in prior ability, motivation, or technology familiarity should be balanced on average, and both groups share the broader pandemic context. This makes the contrast more informative about the assigned teaching arrangement. It does not make the result universally applicable beyond the courses, learners, and outcomes studied.

**Historical grades can also move independently.** Wilhelm, Mattingly, and Gonzalez documented improved anatomy examination grades during emergency remote teaching despite students reporting lower learning, confidence, and engagement. During the emergency phase, examinations allowed two attempts and three minutes per question, compared with one attempt and less than one minute per question face-to-face. Several other assessment arrangements also changed. [30]

**Assessment conditions explain apparent contradictions.** In that anatomy course, grades remained unusually high during subsequent 2020 online semesters and moved back toward earlier levels after Proctorio was introduced in 2021. This does not establish misconduct by particular students or isolate one cause of the change. It demonstrates that higher grades may reflect changed opportunities to complete assessments, different examination formats, or altered supervision—not only stronger learning. [30]

**Experiences deserve their own interpretation.** Poor belonging, motivation, or concentration should not be dismissed merely because examination averages remain stable. Conversely, favorable convenience ratings should not be converted into evidence of superior knowledge acquisition. The methodological resolution is to retain both outcomes and recognize that each measures a different part of educational effectiveness.

**The overall effectiveness judgment is bounded.** Online learning demonstrated the capacity to sustain higher education and, in particular designs, improve assessed outcomes. The evidence supports neither universal superiority nor inevitable academic loss; it supports matching delivery arrangements to learning goals, learner needs, and the conditions necessary for participation.

## Analysis of Influencing Factors

**Five influences explain much of the variation.** Infrastructure determined whether students could participate; instructional design shaped what participation required; interaction affected academic and social support; emotions influenced attention and motivation; and flexibility changed when, where, and how students could study. These influences frequently operated together rather than as independent causes.

| Factor | Typical direction | Main mechanism | Specific examples | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure | Negative when inadequate | Interrupts access, attendance, communication | Chisadza; Owidi; Gillis and Krull | Secure usable access, not platforms alone |
| Instructional design | Positive when deliberate | Structures practice, feedback, self-regulation | Ke; Shirahmadi; Torda | Design learning activities for the medium |
| Lack of social interaction | Generally negative | Weakens belonging, clarification, peer support | Tang; Elmer; Zhou | Build purposeful and informal connection |
| Negative emotions | Usually negative; intensity-dependent | Diverts attention and reduces motivation | Gillis and Krull; Greek and Brazilian studies | Address workload, support, functional difficulties |
| Convenience/flexibility | Positive for access and satisfaction | Saves travel time; enables pacing and replay | West Attica; asynchronous engineering study | Pair choice with structure and feedback |

### Infrastructure: a participation bottleneck, not a sufficient explanation

**Reliable connectivity was associated with achievement.** Chisadza and colleagues’ University of Pretoria study provides a particularly useful example because it connected infrastructure measures with assessment records. Among 275 complete pre/post cases, good Wi-Fi access was associated with approximately 4.5 points higher grade change than mobile-data or zero-rated access. Students also reported poor internet access, high data costs, and missing laptops or tablets as transition barriers. [4]

**The mechanism begins before learning.** A student experiencing repeated disconnections may miss explanations, lose opportunities to ask questions, or fail to access the materials around which an activity is organized. Limited data allowances can make a technically accessible course practically unaffordable. A device suitable for reading short messages may also be poorly suited to following a lecture while consulting documents or completing an assessment.

**Access quality matters more than access labels.** The Pretoria comparison distinguishes reliable Wi-Fi from other forms of connection instead of treating all internet access as equivalent. That distinction is educationally important: being able to enter a learning-management system does not necessarily mean being able to stream demonstrations, sustain discussion, or upload work reliably. The observed association supports infrastructure as a plausible constraint on learning opportunity, while the study’s observational design keeps the 4.5-point contrast from becoming a universal causal estimate.

**Institutional readiness extended beyond connectivity.** Owidi and colleagues studied 682 staff and students at Masinde Muliro University of Science and Technology in Kenya. They found insufficient online pedagogical infrastructure, inadequate access among many students and lecturers, limited ICT skills, and absent clear online-learning policy guidelines. Their result concerns the institution’s capacity to deliver online education successfully, not simply whether a particular application had been installed. [16]

**Household conditions constrained synchronous participation.** Gillis and Krull identified unreliable internet, inadequate technology, and lack of dedicated workspace as barriers, particularly for live meetings. Work and childcare responsibilities compounded these difficulties. Their findings show why the same synchronous requirement can be manageable for one student and exclusionary for another, even within the same institution and course. [13]

**Technical friction also displaced teaching time.** In the West Attica survey’s 82 open-text responses, thirteen concerned technical issues. Students described internet and platform problems consuming time that could otherwise support deeper discussion. Here infrastructure affected effectiveness not only through absence from class, but through reduced quality of the time spent connected. [25]

**Prepared institutions offer a contrasting case.** The Spanish engineering school studied by Iglesias-Pradas and colleagues had pre-existing Moodle use, multimedia production, online-learning consulting, and virtual-laboratory support. Academic performance increased during emergency remote teaching in that setting. This institutional context helps explain why its favorable result should not simply be transferred to a university whose students and staff lack devices, connectivity, or assistance. [6]

**Infrastructure did not predict every outcome.** In the 666-student HBCU study, internet access was retained as potentially important in an information-criterion-based model, but was not statistically significant at the 0.05 threshold. The Hong Kong CoOL study likewise found no significant relationship between its university-support measure and perceived learning outcomes or initiative; its discussion noted that students generally possessed the necessary devices and technology skills. [26][31]

**The apparent disagreement fits a threshold mechanism.** Where access is severely constrained, infrastructure can prevent participation altogether. Where adequate access is already common, variation in teaching, motivation, or assessment may explain more of the remaining outcome differences. A nonsignificant infrastructure coefficient in one well-connected or selectively observed sample therefore does not establish that infrastructure was unimportant everywhere.

**The practical judgment is access-first.** Reliable connectivity, suitable devices, usable platforms, technical assistance, and lower-bandwidth alternatives are necessary supports for equitable online provision. They enable effective teaching rather than substitute for it: once access is secure, instructional quality and learner support become more decisive.

### Instructional design: guided activity mattered more than transferring content

**Deliberate designs produced the strongest examples.** Ke and colleagues’ task-oriented flipped classroom combined online preparation with individual and group clinical tasks, teacher explanations, and peer supervision. “Flipped” teaching moves initial exposure to material before the shared session so that subsequent teaching can concentrate on application and problem-solving. Its favorable performance result therefore concerns a coordinated learning design, not a screen replacing a classroom without other changes. [2]

**The mechanism was structured participation.** Preparing online gave students initial familiarity with the material; clinical tasks required them to use it; discussion and explanation helped resolve misunderstandings. Peer supervision added accountability within group work. These components plausibly support the improvements in self-directed learning and problem-solving reported by the study, rather than relying on students to transform passive exposure into active understanding unaided.

**Interactive vaccination teaching illustrates practical adaptation.** The Hamadan intervention used thirty-minute training videos within two-hour sessions, delivered twice weekly for two weeks. Sessions involved fifteen students and were repeated to accommodate the group. The instructor paused demonstrations, asked students to perform the skill, and used feedback to address problems; students were also advised to rehearse with syringes, vials, and dolls at home. [9]

**Practice changed the role of video.** A passive recording presents a procedure, whereas an interactive sequence requires learners to attempt its component actions and receive correction. Dividing the procedure into manageable steps can support confidence as well as execution. The Hamadan result is consequently evidence for a feedback-rich practice package, not evidence that watching a video alone produces the same benefit.

**Guidance helped beyond clinical subjects.** In Pretoria, students preferring self-study from PDF or PowerPoint slides with notes performed less well than those preferring assisted study through narrated slides or lecture recordings. One reported regression model gave a self-study coefficient of −3.649 grade points. The association suggests that explanatory guidance and organization mattered, although preferences were not randomly assigned. [4]

**Low-bandwidth materials are not inherently weak.** The infrastructure evidence supports providing text and downloadable materials, while the Pretoria result warns against expecting unguided slides to replace teaching. These findings are compatible. A well-organized low-bandwidth course can combine clear explanations, worked tasks, questions, and timely feedback; the educational problem is insufficient guidance, not the file format itself.

**Alignment failures impaired particular courses.** In Zheng and colleagues’ dental-school study, students reported unclear requirements and assessments poorly aligned with lecture material in Integrated Case-based Seminars II. This was also the sole comparable course in which the online cohort was less likely to receive an A. The coincidence is consistent with a course-design problem, although it does not isolate alignment as its cause. [8]

**Course fit imposed additional boundaries.** Dental Anatomy and Histology received lower ratings partly because the laboratory component and live demonstrations were difficult to translate online. A course can therefore be well organized but still lose educational functions when the medium cannot reproduce the necessary view, material, or physical action. Practical-learning objectives need separate consideration from whether theoretical knowledge can be explained and tested remotely.

**Self-assessment offered a mitigating mechanism.** A University of Murcia study compared 505 Work Organization students across pre-pandemic face-to-face and emergency-online academic years. Overall marks were higher before the transition, but online self-assessment participation and self-assessment-test marks positively predicted performance, with reported coefficients of β = 0.18 and β = 0.46. This illustrates how a beneficial design feature can partially offset a less successful overall transition. [32]

**Interaction with content was also relevant.** In the 457-student Chinese university study, learner-content interaction had the strongest reported positive path to perceived learning outcomes, β = 0.268. Self-discipline also predicted perceived learning and satisfaction. These associations support a design that helps students work actively with material and regulate their study, while remaining distinct from an experimental estimate of achievement gains. [24]

**The wider synthesis points in the same direction.** TASO’s March 2023 review emphasized planned student–student interaction and staff–student feedback as features associated with stronger attainment. It distinguished designing an online course from simply moving face-to-face materials online. Torda and Shulruf similarly concluded that activity design and teacher facilitation mattered more for students’ learning processes than delivery format alone. [33][5]

**The instructional-design judgment is positive but specific.** Clear goals, guided practice, usable resources, aligned assessments, and responsive feedback made online learning more effective. The conclusion would weaken where equally well-supported designs repeatedly failed on comparable objective assessments; it should not be extended from theoretical or simulated tasks to every hands-on or patient-facing competence.

### Lack of social interaction: weaker belonging and support, but not inevitable grade decline

**Belonging suffered in specific online settings.** Tang and colleagues’ Western Sydney University study found that peer collaboration and opportunities to form friendships were fundamental to first-year clinical-health students’ sense of belonging. Cameras-off classes and the loss of campus clubs, sport, and shared spaces made connection harder. Its qualitative component included four students and five academics—nine participants, calculated as 4 + 5—alongside the student survey reaching approximately 27% of the cohort. [17]

**Social interaction served two educational functions.** Academically, peers and instructors provided explanations, feedback, examples, and opportunities to test understanding. Socially, friendship and recognition helped students feel part of the university and supported them through uncertainty. Reduced interaction could therefore weaken learning indirectly through disengagement or emotional strain, even when the content remained accessible.

**Informal contact was particularly difficult to replace.** A scheduled online discussion can preserve a formal learning activity without reproducing conversation before class, shared study in a library, or spontaneous questions afterward. Tang’s participants valued staff efforts to create quizzes, icebreakers, reflective activities, small groups, and drop-in sessions, yet still described connectedness as stilted. Participation in an online activity was not automatically equivalent to establishing reciprocal relationships. [17]

**Dutch network data quantify the contraction.** Zhou and colleagues compared 102 before-pandemic network participants with 167 during-pandemic participants. More students reported having no connections in each of five out-of-class network types. Each percentage below uses its own cohort denominator; students could lack more than one network type. [19]

| Network type | Before pandemic, n = 102 | During pandemic, n = 167 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Study-related support | 2 students, 2% | 12 students, 7% |
| Collaboration | 6 students, 6% | 27 students, 16% |
| Friendship | 1 student, 1% | 24 students, 14% |
| Information sharing | 9 students, 9% | 37 students, 22% |
| Learn-from relationships | 22 students, 22% | 58 students, 35% |

**New students faced particular difficulties.** First-year students beginning university under distancing restrictions had fewer opportunities to establish the networks that continuing students could draw upon. International students also described difficulties building friendships while living away from established support. The mechanism is not simply less conversation: it is a reduced opportunity to create relationships that make later help-seeking and collaboration easier.

**Academic and social outcomes nevertheless diverged.** At the same Dutch medical school, first-year written-test means rose from 6.33 before the pandemic to 6.54 during it, while second-year means rose from 6.51 to 6.69 on the study’s 1–10 scale. Written tests were administered offline in both periods. This is a strong counterexample to assuming that smaller networks must immediately produce lower knowledge-test scores. [19]

**Compensating mechanisms were plausible, not isolated.** More time for examination preparation and access to repeatable lectures might have offset some academic disadvantages of weaker interaction. These explanations do not cancel the social losses. They show that examination knowledge, collaboration, belonging, and wellbeing can respond differently to the same educational environment.

**Communication problems appeared across countries.** In West Attica’s open-text responses, seventeen of 82 comments concerned socialization and fourteen concerned student–teacher communication. In the full questionnaire, face-to-face teaching was favored over remote teaching for tutor communication by 51% versus 17%. Students connected reduced direct contact with isolation and difficulty communicating, illustrating both the psychosocial and academic sides of interaction. [25]

**Structured online dialogue offered a counterexample.** The Hong Kong CoOL study surveyed 409 undergraduates from eleven universities. Student–student dialogue positively predicted perceived learning outcomes, with β = 0.438, while course design also had a positive relationship. Instructor–student dialogue predicted student initiative, β = 0.327, rather than showing the same pattern across every outcome. These coefficients describe model relationships, not percentage increases in learning. [31]

**Different interactions served different purposes.** The Chinese 457-student study similarly found positive associations between learner–learner interaction and perceived learning and satisfaction, but no significant direct association for learner–instructor interaction with those outcomes. This does not imply that instructors were unnecessary: teachers also shape course structure, explanations, feedback, and the conditions under which peer activity becomes useful. [24]

**Online communication sometimes reduced participation barriers.** Dental students in Zheng and colleagues’ study reported that typing questions in a chat box could feel less intimidating than speaking in class. Thus, the relevant alternative is not “social face-to-face learning” versus “asocial online learning.” Online tools can increase certain forms of questioning while reducing other forms of relationship-building. [8]

**The social-interaction judgment has two parts.** Reduced interaction generally harmed belonging, informal support, and some perceived learning processes. Deliberately facilitated dialogue could recover important functions, but stable grades alone should not be treated as evidence that the social purposes of higher education were preserved.

### Negative emotions: attention and motivation depended on burden and intensity

**Emotional strain often constrained engagement.** Gillis and Krull documented distractions, increased anxiety, and reduced motivation during the spring 2020 transition. Students were managing practical disruptions alongside coursework, and the resulting stress made academic work harder to prioritize. Their evidence points to a pathway from adverse circumstances to emotional burden, then to reduced capacity for sustained study. [13]

**Worry competed with academic attention.** A 2023 analysis of 9,418 higher-education students from 41 countries, using data collected May 5–June 15, 2020, found a negative direct association between worry about life and reported learning outcomes, β = −0.037. Course organization, computer skills, administrative support, and teacher support had positive associations, with academic emotions also involved in the modeled relationships. [34]

**The mechanism extends beyond feeling unhappy.** Persistent worry can occupy attention needed to follow explanations, while uncertainty about family health or finances changes what students consider urgent. Boredom can reduce willingness to persist, and high cognitive load—the amount of mental processing a task demands—can leave fewer resources for understanding new material. Emotional and instructional burdens therefore interact rather than operate in separate compartments.

**Greek evidence identified several pathways.** A study of 116 undergraduate and postgraduate students attending synchronous emergency remote education found that boredom and cognitive load affected acceptance of remote-learning components and indirectly related to perceived learnability and knowledge improvement. Its model explained 58% of variance in perceived knowledge improvement and 63% in perceived learnability. The outcomes were students’ evaluations of learning, rather than externally assessed examination gains. [35]

**Anxiety did not have one direction.** In that Greek study, anxiety was positively associated with perceived knowledge improvement. The authors suggested that anxiety might motivate effort to avoid failure and that the result could reflect moderate rather than severe anxiety. Because anxiety levels were not separately tested, the study does not establish a general beneficial dose of anxiety or show that raising anxiety would improve learning.

**Brazilian evidence connected symptoms with measured learning.** A University of São Paulo study examined 51 psychology undergraduates in a remote psychopharmacology course across 2020 and 2021. Across 1,636 pre/post question pairs from seven modules, correct answers increased after lectures, with reported d = 1.28. Learning therefore occurred in this remote course despite the emotional burdens students experienced. [36]

**Severe combined difficulties predicted poorer performance.** Within that sample, the combination of a GAD-7 anxiety score above fifteen and a Work and Social Adjustment Scale score of at least 21 significantly predicted fewer incorrect-to-correct answer changes. The latter measure captures interference with functioning, making the finding more informative than an anxiety score alone. The relationship suggests that learning difficulties were especially relevant when severe symptoms coexisted with disruption of everyday functioning. [36]

**Not every modality difference was emotional.** Rodríguez-Hernández and Rincón-Flores found no statistically significant differences in negative emotions across face-to-face, hybrid, and remote groups, despite differences in social relations and perceived learning processes. Emotional burden therefore cannot be assumed to explain every observed online disadvantage. It needs to be distinguished from course design, interaction, access, and the wider pandemic context. [7]

**Pandemic distress was not synonymous with online distress.** Elmer and colleagues associated worsening mental-health trajectories with COVID-specific worries, isolation, and lack of emotional support. Online learning was part of the surrounding situation, but restrictions on movement, health threats, and disrupted relationships also mattered. A delivery-mode explanation that absorbs all pandemic distress would overstate what these data establish. [18]

**The practical response should reduce burdens.** Predictable course organization, manageable workloads, responsive communication, and accessible social and psychological support address mechanisms identified across these studies. Support should be sensitive to functioning and circumstances, not merely whether a student reports some anxiety. The positive anxiety association in one perceptual study is not a reason to regard emotional strain as an instructional tool.

**The emotional judgment is intensity-dependent.** Worry, boredom, severe anxiety, and functional impairment generally undermined the conditions for learning. Some anxiety could coexist with greater effort, but the evidence supports reducing disabling burdens rather than assuming either that all anxiety is harmful or that anxiety improves achievement.

### Convenience and flexibility: clear access benefits, conditional learning benefits

**Convenience was a genuine positive outcome.** In West Attica, 77% of students expressed positive feelings about avoiding the commute, even though most preferred face-to-face teaching. Removing travel can save time, reduce fatigue, and make education easier to combine with other responsibilities. The same students’ stronger ratings for face-to-face understanding and concentration show why convenience and learning effectiveness require separate judgments. [25]

**Different formats offered different choices.** Online provision was not uniformly flexible in time. Synchronous teaching removed travel but retained scheduled attendance, while asynchronous resources allowed students to choose when to engage. Blended and simultaneous-hybrid arrangements combined these features differently.

| Format | Time and location choice | Main educational opportunity | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Synchronous online | Remote location; scheduled time | Immediate discussion and clarification | Live access and scheduling demands |
| Asynchronous online | Flexible location and timing | Replay, pacing, later access | Greater self-management demands |
| Blended or flipped | Online preparation plus shared sessions | Flexible preparation with guided practice | Coordination across components |
| Simultaneous hybrid | On-campus or remote participation | Location choice with shared teaching | Unequal participation can require attention |

**These distinctions appeared in actual courses.** Cheung’s experiment concerned synchronous online statistics teaching; Gillis and Krull used asynchronous activities and alternatives to fixed-time attendance; Ke combined online preparation with classroom tasks; and the Mexican study included simultaneous on-campus and remote teaching. Treating all four as an identical “online” intervention would conceal the mechanisms responsible for their different benefits. [1][13][2][7]

**Activity logs demonstrate that flexibility was used.** A 2023 engineering case study analyzed 188 final-year students in a fourteen-week asynchronous course during spring 2022. Its 36,067 activity records showed that 12.51% of content consumption occurred during the institution’s traditional “dead hours.” Seventy-nine percent of students accessed content between 8 p.m. and 10 p.m. at least once during the semester. [37]

**Usage is an access measure first.** Those logs show that students used learning opportunities outside conventional teaching times. They do not, by themselves, establish that evening access caused higher achievement. The plausible educational benefit is that students who cannot consistently study during scheduled hours gain additional opportunities to engage with material.

**Replay supported different learning paces.** Recorded teaching lets students pause, revisit difficult explanations, and organize note-taking around their needs. Arabian Gulf University’s authors proposed this as one explanation for stronger online examination performance. Mexican remote students likewise identified recordings, professor-provided material, and flexible location as learning facilitators. [27][7]

**Learner circumstances changed the value of choice.** Diong, Lee, and Reed suggested that older postgraduate students might have been better positioned to use independent study while balancing work, family, and childcare. Undergraduates might have depended more on in-person demonstration, informal accountability, and structured learning. These interpretations help explain the opposite anatomy results without establishing that age or postgraduate status independently caused the difference. [28]

**Flexible access did not ensure usable time.** A 2022 qualitative study at King’s College London drew on 417 initial survey participants, 235 follow-up participants, and fourteen interviews. Students consistently recognized greater flexibility, but experiences of autonomy, study space, caring responsibilities, and time management varied. Some could study more deeply; others struggled with crowded homes, blurred study–rest boundaries, or difficulty organizing unstructured time. [38]

**The mechanism depends on self-regulation.** Removing a scheduled session transfers some responsibility for timing and persistence to the learner. For a student with established routines, this can improve the fit between study and other obligations. For a student facing distractions or difficulty organizing work, the same freedom can reduce regular engagement unless the course supplies clear expectations and support.

**Flexibility and interaction can conflict.** A mandatory live meeting may improve immediate communication but exclude students with unstable connections or competing responsibilities. An entirely asynchronous arrangement may improve access while reducing spontaneous clarification or peer contact. Gillis and Krull’s use of alternative lesson plans and forty-eight-hour activity windows illustrates an attempt to preserve participation without requiring every student to engage at one fixed moment. [13]

**The strongest implication is purposeful combination.** Recordings, downloadable resources, and reasonable timing choices can be paired with milestones, feedback, and opportunities for discussion. This does not establish that blended education is always best; it explains why combinations can preserve online convenience while addressing the support and interaction needs identified elsewhere in the evidence.

**The flexibility judgment is therefore differentiated.** Convenience clearly improved access and satisfaction for many students. It becomes an achievement mechanism only when saved time, replay, and scheduling choice result in effective engagement rather than isolation, distraction, or postponed study.

### How the factors worked together

**The influences formed connected pathways.** Poor connectivity could reduce interaction, while reduced interaction could delay clarification and intensify frustration. Weakly organized courses could increase cognitive demands and make flexible study harder to manage. Conversely, accessible materials, clear tasks, and responsive feedback could make both participation and independent learning more productive. These connections explain why studying one factor in isolation often produced an incomplete account.

**Apparently conflicting recommendations can be reconciled.** Providing PDFs is sensible where bandwidth is constrained, but unguided slides are not a complete instructional design. Live discussion can be valuable, but fixed-time video attendance is not equally accessible to every student. Recordings can support revision, but they do not automatically replace the friendship, feedback, or accountability that students previously obtained on campus.

**The relevant unit is the educational arrangement.** The positive nursing intervention combined modality with tasks, practice, and peer supervision. The German experiment added mentoring to an online semester. The Spanish engineering setting combined emergency delivery with established institutional support. Their results should therefore inform decisions about complete teaching arrangements rather than be reduced to evidence that one communication channel is intrinsically superior.

**Learning goals determine the final judgment.** Stable written-test performance is encouraging where the immediate objective is conceptual knowledge. It is a narrower achievement where a course also aims to develop collaboration, clinical interaction, laboratory technique, or a durable sense of academic community. The Dutch medical findings make this distinction particularly clear: stronger knowledge-test results did not erase weaker social networks.

**A general claim requires broader consistency.** The conclusion would become more favorable to online delivery if comparable, well-controlled studies repeatedly showed objective gains across disciplines while preserving participation and broader educational outcomes. Repeated objective declines under similarly supported and assessed conditions would strengthen the contrary conclusion. Within the reviewed period, variation by design, learner group, and outcome is itself the most important substantive finding.

**The evidence has defined boundaries.** This is an outcome-separated narrative synthesis, not a pooled effect estimate. Samples retain their reported units—students, responses, records, courses, or focus-group participants—and derived counts or differences are identified as calculations. Effectiveness labels summarize named outcomes; neutral findings do not prove statistical equivalence. Self-report measures, selective participation, non-equivalent cohorts, changing assessments, bundled interventions, single-institution settings, and relatively sparse concurrent randomized evidence constrain general causal claims.

**The practical conclusion is conditional effectiveness.** Online learning sustained university education and sometimes improved achievement, particularly when access, design, feedback, interaction, and learner support worked together. Its clearest lesson is not that campuses or screens were universally better, but that effective higher education required preserving the functions of learning—not merely changing where teaching appeared.

Instructional design is positively linked to effectiveness when it aligns objectives with assessment, organizes content into logical modules, and provides interaction, formative assessment, clear expectations, and frequent specific feedback; these steps support concentration, engagement, self-monitoring, participation, persistence, learning outcomes, retention, and satisfaction [263]. During pandemic interventions, learning input and learning support mediated improved college foreign-language learning performance, whereas emergency remote teaching required unplanned choices of communication tools and assessment practices; effective provision therefore depends on preparation and implementation support [102][192].

Lack of social interaction is generally negative: reduced peer and university contact led to isolation, lower engagement, accountability, independent work, and willingness to ask questions, hindering group and thesis projects, whereas stronger social presence with peers and lecturers could reduce isolation [294]; a study of 464 students in spring 2021 also linked lack of academic social interaction to greater life disruption and poorer adjustment [296]. In the 2020 and 2021 path analysis, social interaction predicted emotional engagement, which predicted behavioral engagement and then self-directed study time; the pathway explained 6% in 2020 and 8% in 2021, supporting meaningful interaction and social presence, although the self-report and correlational evidence is not a causal estimate [290][291].

rejected: number_unsupported. Negative emotions can constrain effectiveness conditionally: in a model of 116 university students during the crisis, anxiety, boredom, and cognitive load could consume attention and working-memory resources, undermine motivation and self-regulation, and affect participation, technology acceptance, learnability, and perceived knowledge improvement [286][131][303]; achievement emotions can affect achievement through attention, motivation, and self-regulation, while coping can improve attention and problem solving [298][303].

Convenience/flexibility can enable participation for learners balancing work and family, but its benefit is conditional because flexibility may coexist with self-regulation demands [179]. The implication is to pair flexible access with self-regulation support rather than treat convenience alone as evidence of effectiveness [179].

Infrastructure is conditionally supportive when reliable internet, equipment, and platforms enable access, but negative when poor or costly connectivity and unequal technology access block participation [179][48]; instructional design is generally positive but mixed or conditional because organization, interaction, feedback, and assessment shape engagement and learning [222][96]; lack of social interaction is negative through isolation, reduced belonging, collaboration, and feedback, while interaction supports engagement [222][179]; negative emotions constrain motivation and engagement [222]; and convenience/flexibility supports work and family participation but depends on self-regulation [179]. The factors are interdependent: technology, pedagogy, and socioeconomic conditions are described as symbiotic, so effectiveness depends on their alignment; the practical implication is to combine infrastructure investment, deliberate design and feedback, social presence, emotional support, and flexibility with regulation support rather than rely on any single factor [179][222].

During the reviewed pandemic period, infrastructure affects effectiveness through access and participation: reliable internet, devices, and online platforms enable remote learning, whereas weak or costly connectivity, unequal access, and limited technology familiarity interrupt attendance, submissions, assessment, and interaction [48][188][197]. An Indonesian higher-education example reported weak signals, lack of Wi-Fi, and high data costs with low attendance, late or missing assignments, exam, file-upload, and Zoom problems; Wi-Fi access could not be causally isolated because it was not randomly assigned, and no common quantitative infrastructure difference is reported [48][258].

Instructional design affects effectiveness through communication, engagement, and learning access: reported examples include chats and virtual feedback in large introductory classes and blended learning with short videos, while the combined pathway links infrastructure, design, communication, engagement, social interaction, and flexibility [223][330]. The direction is mixed and conditional, with no common effect size supplied; weak collaboration, communication, and social presence increase frustration and disengagement, so design benefits depend on implementation and interaction quality rather than modality alone [110][270].
