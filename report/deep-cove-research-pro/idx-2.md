# Why Social Protection Misses the Poorest in South Asia: Evidence from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nepal through September 2023

**The central finding:** These programs are not uniformly ineffective, but their protection repeatedly breaks down between formal entitlement and actual receipt. Restrictive eligibility rules, insufficient work or funding, inaccurate beneficiary lists, political influence, payment delays and access costs leave many poor households unprotected; small benefits can then limit the gains even for recipients. The ultra-poor are particularly disadvantaged because participation often requires precisely what they lack: documents, connections, mobility, spare cash, available labor and the ability to wait for payment. These patterns recur across the country assessments, alongside evidence of useful and sometimes strongly progressive programs. [1][2][3]

| Country | Decisive evidence | People especially affected | Main judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| India | 63% of sampled MGNREGA wage transactions exceeded seven-day central processing in FY2021–22. [4] | Poor jobseekers, women with care responsibilities, migrants | Legal rights are weakened by rationing and delivery barriers. |
| Pakistan | BISP recorded 12.2 million eligible families versus 8.2 million active beneficiaries in 2022. [5] | Eligible but inactive families; disconnected and informal-worker households | Registry improvements coexist with activation gaps and segmented protection. |
| Bangladesh | One CFPR/TUP poultry-selection case rejected 20 of 50 selected women. [6] | Remote ultra-poor women, unconnected applicants, poor elderly people | Poverty targeting can select households whose circumstances fit delivery requirements rather than greatest need. |
| Nepal | FY2020–21 coverage was 80.2% among older people but 9.9% among working-age people. [3] | Informal workers, children needing income support, undocumented people | Protection is concentrated in particular ages and employment categories. |

**The report’s route:** Part One examines named programs country by country, identifying their objectives, practical failures, affected households and exclusion mechanisms. Part Two compares those findings under the five requested categories: Program Design Flaws, Elite Capture, Targeting Errors, Invisible Barriers, and Self-Exclusion by the Poor. The report ends with the requested four-column program catalog.

## Scope and interpretation

**The evidence cutoff:** This report concerns information available through **30 September 2023**. Earlier evaluations remain important for explaining how exclusion developed, but their results retain their original dates. Historical programs are identified separately from later schemes.

**The population of concern:** “Ultra-poor” refers here to households experiencing the deepest deprivation, often combining insecure livelihoods, few assets, food insecurity and limited capacity to absorb shocks. It is not treated as one identical statistical category across the four countries. A household may also be poor without fitting a program’s administrative definition of poverty.

**Effectiveness has several dimensions:** A program can reach relatively poor recipients yet cover only a small fraction of poor people. It can transfer the correct amount but too late, or enroll households successfully while providing too little to meet its objective. The investigation therefore distinguishes reach, selection, delivery, adequacy and outcomes rather than treating enrollment alone as success.

## Part One: India — substantial entitlements, uneven access

### Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme

**The guarantee is underdelivered:** The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme, commonly called MGNREGS or MGNREGA after its governing Act, guarantees up to 100 days of wage employment annually to rural households whose adult members seek unskilled manual work. A 2022 review reported an average of only 45 days per participating household over the preceding decade. Drawing on survey comparisons spanning the 2004–05 baseline and 2011–12 follow-up, it also reported that nearly 70% of poor households keen to work obtained no employment. [7]

**Work supply restricts access:** During the pandemic, an Azim Premji University study cited by LibTech found that 39% of households needing work in its study blocks obtained no workdays. The cited study estimated that meeting demand would have required at least four times the allocated budget. This illustrates a fundamental contradiction: a demand-based guarantee can operate as a rationed program when budgets, project availability and administrative capacity determine how much work is offered. [4]

**Payment timing weakens protection:** LibTech analyzed 31.36 million wage transactions from 327 blocks in ten states in FY2021–22. It found that 63% exceeded the Union government’s seven-day processing requirement and 42% exceeded 15 days. These are central processing intervals after payment orders, an important component of—but not the same as—the entire period between work and money reaching a worker. [4]

**Low wages compound delays:** In 2020–21, notified MGNREGA wages were below the relevant minimum wages in 17 of 21 states examined. Even completing 100 days in Kerala, then the highest-paying state in the comparison, yielded only INR 27,100 over a year. Workers needing money immediately may prefer casual employment with daily payment, making delayed public-work wages particularly unsuitable for households with no savings. [7]

**Digital compliance creates exclusion risks:** When the Aadhaar-Based Payment System, or ABPS, mandate was issued on 30 January 2023, only 43% of workers were eligible for that payment route. On 28 August, 41% remained ineligible. LibTech’s fieldwork and corroborating reports described deletion of genuine workers’ job cards during the compliance drive, linking an administrative performance target to loss of access. [4]

**Women face additional filters:** The household—not each adult—holds the 100-day entitlement, leaving allocation between women and men open to household bargaining. UNICEF’s gender analysis describes localities treating the one-third provision for women as a maximum, preferences for able-bodied workers, turnaways of pregnant or lactating women, and inadequate childcare and worksite facilities. Women can therefore be formally eligible but unable to obtain or sustain participation. [8]

**Progressive participation still matters:** Evaluations summarized by the World Bank found that MGNREGA attracted Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe workers, illiterate households, households without electricity and people living in poor-quality housing. This is meaningful counterevidence to a blanket failure claim. It shows that a program can select disadvantaged participants relatively well while still excluding many other disadvantaged households through limited work supply. [1]

### Public Distribution System and Targeted Public Distribution System

**Food access depends on classification:** India’s Public Distribution System, or PDS, distributes subsidized food through ration shops. The 1997 Targeted PDS divided households into Below Poverty Line, or BPL, and Above Poverty Line categories; the National Food Security Act of 2013 subsequently established priority-household and Antyodaya Anna Yojana entitlements. Antyodaya specifically addresses the poorest households, including landless laborers, destitute people and households without assured subsistence. [9]

**Identification excluded genuinely poor households:** A 2009 expert-group estimate reported in Sakshi Balani’s 2013 review found that about 61% of the eligible population was excluded from BPL lists, while 25% of non-poor households were included. The second percentage concerns non-poor households entering the list, not the proportion of all beneficiaries who were non-poor. [9]

**Poverty proxies misread actual need:** Asset-based classification could treat households owning a fan, television, vehicle or land as above the poverty line even when they remained food insecure. Other poor families lacked ration cards altogether. Central allocations also relied for years on old poverty estimates, while incomplete surveys and card issuance separated present deprivation from administrative recognition. [10][9]

**Leakage reduced usable supplies:** A review reported national grain diversion of 44% in 2007–08, while the World Bank’s 2010 assessment cited an estimated 58%. Ghost cards—issued to nonexistent people—provided one concrete route through which grain could move into the open market instead of reaching deserving households. These mechanisms affect both people omitted from lists and genuine cardholders whose allocated supplies are diverted. [10][1][9]

**Different measures reveal different failures:** A Tamil Nadu study summarized in the systematic review estimated that only 1 kilogram of rice reached people classified as needing it for every 5.43 kilograms distributed. That measures distribution toward a defined needy population; it should not automatically be read as a theft rate, particularly in a state operating a broader, universal PDS. Universal eligibility reduces poverty-classification barriers, but it does not by itself guarantee complete delivery. [10][9]

**Migrants face practical non-portability:** A 2022 field study of 25 migrant workers found problems despite One Nation One Ration Card, the portability initiative. Reported barriers included refusal of out-of-state cards in Gujarat, server failures, fingerprint mismatches and incomplete grain delivery. Household cards were also left in villages for relatives, and collecting rations could mean sacrificing paid work. [11]

**Technology has opposing effects:** Qualitative research in Kerala and Karnataka found that biometric verification reduced dealers’ opportunities to fabricate transactions, while exclusion problems persisted. The practical comparison is therefore not simply digital versus manual delivery: controls that reduce diversion must also provide a workable route for legitimate recipients whose authentication fails. [12]

### National Social Assistance Programme

**Categorical assistance leaves boundaries:** The National Social Assistance Programme, or NSAP, provides pensions, bereavement assistance and food support to eligible BPL households. Its 2023 guidelines describe 30.9 million beneficiaries but impose scheme-wise ceilings for each state and union territory. They acknowledge that deserving people may exceed those ceilings, leaving additional assistance dependent on state resources. [13]

| NSAP component | Main objective | Central benefit in 2023 guidelines | Important eligibility boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme | Income support in old age | INR 200/month at 60–79; INR 500 at 80+ | BPL status and age |
| Indira Gandhi National Widow Pension Scheme | Support poor widows | INR 300/month before 80; INR 500 at 80+ | Widowhood and minimum age 40 |
| Indira Gandhi National Disability Pension Scheme | Support eligible disabled adults | INR 300/month before 80; INR 500 at 80+ | BPL status, age and disability criteria |
| National Family Benefit Scheme | Assist bereaved households | INR 20,000 once | Breadwinner’s death after 18 and before 60 |
| Annapurna | Food support for uncovered elderly people | 10 kg grain/person/month | Eligibility plus noncoverage by old-age pension |

**Exclusion follows several rules:** Poor people outside BPL lists and eligible people beyond state ceilings can miss assistance before any payment is attempted. A parliamentary committee also criticized the disability pension’s threshold of more than 80%, compared with more than 40% under the disability legislation it examined. Younger widows, single women and divorced unemployed women without alimony were among the groups for whom it recommended wider coverage. [14]

**Small transfers limit protection:** The amounts above are central contributions; states may supplement them. Nevertheless, a low central floor makes protection dependent on where a poor person lives and the state’s capacity and willingness to add support. The committee’s recommendations to raise and regularly inflation-adjust pensions addressed adequacy as well as enrollment. [14]

**Saturation can conceal unmet need:** Calling a capped pension scheme “100% saturated” means it has filled its sanctioned allocation, not necessarily that every needy person receives a pension. The distinction matters particularly for ultra-poor households incorrectly classified above BPL thresholds or living in states unable to finance additional beneficiaries. [13]

### Rural housing, employment and livelihood schemes

**Historical schemes show recurring mechanisms:** Earlier programs demonstrate that the gap between allocation and protection predates digital delivery. The affected groups below include shortpaid recipients and poorly reached intended populations, as well as households exposed to competition from better-off beneficiaries.

| Program and evidence period | Purpose/type | Documented failure | Poor people affected | Concrete mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Indira Awaas Yojana/targeted rural housing; 2010 assessment | Housing grants | Receipts below sanctioned transfers | Targeted recipients receiving reduced grants | Capture and leakage |
| Sampoorna Grameen Rozgar Yojana; 2010 assessment | Rural employment/assets | Considerable richest-quintile inclusion | Intended rural poor competing for resources | Better-off participation |
| Swarnajayanti Gram Swarozgar Yojana; 2010 assessment | Self-employment | Low coverage; larger benefits to rich households | Poor nonrecipients and smaller-benefit recipients | Unequal benefit allocation |
| Employment Assurance Scheme; 1990s | Rural employment | About 5% annual target-group reach; 17 days versus 100 targeted | Intended workers left without sufficient employment | Fund-release problems, contractors, fictitious workers |
| Jawahar Rojgar Yojana; 1992 evidence | Rural employment/assets | Over half of workers above poverty line; 22% of funds reached poor households | Intended poor households inadequately reached | Work and funds poorly directed by poverty status |

**Housing and livelihoods differ:** The World Bank judged rural-housing targeting mildly progressive, despite grant shortfalls. It likewise found SGRY progressive overall, with particularly good reach among Scheduled Tribe households and the lowest quintile. SGSY illustrates a different problem: relatively progressive participation can be outweighed by much larger benefits going to richer households. [1]

**Employment reporting overstated real protection:** EAS evaluations found that only 15% of expenditure reached workers against a 60% labor-cost norm. Contractors undermined self-targeting, while top-down pressure to spend allocations encouraged irregularities and fictitious workers. JRY’s average participant worked only 5.15 days per month, adding shallow employment to poor targeting. [15][16]

**State capacity shaped unequal reach:** A 2002 assessment of centrally sponsored schemes described typical state matching requirements of 25%. Poorer states could struggle to provide matching funds or absorb allocations, while more developed states received more transfers per person. Thus, administrative and fiscal capacity could distribute resources away from places with especially high need. [17]

### Lakshmi Bhandar in West Bengal

**Outreach did not ensure receipt:** Lakshmi Bhandar, launched in 2021, provided monthly cash transfers of INR 500 to general-category women and INR 1,000 to Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe women. A survey reported in May 2023 found that 40% of eventual recipients had applied more than once. Across the broader Duare Sarkar camp channel, a survey of 2,500 respondents in 16 districts found only 42% had received what they sought. [18]

**Linked requirements multiplied burdens:** Riya Soren, a rural agricultural worker, repeatedly applied but encountered problems involving the linked Swasthya Saathi health card and referrals between offices. She stopped pursuing the matter because further visits meant losing daily wages. Other applicants reported paid forms, missing acknowledgments and missed camp schedules; only one in five Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe respondents found the camps beneficial. [18]

**The Indian judgment:** India’s programs show why neither a statutory right nor a nearby application camp is sufficient. Protection improves when work, enrollment corrections and payments actually follow a claim; digitization or outreach alone changes only part of that process. The central failure is the distance between entitlement and usable, timely receipt.

## Part One: Pakistan — religion, connections, registries and employment status

### Zakat Programme and Guzara Allowance

**Subsistence assistance is narrowly mediated:** Pakistan’s Zakat system, established in 1980, includes the Guzara or subsistence allowance for eligible poor Muslims. The studied allowance is intended as monthly assistance and is selected locally. Non-Muslims are outside this channel by design, unlike Pakistan Bait-ul-Mal’s general-revenue-financed assistance. [19]

**Scarcity makes connections valuable:** A 2023 study of Guzara Allowance and Bait-ul-Mal’s Individual Financial Assistance used 44 interviews across four Punjab districts. Twenty-six of 32 successful and unsuccessful claimants described a *sifarishie*—an informal personal reference—as instrumental in securing assistance. The study described competition for approximately ten benefits among 100 eligible applicants, giving local officials substantial power to choose between needy claimants. [19]

**Disconnected poor applicants lose out:** A household may satisfy the poverty conditions yet lack someone able to approach or persuade the local decision-maker. Sifarish does not necessarily select a wealthy beneficiary: it can privilege one genuinely poor household over another, leaving the least connected among the poor excluded. This is especially important for ultra-poor people whose social networks contain few influential intermediaries. [19]

**Irregular finance weakens delivery:** An official program inventory rated targeting, transparency, ease of access, adequacy, coverage and monitoring as low. Separately, the Auditor General’s FY2019–20 findings included PKR 16.634 billion in unreconciled collections and PKR 645.188 million deposited late. The Islamabad committee also failed to release PKR 114.674 million for health, education and religious-school channels, interrupting the conversion of collected funds into assistance. [20][21]

**Historical findings are mixed:** Shirazi’s 1996 Zakat and Ushr study reported 94.3% of funds reaching the lowest income decile, but only 18.1% of households in that decile receiving support. Arif’s 2006 study instead found 35% of funds going outside the bottom two deciles and 42% of beneficiaries recommended by community elites. Sajid and colleagues note differences in datasets and periods, including difficulty separating official and private Zakat in the earlier evidence. [22]

**The resulting assessment:** Zakat can reach very poor recipients, but concentration among selected poor people does not establish broad protection. Religious eligibility, limited slots, personal mediation and irregular disbursement create distinct routes to exclusion.

### Benazir Income Support Programme

**A national registry improved selection:** The Benazir Income Support Programme, or BISP, provides regular cash assistance to poor households, primarily through women. Its National Socio-Economic Registry, or NSER, replaced an early application process mediated through parliamentarians with poverty-scorecard identification. That reduced reliance on access to a political representative, but did not remove enrollment and payment gaps. [5]

| Administrative snapshot | Families identified as eligible | Receiving payments/active | Calculated administrative gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| July 2013 evaluation | Almost 7.5 million | 4.6 million receiving payments | Approximately 2.9 million; 38.7% |
| 2022 administrative position | 12.2 million | 8.2 million active | 4.0 million; 32.8% |

**The gap measures activation:** The approximate 2013 percentage is calculated as `(7.5 − 4.6) ÷ 7.5 × 100`; the 2022 percentage is `(12.2 − 8.2) ÷ 12.2 × 100`. These comparisons show the distance between identified eligibility and payment or active status, rather than the proportion of every poor family in Pakistan reached. [23][5]

**Enumeration can miss vulnerable households:** The process evaluation shadowed 3,290 household interviews and found maps insufficiently detailed for a reliable census. Other registration evidence described enumerators refusing to travel to remote or insecure settlements and difficulty locating isolated houses. People missed at enumeration never reach the poverty-scoring stage, making this different from an inaccurate score assigned to a household already registered. [23][24]

**Identity checks can delay assistance:** Computerized National Identity Card requirements and certification through the National Database and Registration Authority, or NADRA, created additional processing stages. The evaluation identified certification delays affecting payments, alongside weak explanations of the program during some household interviews. The excluded or underserved groups therefore include eligible women with unresolved records, households omitted from maps and families outside accessible enumeration routes. [23]

**Payment controls also failed:** The Auditor General’s FY2019–20 findings flagged PKR 23.135 billion in irregular cash transfers involving government employees, pensioners or spouses; PKR 2.462 billion in unauthorized withdrawals outside districts or provinces; and PKR 35.706 million paid to non-verifiable beneficiaries. These findings concern payment integrity alongside undercoverage, rather than showing that one problem replaced the other. [21]

**Livelihood expansion lagged behind allocation:** BISP’s graduation initiative received PKR 904.93 million in FY2019–20 but used PKR 42.5 million. That is an estimated utilization rate of **4.7%**, calculated as `42.5 ÷ 904.93 × 100`. Low execution matters because a planned pathway beyond subsistence transfers offers little additional protection when most allocated resources do not become activities or benefits. [21]

**The BISP judgment:** Scorecards and large registries are a substantial alternative to discretionary selection, and local comparative evidence below favors BISP’s targeting. Their success nevertheless depends on finding households, keeping records current, activating eligible families and completing payments.

### Pakistan Bait-ul-Mal

**Faith-neutral assistance still faces gatekeepers:** Pakistan Bait-ul-Mal, or PBM, provides food support and individual financial assistance financed from general revenues. Its Individual Financial Assistance component studied in Punjab offered a one-off annual payment, contrasting with Zakat’s intended monthly allowance. The same interviews documented personal references and street-level discretion influencing which eligible applicants obtained limited assistance. [19]

**Poor nonrecipients face rationed selection:** The relevant excluded population is needy applicants who lose out when local judgment, influential recommendations and limited resources determine awards. General availability across religious groups removes Zakat’s faith boundary, but does not remove the social and administrative price of approaching decision-makers. [19]

**Financial irregularities reduce confidence:** An Auditor General report described by *Dawn* in May 2020 identified PKR 3.1 billion in irregular PBM expenditure against a PKR 5 billion budget, equivalent to 62%; PKR 475 million had been recovered. These are audit classifications of expenditure and recovery, not a household-level poverty-impact estimate. [25]

### Dandot Bait-ul-Mal: a separate private initiative

**The institutional distinction matters:** Dandot Bait-ul-Mal, or DBM, is a locally administered private charity in Chakwal, not the federal Pakistan Bait-ul-Mal institution. It paid PKR 700 per month to 79 households in 2012–13, supporting groups including widows, disabled people and low-income households. [22]

**Reliable payments coexist with mistargeting:** A 2013 convenience survey of 486 Dandot households found that all three studied programs—DBM, official Zakat and BISP—missed poor households while serving some non-poor households. DBM recipients received their stated entitlements regularly, but the study identified one household with two assisted members and interpreted this as favoritism. Limited coverage and unequal selection thus remained problems even where cash delivery itself worked. [22]

| Local rank | Program | Authors’ targeting-count-gap index | Payment pattern reported |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | BISP | 0.147 | Quarterly, with some missed installments |
| 2 | Official Zakat System | 0.156 | Frequently irregular |
| 3 | Dandot Bait-ul-Mal | 0.209 | Regular monthly payment |

**The comparison separates two successes:** A lower index represents fewer combined targeting errors under the study’s PKR 2,000 monthly adult-equivalent expenditure poverty line. BISP performed best on that measure, while DBM performed well in paying selected households their entitlements. The authors observed no financial corruption in the studied programs, demonstrating why non-poor inclusion and missing payments should not automatically be assigned the same cause. [22]

### Formal employment-linked social security

**Employment status creates structural exclusion:** Pakistan’s formal-worker arrangements include provincial employees’ social security, the Employees’ Old Age Benefits Institution, government pensions and workers’ welfare schemes. An ODI review found that these arrangements did not extend to agriculture—then employing more than 40% of the labor force—the informal economy, temporary contractor workers or establishments with fewer than ten workers. [26]

**The poorest often lack the qualifying job:** Agricultural laborers, casual construction workers, domestic and informal workers, and workers in small enterprises may face substantial income risk without the employment relationship needed for insurance. This is not simply a mistaken poverty classification: the protection channel is attached to a labor-market position many poor people do not hold. [26]

**The Pakistani judgment:** Pakistan’s programs exclude through different doors: religion in Zakat, connections in discretionary assistance, records and activation in BISP, and employment status in social insurance. A better registry can improve several doors, but effective protection also requires accessible payments and a route for informal workers. The main problem is segmentation, not one universally defective program model.

## Part One: Bangladesh — targeting the poorest without accommodating their circumstances

### Challenging the Frontiers of Poverty Reduction: Targeting the Ultra Poor

**Ultra-poor identification is only the beginning:** BRAC’s Challenging the Frontiers of Poverty Reduction–Targeting the Ultra Poor project, or CFPR/TUP, combines ultra-poor targeting with assets, enterprise development and related livelihood inputs. Its selection process incorporated community appraisal, household surveys, preliminary selection and final selection. Detailed targeting therefore preceded another important decision: which household could participate in a particular enterprise. [27]

**Enterprise screening excluded vulnerable women:** In a documented poultry-selection episode in Barhatta, Netrokona, an officer selected 50 potential bird rearers from 169 TUP households in 36 villages. Subsequent verification rejected 20 of the 50. The reasons show how delivery requirements can reverse the apparent logic of targeting the most disadvantaged. [6]

| Recorded rejection reason | Women rejected | Calculated share of 50 selected | Exclusion mechanism |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Villages water-locked during rains | 10 | 20% | Feed transport could not reach houses |
| Standard eight-person group could not form | 3 | 6% | Group-size requirement |
| Frequent dependence on husbands’ consent | 4 | 8% | Gendered decision-making treated as unsuitable |
| Gradual loss of interest | 3 | 6% | Staff assessment of participation readiness |
| Total rejected at this screening | 20 | 40% | Combined enterprise-entry filters |

**Geography became a disqualification:** The ten women in water-locked villages were rejected because rickshaws or vans carrying feed could not reach their houses during the rainy season. Their remote location was therefore not merely an inconvenience after enrollment; it was used to deny the poultry activity. The case concerns access to that enterprise component, not a 40% exclusion rate for the entire CFPR/TUP program. [6]

**Gender constraints were selected against:** Four women were denied assets because their decisions depended too frequently on husbands’ consent. Instead of compensating for restricted agency, the enterprise-selection decision favored women already able to exercise more autonomy. Likewise, a group-size requirement excluded three women because enough nearby participants could not be assembled, rather than because their households were insufficiently poor. [6]

**Assets carried hidden entry costs:** Changing goat-shed instructions required some participants to rebuild as many as three times. Average shed construction cost was Tk 300–500, and most participants borrowed to finance it. An asset grant can therefore impose debt-financed complementary costs before its productive benefits arrive. [6]

**Procurement attracted opportunistic intermediaries:** At Samganj bazaar, middlemen attempted to buy goats from farmers and resell them to BRAC at higher prices when it arrived as a large purchaser. This illustrates an attempted market appropriation of program spending, distinct from officials manipulating beneficiary lists. [6]

**The CFPR/TUP judgment:** These cases do not establish that the livelihood model generally failed. They identify a specific tension: a program can locate ultra-poor households successfully, then screen out those whose isolation, household relations or inability to finance complementary inputs make delivery difficult. The model reaches deeper deprivation only when enterprise support accommodates those conditions.

### Vulnerable Group Development and Vulnerable Women Benefit

**Large reach concealed uneven selection:** Vulnerable Group Development, or VGD, provided food and development support to very poor rural women; the related Vulnerable Women Benefit program is abbreviated VWB. IFPRI reported annual direct reach of up to 750,000 women, but in 2015 only 43% of recipients came from the poorest fifth of rural households and 71% from the poorest two-fifths. Selection was progressive, yet many places went outside the poorest groups the program emphasized. [28]

**Criteria were difficult to apply:** Eligibility included functionally landless households owning less than 0.15 acres, but IFPRI found no mechanism for checking the threshold and ambiguity about homestead land. Female headship was also an imperfect poverty indicator: around 30% of female-headed households in the assessment were relatively well-off, often supported by migrants’ remittances. Disability-related criteria likewise had not been demonstrated to distinguish the poorest households reliably in that assessment. [28]

**Local power altered selection:** Uddin’s 2014 qualitative study found that weak social networks restricted potential clients while stronger connections facilitated access. One widowed mother with three children under five repeatedly approached a ward member but was deferred; local women described selection favoring his relatives. Another household facing a husband’s blood cancer and children’s disabilities reported exclusion linked to the selector’s hostility toward its Maimal fishing-community identity. [29]

**Recent verification found correctable errors:** Transparency International Bangladesh’s report dated 14 September 2023 identified 2,259 ineligible names among 93,719 entries in its latest table, or 2.41%. It also identified 2,062 entries, or 2.20%, involving previous-cycle VGD recipients contrary to the cycle rule. The report recorded replacement of 2,092 ineligible beneficiaries—92.61% of identified errors—showing that scrutiny could change lists. [30]

**Publicity and forms affected access:** TIB described bribery, political influence, nepotism and vote-seeking as risks in enrollment. Inadequate publicity disadvantaged women unaware of applications, while missing housing, cultivable-land and income fields weakened screening. The absence of a single registry also impeded checks against previous receipt. [30]

**Participation did not mean package acceptance:** A 606-household study of the Income Generation for Vulnerable Group Development component, or IGVGD, found that most participants did not take the full developmental package. Many entered without intending full participation, reflecting differences between ultra-poor women’s priorities and constraints and planners’ expectations. Selective participation can therefore indicate an unsuitable package rather than absence of need. [1]

### Old Age Allowance

**Poor elderly people remained outside:** The Old Age Allowance, or OAA, provides cash assistance to poor older people. CPD’s 2023 field assessment found that 56% of its OAA nonbeneficiaries had monthly income below BDT 1,000, while 95% received no other allowance, pension or VGD benefit. These were households with very low earnings and little land, not simply people already protected elsewhere. [31]

**Verification poorly matched eligibility:** Using HIES 2016, the World Bank reported that 68% of OAA recipients met demographic criteria, only 12% met economic criteria, and 20% met neither. It linked the mismatch to missing age evidence, difficult income verification and unobservable land ownership, while also suggesting that some economic thresholds may have become obsolete. [32]

**Lobbying favored more capable applicants:** Research summarized by Development Pathways reported that over 90% of successful applicants and 82% of unsuccessful applicants had lobbied decision-makers. Around one-third of recipients in the cited evidence were below eligibility age, associated with younger people’s greater ability to lobby. Some extremely poor people did not apply because they could not afford a bribe. [24]

**Receipt nevertheless provided real benefits:** A qualitative study reported improved food and calorie access and greater status within families and communities among rural poor elderly recipients. The World Bank estimated OAA benefits at about 7% of poor households’ average income, larger than many other transfers but still modest. The appropriate judgment is useful protection with serious gaps in reach and value. [33][32]

### Widow and Husband Deserted Destitute Women Allowance

**Priority status did not ensure coverage:** This allowance provides cash to widows and husband-deserted destitute women. CPD’s 2023 assessment identified a group comprising 25% of surveyed widows who should have been prioritized under the criteria but remained outside coverage. Among nonbeneficiaries, 97% received neither VGD nor another allowance. [31]

**Selection overlooked severe deprivation:** The brief attributed the gap to faulty selection, including insufficient priority for landlessness and homelessness. Poor coordination also meant that exclusion from this allowance was not reliably compensated by another program. Meanwhile, the World Bank estimated the allowance at about 7% of poor households’ average income, leaving substantial unmet needs even among recipients. [31][2]

### Employment Generation Program for the Poorest

**Employment offers can be a poor fit:** The Employment Generation Program for the Poorest, or EGPP, provides wage employment to poor workers. World Bank analysis found that recipients more often satisfied its economic criteria than its demographic age criterion of 18–60. It described older men and working-age women participating while some working-age men traveled farther for better-paid employment. [32]

**Opportunity costs shape participation:** A worker choosing better-paid employment is not necessarily evidence of program failure: the public-work option may function as a fallback. The concern arises where its value, timing or work requirements make it unsuitable for households with the greatest need. Employment-based assistance also does not substitute for income support to people unable to supply the required labor.

**Complementary support remained limited:** Only 1% of EGPP beneficiaries also received NGO assistance in the World Bank review. This measures limited combination of support, which matters when a household needs employment income together with health, disability or shock assistance. The review warned that blanket restrictions on receiving more than one program could undermine protection against distinct risks. [2]

### Primary Education Stipend Programme

**Formal expansion did not remove access costs:** The Primary Education Stipend Programme, or PESP, supports primary-school participation through cash stipends. Early analysis in 2003 found that loosely defined targeting could favor moderately poor families rather than the poorest, with admission costs potentially preventing poor children from entering the eligible pupil population. Weak management and corruption were identified as possible influences on selection. [1]

**The eligibility context changed:** CPD’s 2023 assessment described PESP as universal for the relevant primary-school pupil population. It nevertheless identified documentation, communication, transport and hostel problems, with different barriers in plains, hills, remote settlements and *char* areas—riverine islands and sandbars. Poor children outside accessible eligible schools can therefore remain beyond practical reach despite broader formal eligibility. [31]

**Administrative errors prolong nonreceipt:** CPD described teachers and education officers performing data entry without sufficient technical capacity, producing errors and lengthy corrections. Guardians could also be misled by hackers or mobile-banking agents during withdrawal. At the BDT 150 monthly stipend used in its assessment, modest benefits could be further eroded by repeated administrative and collection burdens. [31]

### Maternal Health Voucher Scheme and public maternal services

**A voucher is partial protection:** The Demand Side Financing–Maternal Health Voucher Scheme, or DSF-MHVS, helps women access maternal services through demand-side financing. The World Bank found that only 3% of its beneficiaries also received NGO support, illustrating limited complementary assistance around a particular health intervention. [2]

**Poor women face additional costs:** A separate national benefit-incidence study using the 2017–18 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey found secondary public maternal facilities more pro-rich, while primary facilities were generally pro-poor. Transport, food, caregivers’ accommodation, user fees and medicines created barriers for poor women needing higher-level care. This establishes the wider service environment in which vouchers operate, rather than a voucher-specific effect. [34]

Poor households received relatively favorable shares of antenatal care and normal deliveries but much smaller shares of Caesarean benefits. A nominally subsidized service can therefore remain inaccessible when reaching the appropriate facility and financing associated expenses require resources the poorest women lack. [34]

### Vulnerable Group Feeding and related food support

**Food programs combine reach and cost problems:** Vulnerable Group Feeding, or VGF, provides food assistance to vulnerable households. At the broader food-support-program level, the World Bank’s MICS 2019 estimates found inclusion errors of 54% in rural areas and 45% in urban areas. These are group-level measures, encompassing food support rather than isolating VGF. [2]

**Delivery consumes substantial resources:** Food-transfer administrative costs were typically almost 2.5 times those of cash transfers because of storage, transport and distribution. Poor food-insecure households are underserved when allocations are poorly directed or a large delivery burden reduces the assistance a budget can purchase. Food can nevertheless be valuable where cash cannot reliably buy supplies; the appropriate comparison depends on functioning markets as well as delivery costs. [32]

**Geographic allocation compounds household errors:** In 2016, 35.7% of rural households received at least one social-assistance benefit, compared with 10.9% of urban households. Higher rural poverty explains part of this difference, but the World Bank concluded that the portfolio was poorly prepared for growing urban poverty. An urban ultra-poor household can therefore face limited program presence before household-level selection even begins. [32]

**The Bangladeshi judgment:** Bangladesh’s central challenge is not simply too little targeting. It is the combination of imperfect poverty proxies, discretionary selection, delivery requirements that penalize difficult circumstances, and benefits often too small to address multiple risks. Targeting becomes more protective when it accommodates—not screens against—the conditions of ultra-poor life.

## Part One: Nepal — strong old-age coverage, large gaps elsewhere

### Social Security Allowances

**Coverage is concentrated rather than absent:** Nepal’s Social Security Allowance system, or SSA, includes old-age, single-women, disability, endangered-ethnicity and child benefits. It recorded approximately 3.48 million recipients in FY2020–21. Across the wider protection system, older people had much stronger coverage than working-age people or children needing cash income support. [3]

| Population or measurement | FY2020–21 coverage | What the figure captures |
|---|---:|---|
| Total population, broad national basket | 32.9% | Cash and in-kind protection |
| Total population, cash-focused SDG measure | 16.9% | Regular cash-benefit coverage |
| Older people, age 60+ | 80.2% | At least one relevant benefit |
| Working-age people, age 15–59 | 9.9% | Income support and other included measures |
| Children, age 0–14, broad basket | 65.7% | Substantial school-related in-kind support |
| Children, age 0–14, cash-focused measure | 13.7% | Income support |

**The apparent coverage conflict resolves:** Human Rights Watch’s approximately 17% coverage figure aligns closely with the ILO’s 16.9% cash-focused measure. The ILO’s 32.9% estimate uses a broader basket including in-kind provision. These represent different protection measures, not evidence that national coverage suddenly doubled. [3][35]

**Identity requirements exclude needy people:** Human Rights Watch described citizenship certificates—or birth registration for child benefits—as entry conditions. A 2015 study projected that 6.7 million people, approximately 23% of the population, would lack citizenship certificates in 2021. A 76-year-old interviewee explained that paperwork problems prevented him from obtaining the old-age allowance despite extremely low earnings. [35]

**Banking creates a second journey:** A June 2023 study in four municipalities used eight focus groups and twenty key-informant interviews. It documented payment delays and difficult travel to banks, including a woman in Bhotekoshi facing fares as high as NPR 300 per person. Landslides blocked roads during the monsoon, making collection particularly difficult for people with limited mobility. [36]

**Stigma also deters applications:** Human Rights Watch described public beneficiary lists and official encouragement to renounce benefits for honorific recognition as practices that could make assistance appear shameful. A single mother with a nine-year-old son avoided seeking support because she anticipated humiliation, including the possibility of returning empty-handed. This is evidence about deterrence from seeking assistance, rather than a measured SSA take-up rate among a defined eligible population. [35]

### Child Grant

**Young-child support remains incomplete:** The Child Grant’s rollout covered Dalit children nationally and up to two children per mother in 25 districts under the arrangements examined by the ILO. Approximately 36.3% of children aged 0–4 received the relevant income support, principally through the grant. A coverage study cited by the ILO found receipt among only 51% of the Child Grant’s intended population, distinguishing incomplete enrollment from deliberate geographic or categorical boundaries. [3]

**The monthly amount is small:** At NPR 532 per child, the grant equaled only 4.6 days of minimal consumption needs in the ILO assessment. Using its NPR 3,450 monthly minimum expenditure basket, the calculated ratio is `532 ÷ 3,450 × 100 = 15.4%`. It offers a contribution to household resources, not a full minimum-income guarantee. [3]

**Older children face a different gap:** Children aged 5–14 lacked a broad cash-support scheme, even though many received school meals or scholarships. A school meal supports nutrition and attendance but does not replace household earnings lost through illness or unemployment. Poor families can consequently remain exposed to pressures for child labor or early marriage after grant eligibility ends. [3]

**Recipients report meaningful gains:** Evidence from the Karnali rollout associated the grant with improved food security and health. In a 2,040-household survey cited by Human Rights Watch, 93% of beneficiaries believed government cared about their socioeconomic welfare, and 85% reported an improved view of government. These gains reinforce the importance of wider reach and adequate benefit value rather than supporting abandonment of the scheme. [35]

### Disability Allowance

**Certification is an access barrier:** Nepal’s Disability Allowance provides cash to eligible people with full or severe disabilities through the national disability-card system. Applicants have faced difficult journeys through mountainous terrain to district assessment offices. An earlier review reported only 16,200 severe-disability grant recipients, explaining low access partly through the difficulty of reaching assessment. [37][24]

**Intended recipients were frequently missed:** A coverage study cited by the ILO found that the allowance reached about 43% of its intended population. The mechanisms include disability assessment, certification burdens and coverage limited to two of the four disability categories recognized in legislation. People with severe mobility limitations may face the greatest difficulty completing the process designed to establish their eligibility. [3]

**Needs exceed a standard benchmark:** The ILO’s benefit tables report NPR 2,128 monthly for severe disability and NPR 3,990 for full disability. The latter exceeds the NPR 3,450 basic expenditure benchmark, but that benchmark does not incorporate disability-related additional costs. Cash adequacy therefore depends on access to healthcare, assistive support and other services as well as the nominal payment. [3]

### Prime Minister Employment Programme

**The employment promise is underfilled:** The Prime Minister Employment Programme, or PMEP, provides temporary public employment, aiming at 100 days for registered unemployed people, with a subsistence-allowance provision when guaranteed work is not supplied. The ILO estimated effective reach at 17.9% of its unemployed-population benchmark and reported an average of 76 workdays among beneficiaries in FY2020–21. [3]

**Annualized earnings reveal the trade-off:** Using the ILO’s national minimum daily wage benchmark, estimated earnings were `76 × NPR 577 = NPR 43,852 per year`, or about NPR 3,654 per month when spread over twelve months. That is not a monthly payment schedule. The calculation shows how an acceptable daily rate can still produce limited annual income when workdays are rationed and earnings support a family. [3]

**Eligibility misses underemployment:** The ILO specifically identified the absence of protection for underemployed people, despite their income insecurity. Budget constraints and local governments’ limited capacity to identify productive projects also restricted opportunities; unskilled temporary work might provide income without substantially improving future employability. [3]

**Registration and payment timing matter:** The June 2023 municipal study described a limited annual registration and work cycle, with payment generally following completion, often in one installment. Applicants missing the registration window and households unable to finance consumption while waiting face additional barriers. The study identified payment and access difficulties without attributing the entire program’s performance to landslides or flooding. [36]

### Contribution-based social security and provident funds

**Formal employment dominates effective access:** Nepal’s Social Security Fund, or SSF, provides contributory protection for medical care, maternity, injury, disability, dependants and old age. The Employees Provident Fund, or EPF, mainly provides accumulated savings on retirement or employment termination. In the ILO’s FY2020–21 analysis, contributory schemes reached only 4.3% of working-age people. [3]

**Legal ambition exceeded operational access:** The ILO identified a gap between legislation envisaging wider worker coverage and working procedures enabling enrollment, which remained concentrated on formal employment in the period examined. This mattered in a labor market where the 2017–18 survey classified 84.6% of employment as informal, including 90.5% of women’s employment. [3]

**Institutional coordination was weak:** A preliminary assessment through December 2019 described an undeveloped database and overlapping medical arrangements across the EPF, Citizens Investment Trust and Health Insurance Board. Later ILO analysis continued to emphasize coordination, employer compliance and processes adapted to informal and self-employed workers. A young system explains part of low enrollment, but does not remove the need for workable access outside standard payroll employment. [38][3]

### Public-sector pensions

**Established pensions absorb substantial spending:** Public-sector pensions protect retired government employees after qualifying service. In FY2020–21, they reached approximately 273,000 recipients while accounting for 43.1% of social-protection expenditure. The World Bank evidence cited by the ILO placed 68% of pensioners in the richest two quintiles and only 6% in the poorest quintile. [3]

**Budget concentration is not theft:** These are employment-earned entitlements, not automatically examples of elite capture. Their significance is comparative: households without qualifying public employment depend on much smaller assistance benefits. A system can honor pension rights while still allocating insufficient resources to ultra-poor children and informal-worker families.

### National Health Insurance Programme

**Legal entitlement exceeds affiliation:** The Health Insurance Board’s national scheme aims to cover all citizens, with contribution subsidies for designated groups including the ultra-poor. It recorded approximately 4.52 million members, or 15.06% of the population, in the ILO’s FY2020–21 analysis. The wider health-protection total reached 17.7% when other arrangements were included. [3]

**Poverty classification can restrict subsidies:** The ILO identified the risk that poor households without contributory capacity could be classified outside the “ultra-poor” category, losing subsidies and being deterred from enrollment. Geographic access, service quality and spending ceilings also limit practical protection. The standard package included an annual ceiling of NPR 100,000 for a five-member family, with additions for extra members up to the stated maximum. [3]

**Insurance cannot replace nearby services:** The ILO cited a facility assessment finding that only about three-quarters of facilities offered the complete basic health-service package. A nominal insurance entitlement provides limited practical value where an appropriate provider is distant, the required service is absent, or treatment exceeds the benefit ceiling. [3]

### Safe Motherhood Programme

**Transport assistance has wider reach:** The Safe Motherhood Programme supports institutional delivery and maternal care, including transport incentives of NPR 1,000 in the Terai, NPR 2,000 in hill areas and NPR 3,000 in mountain areas. The ILO reported coverage of 62.1% of women giving birth using the latest data in its review. [3]

**The benefit covers a different risk:** Mothers outside formal employment may receive transport assistance yet still lack replacement income during maternity. The ILO’s separate maternity-income-protection measure was only about 0.8% of women giving birth. The gap is therefore not evidence that transport incentives have no value; it shows why service-use support cannot substitute for income security around childbirth. [3]

### School meals and scholarships

**School meals provide partial coverage:** Nepal’s School Meals Programme, including government midday meals and World Food Programme food-for-education provision, supported children from early childhood development through grade five in public schools. Its value was calculated at NPR 390 per school month: `NPR 15 per meal × 26 school days`. Enrollment and grade boundaries leave out-of-school children and older pupils beyond this route, while the benefit supports only part of overall nutritional needs. [3]

**Scholarship reach can be shallow:** Targeted scholarships support groups including Dalit pupils, girls, disabled children and other disadvantaged students. The widely received Dalit and girls’ awards were equivalent to about NPR 33 per month and represented an estimated 80% of scholarship beneficiaries; larger awards went to smaller groups. Poor enrolled pupils therefore received support with limited purchasing power, while children excluded from schooling could not benefit through a school-based transfer. [3]

**The Nepalese judgment:** Nepal’s relatively broad old-age protection demonstrates that substantial reach is possible, while child income support, employment protection and informal-worker insurance remain much weaker. Documentation and travel barriers then narrow access within the categories already covered. The decisive issue is extending adequate, usable protection across the life course rather than counting more schemes.

## Part Two: Why these failures occur

### 1. Program Design Flaws

**Design determines exclusion before implementation:** Program Design Flaws are problems in a program’s fundamental concepts, assumptions or rules. A correctly administered scheme can still miss deeply poor people if it assumes that all needy households can work, own the required documents, fit a particular family category or finance complementary inputs.

**Categorical rules create predictable gaps:** Zakat’s Muslim-only channel excludes poor non-Muslims by its governing logic. NSAP’s widow-age, disability and BPL boundaries can exclude needy people even when officials apply the rules faithfully. Nepal’s Child Grant leaves a major age gap, while employment-linked insurance in Pakistan and Nepal makes qualifying employment—not deprivation—the main entrance condition. These examples establish design exclusion without requiring an allegation of corruption. [19][14][3]

**Household assumptions hide internal inequality:** MGNREGA’s household entitlement can expand women’s opportunities, particularly through equal wages and nearby work, but it also makes their access dependent on household allocation of a limited entitlement. A cash transfer paid to a woman likewise does not automatically remove restrictions on her movement, time or decisions. CFPR/TUP’s rejection of women dependent on husbands’ consent shows the consequence of assuming autonomy rather than supporting its development. [8][6]

| Approach | Relevant programs | Main strength | Design-related exclusion risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broad categorical cash | Nepal old-age allowance | Less poverty-ranking discretion | Age boundaries and documentation |
| Poverty-targeted cash | BISP, NSAP pensions | Concentrates a limited budget | Misclassification and beneficiary ceilings |
| Work-based assistance | MGNREGA, EGPP, PMEP | Income linked to employment | Labor constraints and limited workdays |
| Asset/livelihood package | CFPR/TUP, IGVGD | Potential productive support | Complementary costs and enterprise-readiness requirements |
| Service-linked assistance | PESP, maternal vouchers | Supports education or healthcare | Access depends on reaching the service |

**Low adequacy is a design issue:** The World Bank found most Bangladeshi transfers equivalent to only 1–3% of poor or extreme-poor household income. Its 2016 accounting comparison for beneficiaries of seven core programs placed extreme poverty at 22.2% before benefits and 19.8% after benefits—a calculated reduction of 2.4 percentage points. Such evidence shows that correct delivery of a small transfer can provide relief without being large enough to transform poverty status. [32]

**The alternatives involve trade-offs:** Broader benefits reduce the need to rank nearly poor households against one another, but require sustainable financing. Narrow targeting may conserve transfers while increasing screening costs and false exclusions. Work and asset programs can add productive opportunities, but they should complement—not replace—basic protection for labor-constrained households.

**The design judgment:** Design-related failure ranges from partial protection through inadequate benefits to complete legal exclusion by age, faith or employment category. The judgment changes where eligibility reflects actual vulnerability, benefit value addresses meaningful needs and alternative routes protect people unable to meet work or service conditions. The key lesson is that a program should not require households to cease displaying the disadvantages it exists to address.

### 2. Elite Capture

**Power can override formal need:** Elite Capture occurs when wealthy or powerful actors exploit program decisions or resources for their own benefit. It can involve elected representatives, local committee members, officials, dealers or market intermediaries; it is broader than outright theft.

**Scarcity strengthens local gatekeepers:** The Pakistan interviews show how sifarish influences selection among people who may all be eligible. Bangladesh’s VGD cases show ward-level relationships and community identity affecting access. In both settings, the harm includes a deserving but disconnected household losing a scarce place, even when the successful applicant is also poor. [19][29]

**Connections influence formal credentials:** Panda’s analysis of a nationally representative Indian household survey found that connection to a local political executive was associated with a significantly higher probability of obtaining a BPL ration card in national, rural and urban analyses. This supports a connection gradient in access: administrative credentials intended to identify poverty can also reflect political proximity. [39]

**Capture takes different operational forms:** PDS dealers can exploit fictitious cards or diverted grain; housing assistance can reach recipients below sanctioned value; procurement intermediaries can attempt to appropriate margins from asset purchases. These are different mechanisms requiring different interpretations. The CFPR goat-market episode concerned attempted resale at higher prices, whereas the housing evidence concerned shortfalls between sanctioned and received grants. [9][1][6]

**Local influence is not uniformly harmful:** Hossain’s VGD analysis argued that political leaders sometimes supported the vulnerable poorest to rebuild political capital, while community and partner scrutiny constrained selection. TIB’s recorded replacement of ineligible names also shows that local verification can correct exclusion-producing allocations. The important question is whether discretion is answerable to affected people, not simply whether selection is local. [1][30]

**Accountability requires an effective response:** BISP/Ehsaas has grievance mechanisms, but UNDP’s review emphasizes that complaint channels depend on the state’s capacity to respond. Giving an excluded household a complaint number offers little practical protection if the decision remains unchanged indefinitely. Public lists may expose favoritism, yet Nepal’s experience shows that disclosure can also discourage claimants through stigma. [40][35]

**The capture judgment:** Elite influence is strongest where scarce benefits, opaque decisions and weak correction mechanisms interact. It becomes less damaging when scrutiny changes allocations and excluded applicants obtain remedies without needing powerful sponsors. The key lesson is to assess who controls decisions and whether poor people can successfully challenge them.

TPDS supply-chain diversion/corruption and state/FPS gatekeeping excluded entitled households [122][72]; India >50% mid-2000s; later 41% [204]. ### 3. Targeting Errors

**Identification can fail without manipulation:** Targeting Errors are problems identifying and reaching intended poor households. Exclusion error concerns intended recipients left out; inclusion error concerns people selected outside the relevant eligibility or poverty criterion. Diversion of money or grain is a separate delivery problem, even though studies sometimes use “leakage” for both.

**Different denominators answer different questions:** India’s historical 61% BPL exclusion estimate concerns eligible people omitted. VGD’s 43% recipient share from the poorest fifth concerns who received places. BISP’s eligible-to-active gap concerns activation among families already identified. Each is informative, but none can substitute for the others or establish a comparable country ranking.

**A progressive program can under-cover:** VGD’s poorest fifth received a larger recipient share than its population share, yet many poorest households could still remain outside. Shirazi’s Zakat results similarly combined high concentration of funds among the lowest decile with limited coverage within that decile. Progressive distribution among beneficiaries is therefore compatible with widespread exclusion of poor nonbeneficiaries. [28][22]

**Proxies simplify a changing reality:** Asset ownership, household composition and housing materials are easier to record than irregular income. But a durable asset can remain after earnings collapse, remittances can make a female-headed household better-off, and illness can impoverish a household between surveys. IFPRI’s VGD diagnosis and Nepal’s registry experience show why observable characteristics do not automatically track current deprivation. [28][35]

**Registries can reproduce missingness:** Remote BISP households omitted during enumeration receive no score at all. Genuine MGNREGA workers deleted during a compliance exercise can disappear from administrative demand. Nepal’s fragmented records—described in 2023 as 76 schemes within 29 programs across eleven ministries—also complicated identification and emergency outreach after the earthquake and pandemic. [24][4][41]

**Duplication needs interpretation:** Two similar subsistence allowances paid contrary to rules can displace another household under a fixed budget. By contrast, a child’s education stipend and a parent’s disability allowance address different needs. Bangladesh’s evidence on limited benefit combinations warns against treating every overlap as waste: coordination should identify both inappropriate duplication and valuable complementarity. [32]

**The targeting judgment:** Better information helps only if households can enter records, update circumstances and correct mistakes. Targeting performs more convincingly when repeated receipt measures show low omission among intended poor people, not merely few rich beneficiaries. The key lesson is to judge identification from the perspective of poor nonrecipients as well as those enrolled.

**Additional documented evidence**

Targeting error = non-poor included/poor excluded [266]. India: MGNREGS’s rural bias misses urban poor [202]; TPDS/NFSA stale/bogus lists omit poor despite 75% rural/50% urban [122][72]. Pakistan: Zakat/PBM community targeting is unverifiable; BISP (2008) NSER: >35m HH, >90% population targets poor [193]. Nepal: SSA ghosts; fragmented data impede reach [265][267].

### 4. Invisible Barriers

**Nominal eligibility has a real price:** Invisible Barriers are obstacles often overlooked by planners, including gender norms, hidden costs, missing identification documents and distance. They explain why an eligible person may be unable to convert a formal benefit into usable protection.

**Gender allocates time and authority:** MGNREGA’s childcare gaps make employment harder for women responsible for young children. CFPR/TUP’s consent-related rejection penalized women with restricted household decision-making. Nepal’s citizenship difficulties for children of single mothers show how gendered institutions can also restrict the documents needed for benefits. These are different expressions of the same assumption: that an applicant controls her own time, movement and records. [8][6][35]

**Hidden costs can absorb benefits:** CFPR recipients borrowed for sheds; Nepalese SSA beneficiaries paid for transport; Bangladeshi maternity patients faced medicines, food and accommodation expenses. None of these costs appears fully in the headline transfer. Their significance is greatest for ultra-poor households because even a modest upfront expense can prevent participation before any benefit arrives.

**Documents create cumulative barriers:** A missing identity card can block enrollment, an inconsistent name can obstruct a linked application, and an unresolved certification can delay payment. West Bengal’s Riya Soren encountered a ration-card cancellation linked to a spelling difference in her husband’s name, alongside other linked-scheme problems. Such errors turn a small record discrepancy into repeated journeys and foregone wages. [18]

**Distance affects every delivery model:** BISP enumerators could fail to reach isolated houses; CFPR feed transport could not reach water-locked villages; PESP pupils in hills and char areas faced transport and accommodation problems; Nepalese disability applicants had to reach assessment offices despite mobility impairments. Cash, assets, employment and services all depend on physical access somewhere in the process.

**Digital access is not automatically easier:** Authentication may reduce fictitious transactions while requiring working servers, matching records and usable fingerprints. For elderly recipients, CPD described fingerprint difficulties, missing phones or SIM cards, forgotten PINs and unauthorized withdrawals. A digital system shifts burdens rather than eliminating them unless people have an accessible alternative when a check fails. [31]

**The access judgment:** A benefit becomes genuinely accessible when its time, travel, care and documentation requirements fit recipients’ circumstances. Reduced distance, workable alternative identification and reliable payment completion would change the judgment even without changing formal eligibility. The key lesson is that access costs belong inside the assessment of benefit value.

**Additional documented evidence**

TPDS/NFSA: awareness, ration-card and digital gaps impede rural access; misidentification, leakage and weak redressal exclude eligible [72][122]. PM POSHAN: unpaid maternal supervision and precarious women cooks create gendered care burdens [269]. Complex payments and transport/safety costs burden women [268][269]. Nepal SSA: evidence is broader policy [265].

Invisible: TPDS docs/info/rural connectivity block access; Mid-Day Meal adds unpaid care; delivery costs deter use [72][122][269].

### 5. Self-Exclusion by the Poor

**Nonparticipation can be a constrained choice:** Self-Exclusion by the Poor concerns decisions not to apply, participate or collect benefits. It should be separated from administrative rejection: deleting a job card or refusing an eligible applicant is not the applicant choosing to withdraw.

**Foregone wages can outweigh rations:** In the 25-person migrant study, 68% did not carry ration cards to cities, only 8% knew about portability and only one respondent expressed willingness to use it. Reported reasons included leaving cards with village relatives, contractor-provided meals, fear of losing documents and the cost of missing work. [11]

**The arithmetic explains the decision:** Using respondents’ reported comparison, losing one day’s INR 400 wages to collect rations valued at INR 125–200 produces a conditional net loss of **INR 200–275**, before travel: `400 − 200 = 200` and `400 − 125 = 275`. This worked estimate explains why a poor worker could rationally forgo a positive nominal entitlement.

**Bribes can deter entry altogether:** The OAA evidence describes some Bangladeshi applicants who stayed away because they could not afford a bribe. A reported example cited by Development Pathways involved a US$26 entry payment for a US$4 monthly benefit. The simple payback period is an estimated 6.5 months, calculated as `26 ÷ 4`, assuming full payment and no further costs. For a household needing food immediately, that is a substantial barrier. [24]

**Work choices require careful interpretation:** EGPP participants choosing higher-paid work may be using labor markets sensibly rather than rejecting social protection irrationally. MGNREGA’s low wages and delayed payments can similarly discourage primary earners, while nearby work and equal pay remain attractive to women. A household’s need for protection can persist even when one offered activity is unattractive. [32][7]

**Package refusal reveals mismatched priorities:** IGVGD’s partial participation shows that ultra-poor women may value food support without accepting every developmental activity. CFPR’s recorded “loss of interest” likewise describes a participation judgment, while rejection for group size or husbands’ consent is an administrative decision. These distinctions matter because redesigning an unsuitable activity and reversing an improper rejection are different responses. [1][6]

**Dignity affects willingness to apply:** Nepal’s interview evidence shows that shame and anticipated rejection can deter people experiencing hunger from seeking assistance. Publicly presenting support as charity, or honoring people who renounce it, can increase that cost. The resulting nonapplication expresses a socially constrained choice, not proof that the household does not need support. [35]

**The participation judgment:** Higher take-up after timely payments, lower access costs or more suitable activities would indicate that earlier nonparticipation reflected program fit rather than low need. The key lesson is that the poorest may reject an offer because they cannot afford its conditions.

**Additional documented evidence**

MGNREGA (India): 100-day manual work → self-selection; screening/info, stigma/costs deter take-up [42]. SSA (Nepal): awareness/docs, not proven choice; Pakistan BISP women: mobility/finance barriers [15,566]. NFSA/TPDS: 75% rural/50% urban entitlement, ration card/FPS [72]. PESP: 40% female/10% male enrolment; PM POSHAN: attendance/nutrition [159]. ## Comparative judgment

**The countries expose different weaknesses:** India illustrates the fragility of formal entitlements when work and delivery are rationed. Pakistan demonstrates how faith-based assistance, discretionary relief, national cash transfers and formal-worker insurance can leave different populations outside. Bangladesh shows that sophisticated targeting and livelihood support can still penalize households whose circumstances make participation difficult. Nepal demonstrates both the reach achievable through broad old-age benefits and the gaps left by age and employment segmentation.

**No single program type dominates:** Employment programs are useful for people able to work but cannot substitute for protection during disability, maternity or care responsibilities. Asset packages can address productive constraints but require complementary finance and support. Cash is flexible and can cost less to administer than food, while food retains advantages where markets or supplies are unreliable. Health and education transfers depend on accessible services as well as financial entitlements.

**The strongest overall conclusion:** Reaching the ultra-poor requires adequate basic protection that remains usable without political sponsorship, substantial upfront spending or prolonged waiting, complemented by employment, livelihood and service support appropriate to household circumstances. This judgment would weaken if those groups consistently received timely, adequate benefits at low access cost; high enrollment or a cleaner registry alone would not establish that outcome.

**The figures measure different things:** Historical and local findings retain their years, populations and denominators; coverage, recipient composition, authentication readiness and audited irregular expenditure are distinct measures. Program-group estimates are identified as such. TIB’s latest cycle is labeled 2023–24 in tables and 2022–23 in its narrative. Worked estimates cover administrative gaps, rejection shares, benchmark earnings and participation costs. Claims that particular poor households were displaced in historical housing, SGRY, SGSY, JRY or VGF cases turn on household-linked selection and payment records, beyond aggregate distributional findings. Umbrella programs and their components overlap, and the figures should not be pooled into a South Asia-wide failure rate.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Pakistan: scope and lifecycle**

Pakistan’s evidence shows mixed performance: several schemes delivered transfers at scale, but reach, targeting accuracy, benefit adequacy, and outcome evidence remain uneven. BISP and Ehsaas Kafaalat are linked phases rather than wholly separate national cash transfers: the Pakistan profile describes BISP as “rebranded as Ehsaas Kafaalat,” while the government site lists both Benazir Kafaalat and BISP [128][217]. A financial source reporting FY2026 figures and a Nashonuma evaluation dated 2 December 2025 fall outside the 30 September 2023 cutoff; their later allocations, disbursements, and outcome findings are therefore not used [208][234].

**Zakat and Ushr system**

**Benazir Income Support Programme (BISP)**

BISP is Pakistan’s federal, all-province, poverty-targeted unconditional cash-transfer programme. Its reported eligibility rule was a proxy-means-testing score of **16.17 or lower** for ever-married women; the cited profile reports a **PKR 5,000 payment every three months**, **9.1 million beneficiaries**, and a **PKR 200 billion allocation** in a row whose observation period is not stated [128]. The gender and poverty-score rules protect some poor households but formally restrict the recipient pathway to qualifying women.

Reach was substantially less than universal. In the PSLM **2018–19** household data, **7.7%** of Pakistani households reported receiving BISP assistance and **92.3%** did not; the denominator was all surveyed Pakistani households, not only eligible or poor households [128]. A separate scale estimate counted **5 million beneficiaries**, approximately **30 million people**, or **7.2% of Pakistan’s population** using a 2019 population estimate of 216 million, and fewer than **30% of people in need** using the 2015 poverty estimate [128]. These figures are not directly interchangeable because their periods and denominators differ.

**Ehsaas Kafaalat**

Delivery used biometric systems, bank accounts, mandate accounts, or digital wallets [182]. The main documented exclusion mechanism is the registry and targeting chain. The pre-Ehsaas paper survey was a one-off static instrument with no reliable way to establish when or where forms were completed, possible gaming, and inadequate security and validation; the source links these flaws to large inclusion and exclusion errors and many genuine beneficiaries being missed [182]. Broader estimates place National Socio-Economic Registry exclusion errors at **67–82%**, with a World Bank estimate as high as **88%**, but these are registry-wide estimates, not Kafaalat-specific rates [128]. Thus, the affected poor groups are not disaggregated, and the evidence supports a targeting-system failure without proving that every omitted household was ultra-poor.

**Ehsaas Emergency Cash**

Ehsaas Emergency Cash was a nationwide, one-time unconditional transfer for disaster-affected households, including people whose livelihoods were disrupted by COVID-19. One profile reports **PKR 12,000 per household**, **PKR 203 billion allocated**, and **15 million beneficiaries**, but supplies no eligible-household denominator or detailed outcome measure [128]. A separate programme table reports **USD 75 per family**, **15 million beneficiary households or 96 million people including family members**, and **99% delivery**, with **14.8 million individuals receiving cash by September 2020**; it lists **USD 1 billion** for the COVID response [182]. The PKR and USD amounts are retained as source-specific values, not treated as an exchange-rate calculation.

**Benazir Taleemi Wazaif**

The Government of Pakistan lists **Benazir Taleemi Wazaif** as a programme, but the cited official page does not specify its type, objective, eligibility, reach, benefit amount, failure, excluded groups, or exclusion reasons [217]. A related BISP education component, **Waseela-e-Taleem**, is documented separately as a conditional cash transfer for primary-school children: it required **70% school attendance**, listed payments of **PKR 1,500 per quarter for boys** and **PKR 2,000 for girls**, recorded **3.1 million children**, and reported **PKR 78.88 million in 2020–21** and **PKR 15.8 billion in cumulative disbursements** [128]. Those values are not assigned to Benazir Taleemi Wazaif because the shown evidence does not establish that the two programme identities are identical. The programme-specific failure and exclusion status therefore remains unresolved in this evidence set.

**Pakistan Bait-ul-Mal assistance**

PBM-related social-care services also show practice-level gaps. Services for vulnerable children were fragmented, had low residential capacity, and did not serve enough beneficiaries; prioritizing institution-based over community-based care limited reach and excluded those most in need [128]. Services for working-age adults were largely directed toward women survivors of violence and were inadequate in number [128]. These findings identify vulnerable children and women survivors as unreached groups, but do not provide their number or geographic distribution. The evidence therefore supports inadequate service capacity and a design-and-delivery problem, not a quantified national PBM exclusion rate.

**Sehat Sahulat Programme**

Sehat Sahulat is a Pakistani health-insurance programme; the cited profile distinguishes the **Sehat Sahulat Insaf Programme in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa**, restricted to people with KP domicile, from the broader Sehat Sahulat Programme [128]. It offered free healthcare to people below the poverty line, persons with disabilities, and transgender people registered with NADRA and issued a special CNIC. The profile reports **7,296,013 families enrolled** and **PKR 4.08 million allocated**, although the period and adequacy benchmark are not stated [128].

Sehat Sahulat used the National Socio-Economic Registry and a poverty-score threshold of **32.5** federally and in Punjab; the source warns that exclusion errors persist regardless of the threshold [128]. Registry-wide exclusion estimates of **67–82%**, and up to **88%** in a World Bank estimate, indicate a serious targeting risk but are not Sehat-specific rates [128]. Registration, CNIC issuance, and registry classification are concrete access conditions. The shown evidence does not identify the number or characteristics of people denied care, nor establish utilization, service quality, financial protection, or health outcomes; the documented problem is therefore a plausible registry and documentary exclusion pathway rather than a measured programme-wide failure.

**Benazir Nashonuma Programme**

Benazir Nashonuma is a nutrition-linked conditional cash transfer under the BISP/Ehsaas programme family, intended to address child stunting through specialized nutritious food, immunization, antenatal care, and health-awareness sessions [182][217]. It was launched in **August 2020** as a pilot in **nine high-stunting districts** [128]; a later pre-cutoff programme table reports nationwide scope and **100 districts covered** [182]. The phased figures should not be treated as contradictory: they indicate expansion from the initial pilot.

**Ehsaas Rashan Riayat**

Ehsaas Rashan Riayat was a nationwide targeted food subsidy for low-income households facing food inflation. It offered **USD 6 per family per month** on flour, lentils, and cooking oil through a digital token; applicants needed income below **USD 313 per month** and a phone SIM matching their National ID [182]. From **January to June 2022**, reported coverage was **6%: 1.25 million households out of an intended 20 million** [182]. The source attributes the shortfall to premature closure and a programme duration of only five months; it also records mobile-point-of-sale verification, OTP authentication, digital merchant settlement, and an **8% merchant commission** [182].

**Pakistan-wide protection gap**

As wider context rather than evidence against one named programme, the Pakistan profile reports that employment-linked contributory schemes covered only formal workers: approximately **2 million registered workers**, fewer than **2%** of an estimated **120 million working-age population** in the referenced **2017–18** data, while **27 million informal workers** were not covered. Old-age pensions were available only to formal-economy workers, leaving an estimated **80% of older persons** without any pension [128]. These structural gaps help explain why even large named programmes did not constitute comprehensive protection for Pakistan’s poor and ultra-poor population.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Nepal: mixed protection, reach, and delivery**

**Child Grant**

Nepal’s Child Grant is an unconditional **cash transfer** introduced in **2009**: mothers of eligible children under five received **NRs 1,600 every four months** to meet basic needs. Its objective was to support vulnerable children and reduce poverty, hunger, food insecurity, and malnutrition [71]. Although intended to become universal, it initially covered under-five children in remote Karnali and children from Dalit families. By 2019, all under-five children in eight deprived districts—**560,000 children**—were covered, while enrollment of a further **300,000 children** in six districts was being supported, not confirmed as completed receipt [71]. On 8 August 2023, universal availability still covered only **25 of Nepal’s 77 districts**, while nationwide availability to all children under five applied only to Dalits; children outside those geographic or categorical conditions were therefore the documented unreached group [70].

The Child Grant nevertheless produced mixed evidence rather than total failure. Studies reported increased birth registration, greater ability to buy food, clothes, and essentials, and lower child-labour likelihood among recipients and their siblings; research in Karnali reported improved food security and health. In a **2,040-household** survey in Karnali, **93%** of beneficiaries believed the government cared about their socioeconomic well-being and **85%** reported an improved opinion of government [70]. A **2021 UNICEF projection**, not an observed outcome, estimated that extending the grant to all children up to age 17 by 2035 would cost less than **0.7% of GDP per year** and could reduce poverty among households with children by up to **16.8%** [70].

**Aama Programme**

Nepal’s **Aama Programme (Safe Motherhood Programme)** began in **2005**, succeeding the Safe Delivery Incentive Programme, and is a nationwide mixed **social-assistance, conditional-cash-transfer, health-fee-waiver, and service-subsidy programme**. It targets Nepali pregnant and lactating women, newborns, children, and health workers, with the objective of reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [248][159]. It provides selected free services for mothers and newborns, cash incentives for four antenatal visits, institutional-delivery transport assistance, and payments to health workers; reported coverage was **248,750 women in 2017** [248]. The reported transport payments were **NPR1,500 in mountain areas, NPR1,000 in hills, and NPR500 in the Tarai**, plus **NPR400** after the required antenatal visits, institutional delivery, and postnatal care; health workers received **NPR300** for specified delivery or newborn-care services [248].

Accredited Social Health Activists supported early registration and were expected to help women without BPL cards obtain certification; some PHCCs conducted awareness activities [159]. Institutional deliveries, antenatal care, postnatal care, and maternal, infant, and newborn mortality were identified as monitoring measures. A 2011 Service Tracking Survey monitored implementation, and the 2018 Safe Motherhood and Reproductive Health Right Act supplied a legal framework; grievance provisions existed, but one cited report said the grievance mechanism had not been activated [248][159]. The available evidence does **not** identify a specific poor or ultra-poor group excluded from Aama or establish a quantified failure rate; that gap should not be converted into proof of exclusion.

**Karnali Employment Programme**

Nepal’s **Karnali Employment Programme (KEP)** was a regional **public-employment and development-works programme** launched in **2006/07** in five remote districts, promising **100 days of work for at least one member of every family** and a sustainable income for poor and marginalized people [116][255]. Priority criteria included disability, Dalit/minority/Indigenous status, female-headed households, pregnancy or lactation, and many dependent children or elderly people [159]. Implementation fell short: an assessment found an average of only **13 days** of work, wages of **Rs201 per worker per day**—below market and statutory minimum rates—and delayed payment [255]. Records report **60,539 beneficiaries and 13 days** at launch, **67,999 and 14 days** in FY2007/08, **71,005** beneficiaries in 2008/09, and only **10.50 days** on average in the last three years reported [116].

KEP also showed targeting and capture problems: non-poor people benefited, ruling-party cadres were reportedly favored, and ineffective district development committees produced serious errors in identifying needy families [116]. Funds intended for local development jobs were reportedly used to appoint office staff, while poor coordination weakened delivery [116]. A target of **Rs5,000** minimum income contrasted with reported annual household income of only **Rs2,600**; one record said at least **Rs1 billion** was needed to operate effectively, although reported government investment over seven years was **Rs1.47 billion**, an unresolved funding inconsistency [116]. Effects were mixed: **20.5%** of beneficiary households invested in assets, **42%** gained irrigation access, education expenditure rose from **18.1% in 2006/07 to 22.5% in 2010/11**, health expenditure reached **7.6%**, but consumption did not change [255]. KEP’s status after the cited 2014 evidence is not established here.

**Prime Minister Employment Programme**

Nepal’s **Prime Minister Employment Programme (PMEP)** is a national **public-employment social-protection programme** launched in **February 2019** for people aged **18–59**, with Employment Information Centres in all **753 local units**. Its initial objective was at least **100 days of employment for 5 million beneficiaries** through public infrastructure, alongside reduced labor-market gaps, lower labor migration, and subsidized loans for eligible entrepreneurs [160]. In practice, the target was quickly reduced to **30 days**. In FY2018/19, **175,909 applicants from 599 local units** received an average of **13 days** of work; government expenditure was **Rs2.36 billion**, with a further **Rs5.6 billion** provisioned for 200,000 jobs. In FY2019/20, **743,081 unemployed workers registered**, but more than **99%** had not received jobs. Workers without 100 days were legally eligible for a sustenance allowance equal to **50% of the minimum wage** for the remaining period [160].

**Poverty Alleviation Fund**

Nepal’s **Poverty Alleviation Fund (PAF)** is supported here by a historical **2004**, 142-page environmental assessment. It describes a mixed **livelihood, infrastructure, employment, and income-generation** project intended to improve livelihoods for **80% of the rural poor in six pilot districts**, including poor and socially excluded groups [117]. PAF used community participation and required baseline information on population, location, language, culture, Indigenous relationships, maps, social structure, income sources, resources, and production systems. The evidence establishes historical design and intended reach, but not realized coverage, benefit adequacy, exclusion rates, or practice-level failure; its status at the 2023 cutoff is therefore historical/uncertain rather than current [117].

- **Aama (Nepal):** Maternal CCT/fee waiver for women/newborns; 4 ANC visits plus institutional delivery; transport/free delivery reduce barriers. Reach: 336,417 women/62.1% of births (2020); 91% of entitled women received transport, and 87% of facility deliveries were free (2012). Distance/monitoring limit reach [249][99].
- **Child Grant:** 2009; NPR400/child/month (maximum NPR800/family); Karnali children ≤5, poor Dalit under-5s elsewhere; birth registration and maximum two children; 529,992 children (2017–18). Documentation/support gaps constrain access; exclusions and outcomes are unmeasured [161][250].
