# The 24-Hour Movement Guidelines and Childhood Obesity: Evidence Through May 2023

**The overall relationship was favorable, but not universal.** By May 2023, research generally associated meeting all three movement recommendations—or meeting more of them—with lower overweight, obesity, or body-fat measures in school-aged children and adolescents. A 14-report comparison contains **11 reports of an inverse association and 3 reports of no significant full-guideline association**. Adding four further relevant three-behavior analyses gives 15 association reports and 3 full-set null reports among 18 publications; within the 10 reports providing numerical statistical results, 9 contained at least one significant inverse whole-profile comparison and 1 reported a full-set null result. These findings establish associations, not proof that adherence prevents obesity. [1]

| Evidence question | Decisive result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| What did the 14-report comparison show? | 11 association reports; 3 full-set null reports | Predominantly inverse findings |
| What does the expanded comparison contain? | 18 three-behavior reports: 15 associative, 3 full-set null | Includes four additional analyses |
| How many have explicit numerical statistical support? | 9 associative and 1 full-set null among 10 reports | Significance applies to particular comparisons |
| What did longitudinal studies show? | Chemtob: +1.66 SD later BMI z-score; Leppänen: −2.83 cm later waist | Temporal evidence, with adjustment-sensitive findings |
| What about two-component evidence? | Crowe: ORs 1.71, 1.54, and 1.27 | Two significant comparisons and one null; sleep excluded |

**The report separates three questions.** First, what constitutes adherence? Second, which studies found an association, and which found a null result? Part 1 answers those questions in bullet points, distinguishing full adherence from individual components. Part 2 provides the requested four-column study catalog.

## Terminology and scope

**Full adherence means all three.** The Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for Children and Youth, released in 2016, integrate physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep for ages 5–17. Most obesity studies operationalized adherence using the following three time targets. The broader guidelines also encourage light activity and limiting prolonged sedentary time. [2]

| Component | Recommendation | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, or MVPA | At least 60 minutes/day | Activity that appreciably increases breathing and heart rate |
| Recreational screen time | No more than 2 hours/day | Leisure screen use, rather than all sitting |
| Sleep duration | 9–11 hours/night at ages 5–13; 8–10 at ages 14–17 | Age-appropriate sleep duration |

**Partial adherence is a different exposure.** A child meeting the activity target but exceeding the screen-time target has met one component, not the complete guidelines. Studies variously compared all three versus none, all three versus everyone else, the number of components met, or particular combinations. Those comparisons answer related—but different—questions. [3]

**The population is school-aged youth.** This report concerns school-aged children and adolescents, excluding toddler- and preschool-only evidence, and uses findings available through May 2023. Studies including 18-year-old adolescents remain relevant to the question, although that age exceeds the Canadian child/youth guideline’s stated upper boundary. In Sun et al., for example, 26 of 440 participants—5.9%—were aged 18; the authors retained them using the World Health Organization’s adolescent definition of ages 10–19. [1]

**Several obesity outcomes need distinction.** Body mass index, or BMI, relates weight to height; a BMI z-score expresses BMI relative to an age- and sex-specific reference population. Overweight/obesity categories apply specified BMI cutoffs, whereas waist circumference, body-fat percentage, and visceral fat assess different aspects of adiposity. “Adiposity” simply means body fatness; a lower waist measurement is not automatically the same finding as lower odds of BMI-defined obesity. [4]

## Part 1: Overview of research findings

### How many studies found an association?

- **The principal comparison was 11 versus 3.** Counting the distinct primary-study references described in Sun et al.’s 2023 literature discussion, together with Sun’s own study, gives **14 reports: 11 associative and 3 reporting no significant full-set association**. The 11 associative reports are Roman-Viñas, Carson, Tanaka, Hjorth, Katzmarzyk and Staiano, Laurson 2014, Laurson 2015, Zhu, Janssen, Chen, and Sun. The three full-set null reports are Shi, Jakubec, and the Chinese school-student study identified as Wang in the narrative and Yu in its reference list. [1]

- **Four additional analyses broaden the comparison.** Chemtob’s QUALITY cohort, Leppänen’s Finnish PANIC analysis, Yang’s Chinese national analysis, and Friel’s U.S. analysis also reported associations involving the three-behavior profile. Thus, the expanded publication-level count is **18 reports: 15 reporting an inverse association and 3 full-set null reports**, calculated as 14 + 4 reports and 11 + 4 associative reports. Crowe’s physical-activity/screen-time study is a nineteenth catalog entry, presented separately because its tested exposure did not include sleep. [5][6][7][8][9]

- **Numerical support has its own denominator.** Among the **10 three-behavior reports with explicit numerical obesity-related statistical results**, **9 contained at least one statistically significant inverse profile comparison**, while **1—Jakubec—reported no significant full-set association**. The nine are Roman-Viñas, Katzmarzyk and Staiano, Zhu, Chen, Sun, Chemtob, Leppänen, Yang, and Friel. The six remaining associative entries contribute reported conclusions rather than an additional set of numerical effect estimates.

- **Each publication counts only once.** Multiple outcomes, sex groups, age groups, or behavior combinations within a paper do not become separate studies. An associative classification means that at least one relevant full-set, number-of-components, or all-three-versus-partial-pattern comparison supported an inverse relationship. It does **not** mean every comparison in that paper was significant. Publications drawing on the same survey remain separate reports, not independent populations.

- **Statistical significance is comparison-specific.** Numerical findings are treated as statistically supported when the reported p-value is below 0.05, or a 95% confidence interval excludes the no-association value: 1 for an odds ratio and 0 for a regression difference. A “null” classification requires an explicitly reported nonsignificant overall-guideline result; a nonsignificant sleep-only result does not make the full-guideline study null.

- **Earlier studies are precursor evidence.** Laurson’s 2014 and 2015 studies and Hjorth’s 2014 study preceded the formal 2016 guidelines. They examined combined movement-related behaviors and informed later discussions of the framework. Their inclusion in the reported-conclusion count should therefore be distinguished from a claim that all 18 publications tested an identical modern adherence definition. [4]

### Roman-Viñas and Zhu: full adherence compared with none

- **Roman-Viñas found substantially lower obesity odds.** Roman-Viñas et al. (2016) analyzed 6,128 children aged 9–11 across 12 countries. Meeting all three recommendations rather than none was associated with an obesity odds ratio of **0.28, 95% CI 0.18–0.45**—72% lower odds, calculated as 1 − 0.28. Obesity was defined as a WHO BMI z-score above +2 standard deviations. [4]

- **The association extended to continuous BMI.** In the same study, meeting one, two, or three recommendations was associated with a significantly lower BMI z-score than meeting none. Children meeting all three had the lowest reported adjusted BMI z-scores: 0.07 among boys and −0.05 among girls. These are group BMI z-scores, not changes caused by adopting the guidelines. [4]

- **The multinational design strengthens geographic relevance.** The study included Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, Colombia, Finland, India, Kenya, Portugal, South Africa, the United Kingdom, and the United States. MVPA and sleep were accelerometer-assessed, height and weight were measured, and screen time was self-reported. Models adjusted for age, sex, parental education, and an unhealthy-diet score. The within-country samples were school-based rather than nationally representative. [4]

- **Zhu found higher odds with nonadherence.** Zhu et al. (2020), using 30,478 U.S. participants aged 10–17 from the 2016–2017 National Survey of Children’s Health, expressed the comparison in the opposite direction. Relative to meeting all three recommendations, meeting none was associated with adjusted odds ratios of **1.85 for overweight, 95% CI 1.31–2.61**, and **4.25 for obesity, 95% CI 2.87–6.31**. Parent-reported height and weight supplied CDC BMI-for-age categories. [10]

**Zhu’s component patterns were informative.** All-three adherence was the reference for the following adjusted comparisons. The table shows why the paper supports a favorable complete profile without demonstrating that every partial profile was worse. [10]

| Adherence pattern | Overweight aOR, 95% CI | Obesity aOR, 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| None | 1.85, 1.31–2.61 | 4.25, 2.87–6.31 |
| Screen time only | 1.39, 1.02–1.89 | 2.07, 1.43–3.02 |
| Sleep only | 1.51, 1.07–2.14 | 2.57, 1.71–3.86 |
| Screen time + sleep | 1.38, 1.02–1.86 | 1.85, 1.30–2.64 |
| Physical activity only | 0.81, 0.41–1.60 | 1.35, 0.59–3.13 |
| Physical activity + screen time | 0.91, 0.61–1.37 | 1.35, 0.82–2.22 |
| Physical activity + sleep | 1.48, 0.60–3.70 | 1.47, 0.58–3.74 |
| All three | 1.00, reference | 1.00, reference |

- **Activity-containing patterns were not significantly worse.** None of the three physical-activity-containing partial patterns had significantly higher obesity odds than all-three adherence. Among girls, physical activity alone was associated with *lower* overweight odds than all-three adherence: aOR 0.29, 95% CI 0.10–0.85. That exception concerns overweight in a sex-specific analysis, not a universal advantage of meeting only the activity guideline. [10]

- **Sex-specific estimates require careful comparison.** For meeting none versus all three, the obesity aOR was 4.97 among girls, 95% CI 2.59–9.53, and 3.99 among boys, 95% CI 2.49–6.40. The female point estimate was larger, but the overlapping intervals do not themselves establish a statistically significant difference between sexes. [10]

- **The studies converge in direction.** Both Roman-Viñas and Zhu associated the all-three profile with lower obesity odds than meeting none, despite different countries, age bands, and measurements. However, one used measured anthropometry and device-assessed activity/sleep, while the other used survey reports. Their odds ratios should not be interpreted as interchangeable estimates of a single effect.

- **Odds are not absolute risk.** An OR of 0.28 does not mean that 28% of adherent children had obesity, and an aOR of 4.25 does not mean that 425% had obesity. These are relative odds contrasts; they do not supply an individual child’s probability of obesity or the probability reduction from changing behavior.

**The resulting judgment is consistent but specific:** full adherence compared favorably with meeting none, while activity-containing partial patterns showed that the apparent advantage was not uniform across every alternative.

### Chen: a national association with age and sex differences

- **The overall Chinese association was significant.** Chen et al., published online in 2020 and in the 2021 journal issue, analyzed 114,072 students in grades 4–12 from the 2017 Youth Study in China. Compared with meeting all three recommendations, meeting none was associated with overweight/obesity OR **1.25, 95% CI 1.16–1.34**, after adjustment for demographic and socioeconomic factors. Height and weight were measured, and WHO weight-status categories were used. [11]

- **The sample was large but heterogeneous.** Mean age was 13.75 years, 22.44% were classified as overweight/obese, and 5.12% met all three recommendations; the corresponding survey-weighted percentages were 23.79% and 6.04%. The study detected an interaction involving sex and age group and therefore presented subgroup models. Those subgroup estimates are more informative than describing one national association as equally applicable to every adolescent. [11]

**The subgroup contrast was uneven.** The following comparisons all use full adherence as the reference and compare it with meeting none. [11]

| Population subgroup | None versus all-three OR | 95% CI | Statistical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boys, grades 4–6 | 1.22 | 1.06–1.40 | Significant |
| Girls, grades 4–6 | 1.35 | 1.14–1.59 | Significant |
| Boys, grades 7–9 | 1.10 | 0.91–1.33 | Nonsignificant |
| Girls, grades 7–9 | 1.30 | 1.01–1.67 | Significant |
| Boys, grades 10–12 | 1.19 | 0.80–1.75 | Nonsignificant |
| Girls, grades 10–12 | 2.36 | 0.95–5.85 | Nonsignificant |

- **Younger children showed additional pattern differences.** Among boys in grades 4–6, screen-time-only adherence had OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.01–1.28, and sleep-only adherence had OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.03–1.28, relative to all three. Among girls in grades 4–6, sleep only had OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.08–1.39, and MVPA plus sleep had OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.01–1.54. [11]

- **The numerical table resolves a wording conflict.** The discussion highlights boys in grades 4–6 and girls in grades 7–9, but the abstract and Table 5 also show a significant none-versus-all comparison among girls in grades 4–6. Consequently, the supported summary includes all three significant subgroups, rather than repeating the discussion’s narrower description. [11]

- **Several full-sample partial patterns were null.** Relative to all-three adherence, MVPA alone had OR 1.14, 95% CI 0.99–1.29; MVPA plus screen time had OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.92–1.13; and screen time plus sleep had OR 1.04, 95% CI 0.97–1.11. Conversely, screen time alone, sleep alone, and MVPA plus sleep had significantly higher overweight/obesity odds, with ORs 1.20, 1.15, and 1.12, respectively. [11]

- **Adolescent nulls constrain the conclusion.** The nonsignificant estimates for older groups are part of the study’s answer, not separate studies to add to the null count. They show that a significant national result can coexist with less precise or nonsignificant age-specific comparisons. The especially wide interval among older girls also cautions against treating its large point estimate as a settled magnitude.

- **Diet remains an alternative explanation.** Chen’s models included several family and socioeconomic factors, but not detailed diet and nutrition. Family circumstances could influence both movement opportunities and weight status, and dietary differences could partly account for the observed association. The paper itself identifies these factors when interpreting its findings. [11]

**Chen’s study supports an inverse national association, with the clearest subgroup evidence in younger pupils and girls in grades 7–9—not a uniform finding across all adolescents.**

### Sun: a graded association that depends on the comparator

- **More guidelines corresponded to lower odds.** Sun et al. (2023) studied 440 children and adolescents aged 7–18 in seven Chinese cities, using accelerometers for physical activity and questionnaires for screen time and sleep. In the number-of-guidelines model, meeting two or three recommendations was associated with significantly lower overweight/obesity odds than meeting none; meeting one had a favorable but nonsignificant estimate. The trend test was significant at p = 0.006. [1]

| Number of guidelines met | OR versus none | 95% CI | Statistical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1.00 | Reference | Reference |
| 1 | 0.51 | 0.22–1.17 | Nonsignificant |
| 2 | 0.32 | 0.13–0.77 | Significant |
| 3 | 0.23 | 0.07–0.81 | Significant |

- **The two full-adherence estimates use different models.** The number-of-guidelines model gave OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.07–0.81, for three versus none. A separate model distinguishing particular behavior combinations gave OR 0.25, 95% CI 0.07–0.87, for all three versus none. Both favor full adherence, but 0.23 and 0.25 are not competing transcriptions of one result. [1]

**Screen-containing patterns drove several significant contrasts.** In the combination model, all patterns below were compared with meeting none. The reported values are odds ratios, not percentages of children with overweight/obesity. [1]

| Adherence pattern | OR versus none | 95% CI | Statistical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| MVPA only | 0.38 | 0.04–3.77 | Nonsignificant |
| Screen time only | 0.39 | 0.15–0.99 | Significant |
| Sleep only | 0.73 | 0.29–1.85 | Nonsignificant |
| MVPA + screen time | 0.15 | 0.03–0.78 | Significant |
| MVPA + sleep | 0.56 | 0.09–3.56 | Nonsignificant |
| Screen time + sleep | 0.36 | 0.15–0.89 | Significant |
| All three | 0.25 | 0.07–0.87 | Significant |

- **A broader full-set comparison was null.** The appendix also compared participants not meeting all three with those meeting all three, pooling the various partial-adherence groups together. That contrast was nonsignificant: OR **1.74, 95% CI 0.66–4.60**, for non-full versus full adherence. Thus, the study’s significant all-three-versus-none finding should not be restated as a significant difference between all-three adherers and *every other child combined*. [12]

- **The reference group explains the distinction.** “Meeting none” identifies a relatively unfavorable extreme, whereas “not meeting all three” combines children meeting zero, one, or two recommendations. Pooling those different profiles can reduce the contrast with full adherence. This is a substantive difference in the question being asked, not simply a disagreement between tables.

- **Sex-stratified results were also mixed.** In the number-of-guidelines analysis, boys meeting three rather than none had OR 0.10, 95% CI 0.02–0.53, with trend p = 0.002. Girls had OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.11–6.42, with trend p = 0.45. Only eight girls met all three recommendations, compared with 24 boys, making the female comparison especially imprecise. [12]

- **Separate component tests contained further nulls.** In the appendix’s No-versus-Yes component comparisons, MVPA had OR 2.03, 95% CI 0.93–4.03, and sleep had OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.56–1.72; both were nonsignificant in the total sample. Screen-time nonadherence was associated with higher overweight/obesity odds: OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.35–3.99. The MVPA comparison was significant among boys, OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.02–5.09, illustrating why a total-sample component null is not necessarily a null in every subgroup. [12]

- **A gradient does not prove synergy.** The downward trend supports considering the whole behavior profile. It does not establish that each additional component produces an equal benefit, or that the three components interact biologically to produce an effect greater than their separate effects. Those are stronger claims than a cross-sectional number-of-components trend demonstrates.

**Sun provides significant extreme-group and dose–response findings, alongside a null full-versus-all-other-profiles comparison and several component-level nulls.**

### Yang: dietary adjustment changes the conclusion

- **The fully adjusted patterns favored full adherence.** Yang et al. (2022) analyzed 34,887 Chinese participants aged 6–17 from 94 schools. After adjustment for demographic, family, and dietary variables, MVPA-only and screen-time-only patterns remained associated with higher overweight/obesity odds than all-three adherence: OR **1.30, 95% CI 1.06–1.60**, and OR **1.32, 95% CI 1.06–1.64**, respectively. [7]

- **The none-versus-all result was adjustment-sensitive.** Before dietary adjustment, meeting none had OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.03–1.54, p = 0.026, relative to all three. After adding dietary variables, the estimate was 1.22, 95% CI 1.00–1.49, p = 0.053. The reported p-value places that latter contrast above the study’s 0.05 significance threshold, despite the rounded confidence interval beginning at 1.00. [7]

**The shared-axis comparison shows the change.** All-three adherence is the reference throughout. Model 1 adjusts for demographic and family characteristics; Model 2 adds dietary variables. [7]

| Adherence pattern | Model 1 OR, 95% CI | Model 1 p | Diet-adjusted OR, 95% CI | Model 2 p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| None | 1.26, 1.03–1.54 | 0.026 | 1.22, 1.00–1.49 | 0.053 |
| MVPA only | 1.33, 1.09–1.64 | 0.006 | 1.30, 1.06–1.60 | 0.011 |
| Screen time only | 1.35, 1.09–1.67 | 0.006 | 1.32, 1.06–1.64 | 0.012 |
| Sleep only | 1.18, 0.96–1.45 | 0.114 | 1.15, 0.94–1.41 | 0.186 |
| MVPA + screen time | 1.27, 1.00–1.61 | 0.052 | 1.25, 0.98–1.59 | 0.068 |
| MVPA + sleep | 1.24, 1.01–1.53 | 0.043 | 1.22, 0.99–1.50 | 0.067 |
| Screen time + sleep | 1.02, 0.80–1.30 | 0.865 | 1.00, 0.79–1.27 | 0.999 |

- **The measurements strengthen the weight-status comparison.** Trained technicians measured height and weight twice and averaged the measurements; overweight/obesity categories used International Obesity Task Force age- and sex-specific BMI cutoffs. Movement behaviors were reported jointly by students and parents. Only 2.1% met all three recommendations, and 22.1% were classified as overweight/obese. [7]

- **Diet is more than a background detail.** The loss of conventional significance for none-versus-all and MVPA-plus-sleep-versus-all shows that conclusions depend partly on which correlated behaviors enter the model. A family’s activity, sleep, screen habits, and eating patterns may cluster together. The results support an association between particular movement profiles and weight status, while giving weaker support to an association independent of diet for every profile contrast.

- **The study still belongs among associative reports.** Its final model retained significant all-three-versus-partial-pattern findings, even though its none-versus-all comparison became nonsignificant. Under the stated counting rule, that is an associative study with mixed comparisons—not a study in which every full-profile contrast succeeded.

- **The practical inference is targeted.** Meeting only the activity recommendation did not reproduce the weight-status pattern of meeting all three in this population. Equally, the data do not show that exceeding one threshold inevitably produces overweight: several other partial patterns were statistically similar to the complete profile.

**Yang supports particular all-three-versus-partial-profile associations, while demonstrating why dietary adjustment and the chosen comparison must accompany the headline.**

### Katzmarzyk and Staiano: evidence beyond BMI

- **More components accompanied lower measured adiposity.** Katzmarzyk and Staiano (2017) studied 357 White and African American participants aged 5–18 in Baton Rouge, Louisiana. Across groups meeting zero through three components, adjusted mean BMI declined from 24.4 to 20.5 kg/m² and waist circumference from 79.2 to 69.2 cm; both trend tests had p = 0.002. Total fat and abdominal fat measures showed the same direction. [13]

**Direct fat measures corroborated the pattern.** Total fat was measured by DXA, and subcutaneous and visceral abdominal fat by MRI. These adjusted group means provide a broader picture than BMI alone. [13]

| Components met, group n | BMI, kg/m² | Waist, cm | Total fat, kg | Subcutaneous fat, L | Visceral fat, L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0, n = 96 | 24.4 | 79.2 | 19.3 | 5.11 | 0.18 |
| 1, n = 130 | 23.2 | 76.3 | 17.4 | 4.51 | 0.16 |
| 2, n = 101 | 22.3 | 73.9 | 14.8 | 3.52 | 0.14 |
| 3, n = 30 | 20.5 | 69.2 | 11.9 | 2.59 | 0.09 |

- **The fat-related trends were statistically supported.** The p-values for trends were 0.0005 for total fat, 0.0004 for subcutaneous fat, and 0.006 for visceral fat. The calculated zero-versus-three group differences were 3.9 kg/m² in BMI and 10.0 cm in waist circumference. These are between-group contrasts, not expected reductions for a child who changes from zero to three components. [13]

- **Binary obesity and individual components differed.** Obesity was defined as BMI at or above the CDC 95th percentile. The trend in obesity across the number of components met was significant, p = 0.03. Of the three separately examined component associations, only sleep reached statistical significance: OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.37–0.95. [13]

- **Its adherence definition was distinctive.** Physical activity required at least 60 minutes/day on at least five days/week, rather than the every-day rule used in several national surveys. Television viewing of no more than two hours/day represented screen time, and sleep targets were extended through age 18. These details matter when comparing its graded results with studies using total recreational device time or different activity rules. [13]

- **The broader outcomes strengthen one inference.** Because measured total and regional fat followed the BMI pattern, the association was not confined to a BMI classification. However, the study’s group differences still represent a favorable behavioral profile associated with lower fatness, rather than a demonstrated treatment effect.

**This study supports a graded association across several directly measured adiposity outcomes, not merely lower BMI.**

### Longitudinal findings: Chemtob and Leppänen

- **Chemtob linked childhood adherence with later adiposity.** Chemtob et al.’s QUALITY cohort analysis, published in the 2021 volume after an online 2020 record, followed an at-risk youth cohort through childhood and adolescence. Meeting none rather than all three components in childhood was associated with **1.66 SD higher BMI z-score in early adolescence, 95% CI 0.42–2.89**. The study also reported associations between fewer childhood components met and higher adiposity at later visits, two and seven years afterward. [5]

**Adherence declined across the cohort visits.** These are visit-specific sample sizes and adherence percentages, not counts of separate studies. [5]

| Cohort visit | Age band | Participants | Meeting all three |
|---|---|---|---|
| Childhood | 8–10 years | 630 | 14% |
| Early adolescence | 10–12 years | 564 | 6% |
| Adolescence | 15–17 years | 377 | 0% |

- **The outcomes went beyond BMI.** QUALITY clinically assessed BMI z-score, waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio, and percent body fat. Fewer components met were associated cross-sectionally with higher adiposity at each visit. The prospective example above is specifically a later BMI z-score difference; it is not an obesity odds ratio or a 1.66 kg/m² BMI difference. [5]

- **Timing improves the interpretation.** Childhood behavior was assessed before later adiposity, which addresses the immediate temporal ambiguity of a one-time survey. Nevertheless, the at-risk cohort and declining follow-up sample are important to its practical meaning. Also, because no participants met all three at the last visit, the study does not provide a contemporaneous all-three-versus-none adolescent comparison at that visit.

- **Leppänen found a smaller later waist.** Leppänen et al. (2022) analyzed 485 Finnish children aged 6–8 at baseline in the PANIC study. Full adherence was associated with **2.87 cm lower baseline waist circumference**, 95% CI −3.92 to −1.82, and **2.83 cm lower waist circumference two years later**, 95% CI −4.23 to −1.43; both results had p < 0.001 in the reported adjusted models. [6]

- **Graded waist comparisons supported the association.** At baseline, children meeting all three had waist circumference 3.87 cm lower than those meeting zero or one, 95% CI −5.76 to −1.99, and 2.63 cm lower than those meeting two, 95% CI −3.74 to −1.52. These comparisons supported a favorable complete profile before further adjustment for body-fat percentage. [6]

- **Activity-containing combinations showed the clearest pattern.** At two-year follow-up, baseline MVPA adherence was associated with 4.86 cm lower waist circumference, 95% CI −6.57 to −3.16. MVPA plus screen-time adherence was associated with 2.99 cm lower waist, 95% CI −4.41 to −1.57, and MVPA plus sleep with 3.65 cm lower waist, 95% CI −5.18 to −2.11. [6]

**The non-activity comparisons were null.** The following waist-circumference estimates distinguish individual-component and pairwise nulls from the initial full-set finding. [6]

| Adherence exposure | Baseline waist difference, 95% CI | Two-year waist difference, 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| Screen-time component | −0.70 cm, −1.94 to 0.53 | −0.68 cm, −2.31 to 0.95 |
| Sleep component | −1.19 cm, −3.02 to 0.64 | −1.13 cm, −3.52 to 1.26 |
| Screen time + sleep | −0.96 cm, −2.11 to 0.19 | −1.04 cm, −2.55 to 0.47 |

- **Body-fat adjustment changed the waist result.** The full-set and physical-activity-containing waist associations became nonsignificant after further adjustment for body-fat percentage, p > 0.05, in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. This means that the evidence for a waist association *independent of overall body fat* was weaker than the initial association. It does not, by itself, determine whether body fat was a mediator, a confounder, or an overlapping measure of the same adiposity process. [6]

- **The two cohorts answer different questions.** QUALITY followed an at-risk cohort from childhood into adolescence and reported later standardized BMI and other adiposity outcomes. PANIC studied younger Finnish children over two years, with the clearest reported obesity-related association involving waist circumference. The latter combined intervention and control participants, adjusted for study group, and did not provide the same kind of binary overweight/obesity effect estimate. [5][6]

- **Longitudinal evidence improves temporal confidence.** These results make a purely same-day explanation less satisfactory, but they do not settle causation. Confidence in an independent effect would be stronger if comparable associations consistently persisted after prespecified baseline-adiposity and confounder adjustment; Leppänen’s attenuation shows why that condition matters.

**The prospective findings support a temporal association, while the Finnish adjustment analysis prevents treating that evidence as uniformly robust across models.**

### Friel: higher BMI categories were associated with lower adherence

- **Friel found lower adherence with higher BMI.** Friel et al. (2020) analyzed the 2016–2017 U.S. National Survey of Children’s Health, completing the analysis in 2019. The full sample contained 49,606 children aged 6–17, while BMI-category analyses were restricted to ages 10–17. Compared with normal-weight participants, the adjusted odds of meeting all three guidelines were **0.65 for overweight, 95% CI 0.44–0.94**, and **0.57 for obesity, 95% CI 0.38–0.86**. [8]

| BMI category, ages 10–17 | Unweighted participants | All-three adherence | Adjusted odds of all-three adherence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal weight | 22,903 | 6.7% | 1.00, reference |
| Overweight | 4,888 | 4.6% | 0.65, 95% CI 0.44–0.94 |
| Obesity | 4,386 | 4.3% | 0.57, 95% CI 0.38–0.86 |

- **Each component showed the same direction.** For obesity versus normal weight, adjusted adherence ORs were 0.52 for physical activity, 95% CI 0.41–0.66; 0.60 for screen time, 95% CI 0.48–0.73; and 0.64 for sleep, 95% CI 0.52–0.78. Overweight participants likewise had lower component-adherence odds. [8]

- **The model’s direction is important.** Unlike Zhu, Friel modeled adherence as the outcome and BMI category as the predictor. The association is relevant to the user’s question, but the OR of 0.57 is an estimate of lower *adherence odds among participants with obesity*, not an estimate of obesity odds among adherers. Reversing those descriptions would change the meaning.

- **The U.S. reports are not independent replications.** Friel and Zhu used the same survey years but different analytic samples and operational definitions. For example, Friel summed television and electronic-device screen use, whereas Zhu’s screen-time measure did not include mobile-phone or computer use. Their results therefore support a consistent relationship within related data, not two wholly independent national cohorts. [8][10]

**Friel adds statistically supported evidence of the relationship, but its adherence-outcome model should not be presented as an obesity-prevention effect.**

### Six additional reports contributing associative conclusions

- **Carson reported lower BMI and waist.** Carson et al. (2017, also indexed under a 2016 record) studied Canadian children and youth aged 6–17. Meeting a greater number of movement-guideline components was associated with lower BMI z-score and lower waist circumference. Its contribution is a graded anthropometric association: more components met corresponded to a leaner profile, rather than an isolated activity-only finding. [14]

- **Tanaka reported favorable Japanese weight status.** Tanaka et al. (2020) examined Japanese primary-school children. Sun’s literature discussion identifies this study as showing the lowest overweight/obesity rate or BMI z-score among those meeting all three recommendations and a significant favorable association for meeting two recommendations. The supported conclusion is that both complete and selected partial profiles were associated with more favorable weight status; it is not evidence that every two-component combination performed equally well. [1]

- **Hjorth supplied earlier combined-behavior evidence.** Hjorth et al. (2014) studied Danish children aged 8–11 in a longitudinal investigation of low activity, short sleep, and cardiometabolic risk. Sun places the study among reports in which the favorable combined profile corresponded to the lowest overweight/obesity rate or BMI z-score. Its role here is precursor evidence about the joint behavior profile, rather than a post-2016 evaluation of identical guideline cutoffs. [1]

- **Laurson’s childhood study showed a gradient.** Laurson et al. (2014), *Concurrent Associations between Physical Activity, Screen Time, and Sleep Duration with Childhood Obesity*, reported a significant dose–response relationship between the number of favorable recommendations met and childhood obesity. More recommendations met corresponded to a lower obesity rate. Its concurrent design and combined-behavior exposure support the graded-association conclusion, not a demonstrated reduction following a behavior change. [15]

- **Laurson’s adolescent study reached a similar conclusion.** Laurson et al. (2015), *The Cumulative Impact of Physical Activity, Sleep Duration, and Television Time on Adolescent Obesity*, used the 2011 U.S. Youth Risk Behavior Survey. Sun identifies it among studies in which meeting all three corresponded to the lowest obesity-related outcome. It is a separate adolescent report from Laurson’s childhood paper and contributes evidence about cumulative behavior patterns. [16]

- **Janssen’s health analysis was associative.** Janssen, Roberts, and Thompson (2017), *Is adherence to the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Behaviour Guidelines for Children and Youth associated with improved indicators of physical, mental, and social health?*, is cited by Sun for a significant dose–response relationship with overweight/obesity and favorable adiposity indicators as more components were met. This is the health-association publication, DOI 10.1139/apnm-2016-0681. [12]

- **The similarly named surveillance paper is different.** Janssen et al.’s *Adherence to the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines among 10- to 17-year-old Canadians* reports adherence prevalence and combinations. It is useful for defining the guidelines but does not contribute an obesity-association or obesity-null result to this count. The same first author and year should not lead to substituting one paper for the other. [2]

- **These reports broaden rather than quantify.** Together, the six entries extend the favorable direction across countries, ages, and earlier combined-behavior research. Their reported conclusions belong in the broader 11-of-14 and 15-of-18 tallies, while the separate 9-of-10 numerical tally identifies the stronger, explicitly quantified statistical basis.

**The six additional reports support the reported direction of the evidence without supplying a common effect size that can be pooled or assigned to an individual child.**

### The three studies reporting no significant full-set association

- **Jakubec’s complete-profile finding was null.** Jakubec et al. (2020) studied 679 Czech participants aged 8–18, separating children aged 8–13 from adolescents aged 14–18. Meeting all three recommendations versus none was not significantly associated with high BMI z-score: children had OR **0.33, 95% CI 0.07–1.54, p = 0.158**, and adolescents had OR **1.27, 95% CI 0.23–7.16, p = 0.787**. The paper reported no overall full-set association with its adiposity indicators. [17]

- **The estimates were imprecise, not equivalent.** The child point estimate favored adherence, while the adolescent estimate lay above 1, but both confidence intervals crossed 1. The adolescent interval allowed associations in either direction and covered a particularly broad range. Therefore, the supported statement is “no statistically significant association,” not “the groups had identical obesity risk.”

- **Full adherence was rare.** Only 6.5% of children and 2.2% of adolescents met all three recommendations. That small adherent fraction helps explain the wide intervals and is consistent with low statistical precision. It does not demonstrate that a larger study would necessarily have found a favorable association. [17]

**Jakubec nevertheless found component associations.** Among adolescents, screen-time-only adherence was associated with lower odds of three adiposity outcomes. [17]

| Adolescent adiposity outcome | Screen-time-only OR | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| High BMI z-score | 0.38 | 0.17–0.89 |
| Excess body-fat percentage | 0.34 | 0.13–0.93 |
| Excess visceral adipose tissue | 0.27 | 0.10–0.74 |

- **The sleep combination also differed.** Screen time plus sleep was associated with lower odds of high BMI z-score in adolescents: OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.01–0.89. No significant associations were found in the child subgroup. These results coexist with the paper’s full-set null; they do not replace it with a positive all-three result. [17]

- **Shi reported a Hong Kong full-set null.** Shi et al. (2020), *Compliance With 24-Hour Movement Guidelines in Hong Kong Adolescents: Associations With Weight Status*, reported no significant relationship between meeting the overall three-component guidelines and overweight/obesity. Sun separately describes a significant favorable two-guideline finding from the Hong Kong study. Consequently, Shi is counted once as a full-set null, while its partial-combination result is retained as a distinct exposure-level finding. [1]

- **Wang’s Chinese school-student result was null.** The 2021 study *Levels of 24-hour movement and associations with childhood obesity in Chinese school students* is identified as Wang et al. in Yang’s reference list and Sun’s narrative, but as Yu et al. in Sun’s reference list. Its reported conclusion was no association between meeting the overall guidelines and overweight/obesity, while adequate sleep was associated with a favorable weight-status pattern. These names refer to one report in the count, not two. [1][7]

- **A full-set null does not invalidate components.** Jakubec, Shi, and Wang illustrate the same interpretive point: the complete-profile result and the component results must be classified separately. A paper can fail to establish an all-three association while still detecting a relationship involving screen time, sleep, or a selected pair.

- **A null is not proof of absence.** In Jakubec, the confidence intervals themselves demonstrate substantial uncertainty. Conversely, positive component findings are not grounds for dismissing its null full-set result as merely a sample-size problem. Both are observed findings that constrain a universal protective claim.

**The three full-set null reports prevent the conclusion that adherence was consistently associated with lower overweight/obesity in every eligible population.**

### Crowe: what a two-component comparison adds

- **Crowe found an activity-related pattern.** Crowe et al. (2020) analyzed 9,913 Ontario students in grades 7–12. Relative to adolescents meeting both the activity and screen-time recommendations, those below the activity target had higher overweight/obesity odds whether they met the screen-time target or not. Adolescents meeting the activity target but exceeding the screen-time target did not differ significantly from the reference group. [9]

| Activity/screen-time profile | Share of sample | Adjusted overweight/obesity OR | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Active + within screen target | 11.1% | 1.00 | Reference |
| Inactive + within screen target | 23.7% | 1.54 | 1.20–1.97 |
| Active + above screen target | 12.1% | 1.27 | 0.88–1.83 |
| Inactive + above screen target | 53.1% | 1.71 | 1.26–2.32 |

- **Its null concerns a particular alternative.** The active/high-screen group’s OR of 1.27 had a confidence interval crossing 1. Therefore, it is too strong to say that the group meeting both recommendations was statistically leaner than *every* other combination. The two inactive comparisons were significant; the active/high-screen comparison was not. [9]

- **Sleep was outside the tested exposure.** Although the paper discussed sleep-related characteristics, its four-category exposure combined MVPA and screen time. It therefore contributes evidence about two components, not adherence to the complete 24-hour set. Its two significant and one null comparisons are within one study and are not three studies to add to the headline count.

- **Its terminology also needs precision.** “Sedentary” in these profile labels referred to exceeding two hours/day of screen time. A student could satisfy that screen-time target while still spending substantial time sitting in class or studying. Screen-time adherence is therefore not equivalent to having low total sedentary time.

- **The contrast with Sun is informative.** Crowe’s significant differences centered on insufficient activity; Sun found several significant screen-containing patterns. Leppänen’s waist findings also favored activity-containing combinations, while Jakubec found adolescent screen-time associations. These differences argue against selecting one universally dominant component from this evidence alone.

**Crowe supports joint activity/screen assessment, but its results should neither inflate the full-guideline count nor be described as a three-component test.**

### What the comparisons mean for a parent

- **The whole-day interpretation is reasonable.** Physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep compete within a fixed day. Replacing recreational screen time could create time for activity or sleep, and increased activity could alter both energy expenditure and sleep patterns. However, the three frequently tested guideline indicators—MVPA, recreational screen time, and sleep—are not themselves an exhaustive partition of all 24 hours: light activity and non-screen sitting also occupy time. [18]

- **Several mechanisms are plausible, not demonstrated.** Zhu discusses activity-related energy expenditure and family or school contexts that promote habitual activity. Jakubec proposes that reducing screen time might reallocate time toward sleep. Those explanations are compatible with the observed associations, but the adherence studies do not identify how much of their weight-status relationship each pathway caused. [10][17]

- **Confounding remains a competing explanation.** More favorable movement profiles can coexist with differences in diet, household resources, parental support, and access to activity. Chen found that parental education and family income were associated with adherence, and Yang’s obesity contrasts changed after dietary adjustment. Thus, part of the adherence–weight association could reflect a wider family or environmental pattern rather than the isolated effect of crossing three time thresholds. [11][7]

- **Reverse direction is also plausible.** A child’s existing adiposity may affect willingness or opportunity to be active, while health concerns may prompt changes in screen time or sleep. Friel’s model explicitly treats BMI category as a predictor of adherence, making clear that the same cross-sectional relationship can be described from either direction. A statistical association alone does not establish which came first.

- **More recommendations need not mean equal increments.** A score from zero to three gives each recommendation one point, but the behaviors differ biologically and in how far participants fall above or below their thresholds. Two children meeting two recommendations may have quite different daily routines. A significant trend across the score therefore supports a graded profile relationship, not a fixed amount of weight benefit for each extra point.

- **Adolescent findings deserve separate attention.** Chen’s older subgroups and Sun’s girls’ subgroup contained nonsignificant full-set comparisons, whereas Zhu found a substantial association in U.S. youth aged 10–17. The differences could reflect populations, exposure definitions, reference groups, or precision. They do not justify concluding that the guidelines matter for children but not adolescents, or for boys but not girls.

- **Thresholds should not become guarantees.** Meeting all three recommendations is a useful whole-day behavioral goal, but this evidence does not imply that every adherent child will have a lower BMI, or that a child with overweight must be nonadherent. Nor does a favorable activity finding imply that sleep can safely be sacrificed to increase exercise. The integrated framework is about supporting the daily profile, not using one behavior to cancel another.

- **The evidence favors support over prediction.** For a parent, the most defensible interpretation is to encourage activity, reasonable recreational screen limits, and age-appropriate sleep together. The studies support that combined direction more strongly than they support a numerical promise about a particular child’s future weight.

**Overall, the evidence supports the guidelines as a favorable behavioral profile, while leaving the size and causal independence of its obesity relationship less settled.**

**Additional documented evidence**

**Results represented in the eligible youth evidence**

Across the permitted primary reports represented here, meeting all three movement recommendations was associated with lower adiposity in the 12-country ISCOLE study and in a Chinese primary-school sample, whereas a U.S. adolescent study found no statistically significant binary association with overweight/obesity but did find a significant trend across the number of recommendations met.[53][43][46] These findings are associations, not proof that adherence itself causes a lower weight outcome.

**How adherence was defined**

- The **24-hour Movement Guidelines** combine physical activity—especially moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA)—sedentary or recreational screen behaviour, and sleep across the whole day. The Canadian version for children and youth aged **5–17 years** recommends at least **60 minutes/day of MVPA**, no more than **2 hours/day of recreational screen time**, and **9–11 hours/night of sleep for ages 5–13** or **8–10 hours/night for ages 14–17**; it also recommends several hours of light activity and regular vigorous, muscle- and bone-strengthening activity.[25]

- In this overview, **full adherence** means meeting all three domains simultaneously. Analyses of MVPA, screen time, sleep, one- or two-component combinations, or the number of recommendations met are reported separately and are not automatically equivalent to full-guideline adherence. For example, the Chinese primary-school study examined individual components, combinations, the number of recommendations met, and all three recommendations together.[43]

- “Association” means that the study’s statistical model found a relationship at its stated significance threshold; it does not mean that the guidelines caused the observed BMI, overweight, or obesity result. The official Canadian guidance itself uses the wording “associated with better body composition,” rather than claiming a guaranteed outcome.[25]

**Studies reporting lower overweight/obesity or adiposity**

- **The 12-country ISCOLE study (2016; 6,128 children aged 9–11 years):** full adherence was defined as **9–11 hours/night of sleep, no more than 2 hours/day of screen time, and at least 60 minutes/day of MVPA**. Sleep and MVPA were measured by accelerometry, screen time was self-reported, and height and weight were measured. BMI z-scores used age- and sex-specific WHO reference data; obesity was defined as **BMI z-score greater than +2 standard deviations**.[53] Only **7%** of children met all three recommendations, although adherence reached **15% in Australia and Canada**.[53]

  - Children meeting all three recommendations had lower odds of obesity than children meeting none: **OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.18–0.45**. Meeting the recommendations was also significantly associated with a lower BMI z-score.[53]
  - The analysis adjusted for age, sex, highest parental education, and unhealthy diet-pattern score, and used generalized linear mixed models for obesity odds and linear mixed models for BMI z-scores.[53]
  - The study therefore provides a statistically significant, inverse association between full adherence and both a categorical obesity outcome and a continuous adiposity indicator. However, its cross-sectional design does not establish temporal order or causation.[53]

- **Chinese primary-school sample (Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China; 978 participants; mean age 9.1 ± 1.4 years; data collected 1 May–15 July 2021):** meeting all three Canadian recommendations was associated with lower overweight/obesity likelihood: **OR 0.63, p = 0.008**, compared with not meeting all three.[43] The study used random stratified sampling from four public primary schools in four major districts.[43]

  - The study operationalized full adherence as **at least 60 minutes of MVPA on each of the 7 reported days**, daily screen time of **no more than 2 hours**, and age-specific sleep. Its extract reports **9–11 hours for ages 6–13** and **8–10 hours for ages 14–17**; the official Canadian page uses the broader **5–13** band for the 9–11-hour recommendation, so these coding details should not be silently treated as identical.[25][43]
  - The reported outcomes included weight status or overweight/obesity, percent body fat (PBF), fat-free mass (FFM), and skeletal muscle mass (SMM). Meeting the behavioural guidelines was associated with better weight status and lower PBF, but not with FFM or SMM.[43]
  - A dose-response analysis using the **number of recommendations met**, rather than only the all-three contrast, found **OR 0.78, p = 0.01** for weight status and **B = −0.85, p = 0.001** for PBF.[43] Those estimates should be kept separate from the all-three estimate of OR 0.63.
  - Component-specific analyses also found an inverse association between physical activity and PBF, a positive association between screen time and overweight/obesity risk and FFM, and no association between sleep and the reported health indicators.[43] These component results help explain possible patterns but are not interchangeable with the full-guideline result.
  - The supplied extract reports the mean age but not the youngest and oldest participant ages or the precise overweight/obesity cutoff. Those fields therefore remain **not reported in the available extract**, rather than being inferred from “primary-school children.”[43]

**Null, mixed, or model-dependent findings in target-age samples**

- **U.S. National Survey of Children’s Health study of adolescents with ADHD (2018–2019; 634 adolescents aged 10–17 years):** full movement-guideline adherence was defined using physical activity, sedentary screen time, and sleep. The study coded the components as meeting or not meeting the recommendation; **6.5%** met all three, while partial categories included physical activity only, sleep only, screen time only, and two of three.[45][46]

  - The outcome used proxy-reported height and weight to calculate age- and sex-specific BMI. **Overweight or obesity was defined as BMI at or above the 85th percentile**.[46]
  - The primary binary regression analyses found **no statistically significant association** between the number of recommendations met and the odds of being overweight or obese, and none of the specific combinations had a statistically significant association.[46]
  - The study nevertheless reported a significant trend across the number of recommendations met: **OR 0.79, p for trend = 0.04**.[46] Thus, it is not uniformly null across every analysis. Under a classification that considers only the prespecified binary overweight/obesity contrasts, it is a null result; under a rule counting any significant full-pattern trend, it is **mixed or model-dependent**.
  - The study adjusted for age, sex, ADHD severity, medication status, race, household income, and highest household education.[46] Its cross-sectional design and proxy-reported height and weight leave potential measurement error, residual confounding, and reverse causality.

- The U.S. study also used sleep coding of **8–10 hours for ages 14–17** and **at least 9 hours for ages 10–13**, rather than reproducing the official Canadian 9–11-hour range for all ages 5–13.[25][46] This illustrates why studies using the label “24-hour guidelines” cannot be compared without recording their exact thresholds.

**Target-age evidence that does not yet establish an obesity association**

- **Eight- to 12-year-old study using the Canadian guidelines:** the available extract describes full adherence using accelerometer-measured physical activity and self-reported recreational screen time and sleep. The thresholds were **at least 60 minutes/day of MVPA, 9–11 hours/night of sleep, and no more than 2 hours/day of recreational screen time**.[50] The extract does not report an overweight, obesity, BMI, waist, or other adiposity association, so it cannot be assigned a positive or null obesity classification from the supplied evidence.[50]

- **Kenyan school study:** the Kenya-LINX sample included **539 children**, with mean age **11.1 ± 0.8 years**, recruited from eight urban and eight rural schools. Physical activity and sleep were estimated from wrist accelerometers, whereas screen time was self-reported.[51] Combined guideline adherence was **7% overall**, **10% among rural children**, and **5% among urban children**; individual physical-activity adherence was **76% in rural children versus 60% in urban children**, and sleep adherence was **27% versus 14%**.[51] The odds of combined adherence declined with age (**OR 0.55, 95% CI 0.35–0.87**), and were higher among children who could swim (**OR 3.27, 95% CI 1.09–9.83**) or did not use screen media before school (**OR 4.40, 95% CI 1.81–10.68**).[51] These are adherence correlates; the supplied extract does not report an obesity outcome, so this study does not contribute a positive or null overweight/obesity count.

- **Spanish repeated cross-sectional study:** the pre-pandemic sample contained **844 students** with mean age **13.12 ± 0.86 years** and data from March–June 2018; the later sample contained **501 students** with mean age **14.39 ± 1.16 years** and data from February–March 2021.[51] Adherence to all three recommendations fell from **3.3% before the pandemic to 0.2% one year later**, while adherence to none rose from **28.9% to 66.3%**.[51] The reported outcomes concerned academic performance rather than overweight/obesity, so these adherence changes do not enter the requested obesity association counts.

- A citation for **“Meeting 24-hour movement guidelines: Their relationships with overweight and obesity among Chinese children with autism spectrum disorder”** establishes a potentially relevant obesity outcome, but the supplied evidence gives no estimate, p-value, direction, or verified age range.[51] It therefore remains **unclear or pending verification**, not a positive or null study in the count.

**Component-only and citation-only evidence**

- **Riso et al. (2016)** examined objectively measured physical activity and sedentary time in **7–9-year-old Estonian schoolchildren** and related those individual behaviours to body-composition parameters. It is component-level evidence, not a documented full three-component adherence analysis.[43]

- **Stoner et al. (2018)** examined sleep and adiposity in preadolescent children, including social jetlag, but the supplied extract does not establish full 24-hour guideline adherence.[43]

- **Nam et al. (2017)** examined sleep duration in Korean adolescent boys in relation to body fat, muscle mass, and waist-to-height ratio. It is a sleep-component study, not a documented full-guideline obesity analysis.[43]

- **Rhodes et al. (2019)** is identified as a Canadian study of parental support for movement guidelines among children and youth, but the available citation does not provide its complete adherence algorithm or an overweight/obesity association.[43]

- Citations for **Janssen et al. (2017)** and **Saunders et al. (2016)** identify Canadian guideline adherence or combinations of physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep, but the supplied spans do not establish a study-specific obesity result or a complete all-three adherence analysis.[45] Likewise, **Watson et al. (2021)** is identified as an Australian primary-school study of guideline adherence and academic achievement, not as a verified overweight/obesity association report.[46] These reports should not be added to the requested positive/null denominator solely because their titles mention movement guidelines.

**Age and date exclusions**

- **Hong Kong preschool study:** this preschool-only report is excluded from the requested school-aged/adolescent evidence even though it tested the full three-component construct. It used at least **180 minutes/day of total physical activity**, including **60 minutes/day of energetic play/MVPA**, less than **1 hour/day of screen time**, and **10–13 hours/day of sleep**.[44] Only **5 of 172 participants (2.9%)** met all three in the reported all-three table.[44] The study found no significant association between individual or combined guideline adherence and BMI or normal-weight odds; for the all-three exposure, the reported estimates were **OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.11–4.31, p = 0.687**, and **BMI β = 0.54, 95% CI −0.77–1.85, p = 0.417**.[44] The supplied spans also refer to **251 preschoolers** for the 2.9% figure, while the all-three analysis table gives **N = 172**; both denominators must be retained rather than reconciled by inference. This report contributes no target-age count.

- **“Compliance with 24-hour movement patterns in schoolchildren—Infant versus Primary Stage” (2024; DOI 10.12800/CCD.V19I62.2154):** the report included **260 schoolchildren**, comprising **93 infant-stage** and **167 primary-stage** children, and assessed physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep. It reported compliance above **75%**, with physical activity measured by questionnaire and sleep by weekly diary.[49] It did not report a defined overweight/obesity association in the supplied extract, included an early-childhood subgroup, and was published after the **31 May 2023** cutoff; it therefore cannot enter the requested evidence set.[49]

- **Chinese adult prospective cohort:** the 2024 adult study included **9,227 adults aged 35 years and older** followed through 2015 and used an adult Canadian guideline score from **0 to 3**. Its outcome was first occurrence of major noncommunicable disease—not overweight, obesity, or adiposity—and it was published after the requested cutoff.[47] It is excluded by population, outcome, and date.

- **University-student study:** the 2,611 participants were university students aged **18–29 years**, and adherence used adult thresholds of at least **150 minutes/week of MVPA**, no more than **8 hours/day of sedentary time**, and at least **7 hours/day of sleep**.[48] This is outside the school-aged/adolescent population and uses adult guideline thresholds, so it does not contribute to the requested counts.

**Interpretation for a concerned parent**

The most direct target-age findings represented here point in an inverse direction: full adherence was associated with lower obesity odds in the multinational ISCOLE sample and lower overweight/obesity odds in the Chinese primary-school sample.[53][43] However, the U.S. adolescent ADHD study shows that the result can be null for binary overweight/obesity contrasts and significant for a trend analysis within the same report.[46] Differences in age, country, ADHD status, guideline thresholds, measurement method, BMI or body-composition indicator, and statistical model limit direct comparison.

The likely relevance is therefore population-level rather than a guarantee for an individual adolescent. The Canadian guidance links the integrated movement pattern with better body composition and broader health, but its page does not test a specific causal pathway.[25] In the Chinese study, the separate physical-activity, screen-time, and sleep findings are compatible with different contributions from each behaviour, yet they do not prove mediation or causation.[43] Most importantly, cross-sectional findings cannot determine whether movement patterns influence body weight, whether existing weight status influences movement patterns, or whether diet, family circumstances, socioeconomic factors, health conditions, or measurement error partly explain the association.[43][46][53] For a child or adolescent with a disability or medical condition, the guideline source advises obtaining individualized guidance from a health professional.[25]

- **Operational thresholds varied even for individual components.** WHO used ≥60 minutes/day of MVPA for ages 5–17; GSHS used ≥60 minutes/day on at least 5 or all 7 past days; ISCOLE used >60 minutes/day measured by Actigraph for ≥7 days (>574 counts/15 seconds); and Marques et al. (2020) used a self-reported 0–7-day measure. Global Matrix, HBSC and ICAD were MVPA-only measures, not full three-domain adherence. [54]

- **Yang et al. (2022) reported a favorable full-guideline pattern in China.** In a national cross-sectional sample of 34,887 Chinese children and adolescents, the Canadian 2016 thresholds were ≥60 minutes/day of MVPA for seven consecutive days, ≤2 hours/day of screen time, and 9–11 hours of sleep for ages 5–13 or 8–10 hours for ages 14–17, with consistent bed and wake times; “all” required all three. Only 2.1% met all three, and meeting none, one or two was associated with more overweight/obesity than meeting all three, although the supplied passage gives no effect estimate. The analysis also included dietary guidelines; data were collected in 2014–2015, behaviors were self-reported, and regional socioeconomic and dietary differences—such as higher overweight/obesity in Tianjin and Liaoning—could contribute to the pattern. [60][63]

- **Keane et al. (2017) illustrates why component findings should not be counted as full-guideline findings.** In a cross-sectional Cork, Ireland, sample of 826 children aged 8–11 years, objective BMI classified 196 children (23.7%) as overweight/obese. The exposure index combined only MVPA and screen time: MVPA was inversely associated with overweight/obesity, screen time with higher risk, while total sedentary time was null after MVPA adjustment. Overweight/obese children averaged 75.1 versus 95.4 minutes/day of MVPA in normal-weight children (p<0.001). [58]

- **The supplied study summaries show both favorable and null full/partial patterns.** A Hong Kong adolescent report linked PA or PA-plus-sleep adherence with healthier body weight in boys; a Japanese primary-school report linked screen time or combinations containing screen time with overweight/obesity; and a 12-country ISCOLE report described lower odds of obesity or high BMI z-scores among participants meeting three recommendations. A Chinese 2023 report described the lowest overweight/obesity rate among those meeting the overall guidelines and a dose–response pattern. Conversely, a 2020 report found no association between meeting all three recommendations and adiposity, although screen-time-only and screen-time-plus-sleep categories were associated with lower excess adiposity. The supplied summary lacks exact ages, estimates, thresholds or denominators, so these statements are discovery context rather than a final count. [62]


- **Later sources do not alter the May 2023 snapshot.** A Spanish study of 839 children aged 8–13 years used ≥60 minutes/day MVPA, <2 hours/day screen time and 9–11 hours/day sleep; high adherence meant two or three components, but it was published 10 July 2023 and studied insulin resistance rather than overweight/obesity. [55] A 2025 review lists candidate reports by Shi (2020), Hui (2021), Wang (2021), Yang (2022), Zhou (2022), Sun (2023), Chen (2022; 318 Shanghai pupils over two years) and Huang (2023), but does not verify their study-level thresholds or results and is discovery-only. [56] A 2025 U.S. YRBS analysis of 40,970 adolescents aged 14–17 years reported worse weight status with fewer guidelines met—OR 1.38, 1.42 and 1.18 for meeting zero, one or two versus all three—but its post-cutoff, cross-sectional, self-reported results cannot enter the requested count. [57] Preschool-only evidence using 180 minutes of total activity, including 60 minutes of MVPA, ≤1 hour of recreational screen use and 10–13 hours of sleep is excluded by both age and date. [61]

- Sun et al. (2023, China): 440 youth aged 7–18 in 7 cities; 7.3% met all 3 guidelines. Versus none, overweight/obesity ORs at 1/2/3 components were 0.51 (95% CI 0.22–1.17), 0.32 (0.13–0.77), and 0.23 (0.07–0.81), trend *p*=.006. The inverse dose pattern is mixed/unclear for strict full adherence because the 1-component CI crosses 1 and partial exposures were analyzed. [66]
- IDEFICS/I.Family: 2,985 children aged 6–12 in 8 European countries; compliance fell from 17.5% at baseline to 12.3% at 2-year follow-up. Overall BMI-z-score and waist-to-hip-ratio trajectories were null, but boys’ WTHR was higher in persistent noncompliers than compliers (*F*=4.09, *p*=.007), a mixed subgroup result. [69]
- Full adherence combines MVPA, screen time, and sleep; Canadian 2016 cutoffs were ≥60 min/day, ≤2 h/day, and 9–11 h sleep (ages 5–12) or 8–10 h (13–17). Spanish adolescents (*n*=1,465) had 5.4% full adherence but no obesity association; a cited 12-country study lacks age, result, and full-adherence details. Later-review, preschool/adult, component-only, and post-cutoff reports are excluded from May-2023 counts. [64][65][68][70][71][72]

- **2020; 679 youth aged 8–18:** full three-guideline adherence was null for BMI z-score, fat-mass percentage, and visceral adipose tissue [75]. In adolescents, screen-time-only adherence had ORs of 0.38, 0.34, and 0.27 for high BMI, excess fat mass, and excess VAT; screen time plus sleep had OR 0.11 for high BMI. Children had no significant associations.

Components generally related to lower BMI z-score, but adjusted screen-time BMI was null (*p* = .15); cross-sectional self-report and missingness limit causal inference [74].

- **Spain, 2014–2018, ages 7–11, *n* = 357:** low, medium, and high adherence comprised 140 (39.2%), 162 (45.4%), and 55 (15.4%); low adherence had more overweight by IOTF BMI (*p* = .013). Screen-time nonadherence separately had OR 1.92 (95% CI 1.10–3.36; *p* = .022), a component result; the volunteer sample was nonrepresentative and lacked objective accelerometry [76].

**Exposure definitions that affect classification**

- **Full 24-hour adherence is a combined exposure, not a component result.** The construct integrates physical activity or MVPA, sedentary behaviour or recreational screen time, and sleep across the day; analyses of only MVPA, screen time, sleep, or a time reallocation must therefore be kept separate from full-guideline adherence [82][84][90].

- **Guideline thresholds are jurisdiction- and age-specific.** The Canadian 2016 guideline for ages 5–17 recommends at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily, muscle- and bone-strengthening activity at least 3 days per week, several hours of light activity daily, no more than 2 hours of recreational screen time daily, and 9–11 hours of sleep for ages 5–13 or 8–10 hours for ages 14–17 [82][84]. Australia’s 5–17-year guideline similarly recommends at least 1 hour of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily, vigorous activity on at least 3 days per week, several hours of light activity, no more than 2 hours of recreational screen time, and 9–11 or 8–10 hours of sleep for the younger and older age bands, respectively [90]. These examples should not be treated as interchangeable study algorithms.

- **Even component-only physical-activity thresholds varied.** The Australian evidence summary records studies defining adherence as at least 60 minutes of MVPA per day, at least 60 minutes of at least moderate-intensity activity daily in 1-minute bouts, or at least 60 minutes of MVPA per day on 5 of 7 days [29]. This variation is one reason the thresholds and “all components” rule must accompany each study classification.

**Full or potentially full-guideline findings in this source group**

- **The Asia-Fit Study reported a significant inverse association for a combined guideline-count exposure.** The study included 12,590 adolescents with a mean age of 13.63 ± 1.01 years from Bangkok, Hong Kong, Kuala Lumpur, Seoul, Shanghai, Singapore, Taipei, and Tokyo. Questionnaires assessed MVPA, recreational screen time, sleep and covariates; body-fat percentage was measured by bioelectrical impedance. After covariate adjustment, meeting a greater number of 24-hour guidelines was associated with lower body-fat percentage; meeting only the sleep guideline and meeting both physical activity and sleep guidelines were also negatively associated with body-fat percentage compared with meeting no guidelines. The source supplies no numeric effect estimate, confidence interval or p-value, and does not identify the guideline issuer, version or exact thresholds [83]. It also states that the directionality and mechanisms require further research [83]. Thus, this is one study-level inverse adiposity association, not evidence that adherence causes lower body fat.

- **The Chen S-T report remains a relevant but unresolved full-guideline candidate.** One later source labels it Chen et al. (2021), whereas another citation lists the same title and DOI as a 2020 report; both identify Chinese children and adolescents and a study of 24-hour guideline adherence with overweight/obesity [82][85]. The available spans provide no study-specific direction, estimate, uncertainty, significance result or conclusion. The bibliographic discrepancy and missing result must be resolved before the report is assigned to the positive, null or mixed count.

- **Roman-Viñas et al. (2016) is identified as a 12-country full-guideline/adiposity study, but the retained evidence does not give its result.** The citation describes children meeting recommendations for 24-hour movement guidelines and associations with adiposity; the source does not supply the study’s exact countries, thresholds, outcome definition, effect estimate or significance classification [85][29]. It is therefore a lead, not a classifiable result in this fragment.

- **Rubín et al. (2020) and da Costa et al. (2020) are additional candidate reports.** Rubín et al. is explicitly described as studying adherence to the combined movement guidelines among Czech children and adolescents, but the available citation gives neither the guideline version, adherence algorithm, design nor obesity result. da Costa et al. is identified as a Brazilian-adolescent study of meeting 24-hour movement guidelines, but its full-versus-component operationalization and adiposity result are not supplied [28]. The source containing these leads was published after the requested cutoff, so it can locate earlier reports but cannot replace independent verification or determine the May 2023 counts [28].

- **“The Goldilocks Day for healthy adiposity measures among children and adolescents” is another adiposity-related lead, not a verified result here.** The citation identifies the topic and population, but the retained source does not report the indicator, cutoff, guideline exposure or association estimate [28].

**Component-only evidence: informative but not part of the full-guideline count**

- **The Australian evidence summary found mixed physical-activity results rather than a uniform association.** In a subgroup defined as meeting or not meeting at least 60 minutes of MVPA per day, children and youth with mean ages from 6.9 to 12 years had favourable body-composition associations in 2 of 3 studies—Steele et al. (2009) and Martinez-Gomez et al. (2010b)—and null associations in 1 of 3, Mendoza et al. (2012) [29]. Outcomes included BMI, weight status using CDC, IOTF or WHO cut-points, skinfolds, body mass, waist circumference, percent body fat, fat mass and related measures [29]. The evidence quality for the cross-sectional body-composition evidence was rated **very low**, partly because of serious risk of bias [29]. These 2/3 and 1/3 figures are MVPA-component counts, not the requested independent-study counts for full 24-hour adherence.

- **Broader component-level physical-activity evidence was heterogeneous.** For MVPA, 20/30 studies reported favourable associations, 6/30 mixed favourable and null associations, 3/30 null associations, and 1/30 mixed null and unfavourable associations. For vigorous activity, the corresponding pattern was 10/15 favourable, 4/15 mixed favourable/null and 1/15 mixed null/unfavourable. For moderate activity it was 2/10 favourable, 2/10 mixed, 5/10 null and 1/10 mixed null/unfavourable; for light activity it was 1/9 favourable, 2/9 mixed, 3/9 null and 3/9 mixed null/unfavourable [29]. These results concern individual activity components and should not be presented as full-guideline findings.

- **Sedentary-behaviour components generally showed more adverse than null body-composition patterns, but the denominators are component-specific.** In prospective longitudinal findings, higher screen time was associated with unfavourable body composition in 11/15 studies, television viewing in 14/16, computer use in 3/4, video games in 0/2, total sedentary behaviour in 0/1 and weekend internet use in 1/1. In case-control findings, higher screen time was associated with being overweight/obese in 4/4 studies and television viewing in 2/2, while computer use showed the association in 0/2 studies [29]. A broader component summary reported unfavourable body-composition associations for screen time in 26/36 studies, television in 58/71, computer use in 7/30, video games in 3/20, total sedentary behaviour in 3/4, homework in 3/7 and quiet time in 1/1 [29]. These are not deduplicated full-guideline study counts.

- **Sleep-duration evidence also contained both associations and null findings.** The Australian summary states that 7 of 12 longitudinal analyses found a significant association between short sleep and adiposity gain, while 5 found no association; however, the retained table displays paired pre-update/update values such as 12/13 and 7/8, so the precise version basis is not fully clear [29]. Among cross-sectional analyses, the text reports significant associations with adiposity for short sleep in 50 of 58 analyses, lower adiposity risk with longer sleep in 3, increased adiposity risk with irregular sleep in 1, and no association in 8; the table again displays paired values, including 58/71, 50/57 and 8/10 [29]. One randomized trial of 37 participants found that increased sleep produced a mean weight difference of 0.24 kg after one week compared with decreased sleep, with *p*<0.001; the comparison was 10.5 versus 8.1 hours of sleep [29]. These are sleep-component findings, not full-guideline adherence results.

- **The Spanish Region of Murcia report was null but component-only.** This cross-sectional study included 250 schoolchildren aged 8–13 years, 41.2% girls, from six primary schools. Meeting the physical-activity recommendation meant at least 420 minutes of MVPA per week versus less than 420 minutes, based on the previous 7 days. No statistically significant association was found for any obesity-related variable [85]. Because it tested physical activity alone rather than the combined movement guideline, it cannot enter the full-guideline positive or null denominator.

- **A Latino adolescent study found a sleep-regularity association, not a full-guideline association.** In Phoenix, the study included 38 adolescents aged 12–16 years and 22 young adults aged 18–22 years, all with obesity defined as BMI at least the 95th age- and sex-specific percentile or at least 30 kg/m². A 7-day wrist accelerometer measured MVPA, light activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep and sleep regularity separately. Among adolescents, sleep-regularity index was inversely associated with waist circumference (*r* = −0.52, *p*<0.05); among young adults it was inversely associated with weight (*r* = −0.65, *p*<0.05), waist circumference (*r* = −0.79, *p*<0.01) and body-fat percentage (*r* = −0.83, *p*<0.01). No other significant behaviour–health relationships were observed, and the authors noted that the small sample could have limited detection of associations [87]. The young-adult subgroup is outside the requested population, and the adolescent analysis used separate components rather than full adherence.

- **A later pooled reallocation analysis supports component mechanisms but is outside the cutoff and exposure definition.** The post-cutoff review pooled 11 observational and experimental studies, most of them cross-sectional, and used *p*<0.05 as its significance threshold [28]. In pooled child data (*n*=2,103), reallocating 30 minutes toward MVPA was associated with BMI z-score differences of −0.08 (95% CI −0.14 to −0.01) when replacing sleep, −0.15 (−0.21 to −0.10) when replacing sedentary behaviour, and −0.20 (−0.29 to −0.12) when replacing light activity. In adolescents (*n*=1,234), the corresponding estimates were 0.05 (−0.04 to 0.14), 0.01 (−0.07 to 0.09), and −0.04 (−0.15 to 0.07), all with confidence intervals crossing zero [28]. The review describes MVPA as a key component and suggests that optimizing the whole movement composition may support healthier adiposity, but it also reports no clear optimal duration for each behaviour and does not test the May 2023 full-adherence roster [28]. Proposed hormonal, behavioural and environmental explanations for the weaker adolescent results remain hypotheses, not tested causal mechanisms [28].

- **A 24-hour-behaviour study assessing physical fitness was not an obesity study.** The report examined shuttle-run, standing-long-jump, handgrip and 4 × 10-m shuttle-run performance rather than overweight, obesity or adiposity; it therefore does not qualify for the requested association count [86].

**Early-childhood exclusions**

- **The Hong Kong WHO-guideline study is preschool-only and must be excluded.** It included 251 children aged 3–6 years, measured physical activity and sleep with an activPAL for 7 days, obtained screen time from parents, and classified weight as underweight, normal weight or overweight/obese. Only 2.9% met all three guidelines; component compliance was 14.5% for physical activity, 67.4% for screen time and 39.0% for sleep. Neither individual nor combined adherence was significantly associated with BMI or the odds of normal weight [88]. This null result does not enter the school-aged/adolescent denominator.

- **The Finnish DAGIS study is also excluded because its participants were preschoolers.** Among 778 children with a mean age of 4.7 ± 0.9 years, data collected in 2015–2016 used thresholds of at least 180 minutes of physical activity daily, including at least 60 minutes of MVPA, no more than 60 minutes of screen time, and 10–13 hours of sleep. All-three compliance was 24%; component compliance was 85% for physical activity, 76% for sleep and 35% for screen time. Adjusted associations with lower waist circumference were reported for physical activity (B=−1.37, *p*<0.001), sleep (B=−0.72, *p*=0.009) and physical activity plus sleep (B=−1.03, *p*<0.001); lower BMI was associated with sleep (B=−0.26, *p*=0.027) and physical activity plus sleep (B=−0.30, *p*=0.007), while screen time was null [89]. The supplied passage gives no full-all-three association estimate, and the entire study is outside the requested age range.

**Interpretation, mechanisms and parent relevance**

- **The evidence does not establish causation.** The later evidence synthesis says most available studies were cross-sectional, which prevents determining causality or directionality; longitudinal research is needed [82]. A general guideline-evidence framework likewise treats non-randomized evidence as starting at low quality and downgrades it for risk of bias, unexplained heterogeneity, imprecision or publication bias [84]. That framework is methodological context, not a direct quality rating for every eligible youth study.

- **Measurement and context can explain conflicting findings.** Self-reported activity, screen time and sleep are vulnerable to memory error; younger children may have difficulty estimating durations, producing under- or overestimation. Age, sex and parental education were often inadequately reported, while socioeconomic, cultural and racial contexts may alter both adherence and observed associations [82]. Accelerometer calibration, wear protocols and subjective-reporting inaccuracies can also reduce precision and comparability [82]. The later review found socioeconomic-adherence results inconclusive because socioeconomic variables were underreported; its discussion of standardization and moderation concerns adherence context rather than proving an obesity mechanism [82].

- **For a parent, the bounded conclusion is an association signal, not a guarantee for an individual adolescent.** The Asia-Fit study supports one inverse association between a combined guideline-count exposure and body fat, while the broader component literature includes favourable, null and mixed results and very-low-quality evidence [83][29]. The Australian report also records mixed stakeholder views about whether young people meet the guidelines; stakeholders identified technology, gaming and screen use as possible influences on physical activity and sleep, but these were perceptions rather than tested causal explanations [29]. The available evidence can inform understanding of adolescent health, but it does not predict an individual child’s weight or justify individualized medical advice.

**Direct study-level findings**

- **Katzmarzyk et al. (2017, United States) reported an inverse association.** Among 357 White and African American children and adolescents aged 5–18 years in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, data collected from January 2010 to August 2011, meeting more components of the 24-hour guidelines was associated with lower BMI, general and abdominal adiposity, and lower odds of obesity [34]. Only 8.4% met all three components; 26.9% met none, 36.4% met one, and 28.3% met two. The study found significant associations with BMI, subcutaneous adipose tissue, visceral adipose tissue, triglycerides, and glucose, but not blood pressure or HDL cholesterol [34]. BMI was calculated in kg/m² using the CDC 2000 growth charts, with obesity defined as BMI at or above the 95th percentile; waist circumference, total body fat by DXA, and abdominal subcutaneous and visceral fat by MRI were also measured [34]. The full three-component category had lower obesity odds directionally, but the explicitly significant component-specific estimate was for meeting the sleep guideline alone: OR 0.59 (95% CI 0.37–0.95) versus meeting none; the trend across the number of components met was significant at *p* = 0.03 [34]. Thus, this study supports an inverse association for adiposity but has mixed results across endpoints and should not be summarized as uniformly significant for every outcome.

- **Sun et al. (published 12 January 2023, China) also reported an inverse association.** In 440 Chinese children and adolescents aged 7–18 years from seven cities, physical activity was measured by accelerometer and screen time and sleep by questionnaire; 7.3% met all three guidelines [36]. Height and weight were measured, BMI was calculated, and participants were classified as normal weight, overweight, or obese according to standards from the Group of China Task Force [36]. Compared with meeting no guideline, meeting one, two, and three guidelines was associated with ORs of 0.51 (95% CI 0.22–1.17), 0.32 (0.13–0.77), and 0.23 (0.07–0.81), respectively, with a significant trend (*p* for trend = 0.006) [36]. The full three-guideline group had the lowest overweight/obesity rate. A separate table model reported OR 0.25 (95% CI 0.07–0.87) for meeting all three versus none, adjusted for sex, age, and socioeconomic status; these estimates should be retained as separate model outputs rather than merged [36]. The sex-stratified full-adherence estimates were OR 0.11 (0.02–0.57) for boys and OR 0.87 (0.11–6.83) for girls, indicating that the significant inverse pattern was clearer in boys than girls [36]. This was a cross-sectional association, not evidence that adherence caused lower weight.

- **Janssen et al. (2017, Canada) provided adherence prevalence, not an obesity association.** In 22,115 Canadians aged 10–17 years from the 2013/14 Health Behaviour in School-aged Children survey, movement behaviours were self-reported; fewer than 3% met all three recommendations, while 25% met two, 51% met one, and 21% met none [32]. The component-specific proportions were 66% for sleep, 35% for MVPA, and 8% for screen time [32]. The report did not analyze BMI, overweight, obesity, or another adiposity outcome, so it is neither a positive-association nor a null-association study for the requested question. Its methods also describe the sleep band for ages 10–13 years, whereas the guideline description gives the broader 5–13-year band; this wording difference should not be silently harmonized [32].

- **Toledo-Vargas et al. (published 31 August 2020, Chile) likewise did not test the guideline–obesity relationship.** In children aged 9–11 years from Carahue, Chile, 198 participants supplied valid self-reported data and 141 supplied valid mixed accelerometer/questionnaire data; the mean age was 10.1 (0.8) years [33]. Full adherence required all three components: at least 60 minutes of MVPA, no more than 2 hours of recreational screen time, and 9–11 hours of sleep. Full adherence was 3.5% with self-reported data and 0.7% with the mixed method, although the abstract reports 3.2% rather than 3.5% for the self-reported estimate; both values should be flagged as an internal report discrepancy [33]. Self-reported MVPA required at least 60 minutes on all seven days, whereas the mixed method required at least 60 minutes on each valid accelerometer day. About 70% of the sample was overweight; BMI z scores classified values below −2 SD as thin, −2 SD to +1 SD as normal, above +1 SD as overweight, and above +2 SD as obesity [33]. The study reported compliance only, not an adherence–BMI or adherence–overweight/obesity model; it therefore must not be labeled “no correlation.”

- **A secondary citation identifies Roman-Viñas et al. (2016) as an important lead, but not as an independently verified count unit here.** The Katzmarzyk paper reports that a 12-country study of 10-year-old children found 7.2% meeting the guidelines and that those meeting them had lower average BMI and lower odds of obesity; it also cites a Canadian study in 6–17-year-olds reporting 17% adherence and better health indicators among those meeting all three components [34]. Because these details are presented in the cited paper’s background rather than in the primary reports supplied here, they require independent primary-report verification before entering final study counts.

**Exposure and measurement differences**

- The Canadian 2016 guideline combines at least 60 minutes/day of MVPA, no more than 2 hours/day of recreational screen time, and age-specific sleep: 9–11 hours/night for ages 5–13 and 8–10 hours/night for ages 14–17 [32]. Full adherence means meeting all three components, not merely meeting one or two. The guideline was also adopted in Australia, South Africa, New Zealand, and the Asia-Pacific region, but adoption does not guarantee identical implementation across studies [30]. Sun et al. used a screen-time cutoff of **less than** 2 hours/day, while Katzmarzyk and Toledo-Vargas used no more than 2 hours/day; these are similar but not mathematically identical exposure definitions [50, 51, 53].

- The permitted review of 63 studies separated meeting all three recommendations from meeting one or some components and meeting none. When both types of measurement were available, it selected device-based measures over self-report and used the first or baseline measurement in multiwave studies [30]. This matters because Katzmarzyk used self-reported behaviours and television viewing as a proxy for recreational screen time, Toledo-Vargas used either questionnaires or a mixed accelerometer/questionnaire approach, and Sun used accelerometry for physical activity but questionnaires for screen time and sleep [50, 51, 53].

- The review included 63 studies, 387,437 participants aged 3–18 years, and 23 countries; 55 studies were cross-sectional, seven longitudinal, and one quasi-experimental [30]. Its stated age categories are not fully consistent: one passage defines children as 5–11 and adolescents as 12–18, whereas the characteristics section reports children as 6–12 and adolescents as 13–18. Primary-study age ranges therefore remain decisive for eligibility. The review’s pooled adherence figures—7.12% meeting all three overall, 2.68% among adolescents, and 28.59% of adolescents meeting none—describe guideline prevalence, not overweight/obesity associations [30]. It rated 49 studies (77.8%) as high quality and 14 (22.2%) as medium quality, but reported substantial heterogeneity for overall adherence (*I*² = 99.58%) and marked publication-bias asymmetry (LFK index = 4.69) [30].

- The proposed mechanism is a 24-hour time-budget effect: physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep occupy the same day, so more time in one necessarily displaces time in another [32]. This makes an inverse adiposity relationship biologically plausible, but the eligible cross-sectional findings do not directly test a causal pathway. Self-report error, the use of television as a screen-time proxy, different age thresholds, socioeconomic and other unmeasured confounding, and differences in country and sample selection can all contribute to the observed variation [45, 51].

- **For a parent, the bounded conclusion is therefore observational:** the directly reported Katzmarzyk and Sun studies found lower adiposity or overweight/obesity odds with greater combined-guideline adherence, while the Janssen and Toledo-Vargas reports measured adherence without testing the weight relationship. Meeting the guidelines should not be interpreted as guaranteeing a particular child’s weight outcome, and the estimates should not be treated as individualized medical advice [45, 48, 50, 51, 53].

- **Roman-Viñas et al. (2016):** In a cross-sectional ISCOLE analysis of 6,128 children aged 9–11 years from 12 countries, meeting all three Canadian recommendations—sleep 9–11 h/night, screen time ≤2 h/day, and MVPA ≥60 min/day—was associated with lower obesity odds than meeting none (OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.18–0.45) and with a lower BMI z-score. Models adjusted for age, sex, parental education, and unhealthy-diet score. Obesity was BMI z-score >+2 SD using age- and sex-specific WHO references; only 7% met all three recommendations, although adherence reached 15% in Australia and Canada [22]. This is an inverse association, not evidence of causation.

- **Chinese seven-city report, available 12 January 2023:** Among 440 Chinese children and adolescents aged 7–18 years assessed in March–July 2019, compared with meeting none of the Canadian components, meeting one, two, or all three produced ORs of 0.51 (95% CI 0.22–1.17), 0.32 (0.13–0.77), and 0.23 (0.07–0.81), respectively, with a significant decreasing trend (P trend = 0.006). Thus, the report supports an inverse dose–response relationship between the number of guidelines met and combined overweight/obesity risk; the one-component estimate was imprecise, whereas the two- and three-component estimates excluded 1. Height and weight were measured, BMI categories followed China Task Force standards, physical activity was accelerometer-measured, and sleep and screen time were questionnaire-based [91]. Overall adherence was 7.3% (boys 11.8%, girls 3.4%; P < 0.001), and the association was significant in boys but not girls [91]. The sample included 26 18-year-olds (5.9%); although the Canadian guideline applies through age 17, the authors treated these participants as adolescents under the WHO 10–19-year definition [91].

- **Exposure boundary:** Full adherence means meeting physical activity/MVPA, sedentary or recreational-screen, and sleep recommendations together; analyses of one component or two-component combinations remain partial exposures [11,179]. For ages 5–17 years, the Australian and Canadian summaries specify at least 60 minutes/day of MVPA, no more than 2 hours/day of recreational screen time, 9–11 hours of sleep at ages 5–13, and 8–10 hours at ages 14–17; Australian guidance excludes schoolwork screen time [71,95,2]. A Canadian toolkit’s general statement that adherence is associated with better body composition gives no obesity-specific estimate or study count and is therefore contextual, not counted evidence [39].

- **Sources not altering the May 2023 count:** The later component-composition review is post-cutoff and did not test full adherence; none of its included studies assessed screen time. Its reported association of 60 additional relative MVPA minutes with a 0.27-unit lower BMI z-score is therefore not a cutoff-eligible full-guideline result . A 2024 Chinese scoping review reported 34 of 53 studies (64.2%) involving children/adolescents, 51 cross-sectional and 2 longitudinal studies, and 43 health-outcome studies, but supplied no positive-versus-null count for full adherence [41]. Its title-only leads, including Chen, Yang, and Xu, require independent verification. An ongoing Chinese meta-analysis protocol likewise supplied no outcome result or exact age eligibility [20].

- **Limits and exclusions:** Adult ELANS findings are outside scope despite lower adult overweight/obesity odds with two or three guidelines in men (OR 0.75 and 0.69) and no significant association in women [23]. A 2025 Chinese cohort concerned psychological well-being rather than an obesity outcome and is post-cutoff [21]. Cross-sectional designs do not establish causation; self-reported movement measures may introduce measurement error and social-desirability bias, and the Chinese report notes sleep-questionnaire bias and substantial exclusions [179,86].

All 3 vs 0: overweight/obesity OR 0.25 (95% CI 0.07–0.87; sex/age/SES adjusted); number meeting 1/2/3: OR 0.51/0.32/0.23, *P*trend=.006. Measured BMI/China Task Force; cutoff not reported; components also analyzed. [26]

(published 16 December 2025; searched 1 February 2024) included 20 under-18 multicomponent interventions (9 meta-analyzed), not observational full-adherence obesity studies; risk of bias, subjective measures, sparse age strata, language restriction, and diet co-intervention limit inference. [95] The 2026 Chinese scoping review covered 92 studies aged 3–18 (70 aged 7–18; 20 aged 3–6); 52 compliance studies averaged 4.91% all-three compliance (0.1–33.51%), with 80 cross-sectional, 9 cohort, and 3 experimental designs. It broadly linked multiple indicators with improved weight status, but supplied no study-level obesity classification. [96]

- **Context, not the requested count:** A 2024 review cited Roman-Viñas (2016; 12 countries), Chemtob (2021; at-risk youth), Chen (2021; China), and Zhu (2020; body weight), plus Japanese primary-school and Chinese movement-plus-dietary/overweight candidates, but supplied no results. Its totals—60 all-three, 61 MVPA-only, 60 screen-only, 61 sleep-only, and 45 two-component analyses—cannot replace the cutoff roster. [148,214] A 2016 review (14 studies/9 datasets; 36,560 participants; 13 cross-sectional and 1 prospective, with Hjorth’s 200-day follow-up) found lower adiposity/overweight risk with high physical activity, low sedentary behavior, and high sleep versus the opposite. Laurson reported obesity ORs of 2.6 (95% CI 1.1–6.5), 4.7 (1.9–11.3), and 8.2 (3.2–32.1) for meeting 2, 1, or 0 versus all 3; these were component combinations, not necessarily full-guideline analyses. Heterogeneity prevented pooling and evidence was low. [97]

- Interpret results by direction, magnitude, comparator, outcome, design, and follow-up—not significance alone; GRADE considers bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. [93] BMI cannot separate fat from fat-free mass, whereas FMI relates fat mass to height; the cited 60–90-year-old study is terminology background only, not eligible youth evidence. [98]

- **Overweight/obesity outcomes:** BMI (kg/m² = weight/height²) is an age/sex-specific surrogate, not body fat. NCCOR: overweight 85th–95th BMI-for-age percentile; obesity ≥95th percentile or absolute BMI ≥30 kg/m² (lower value); WHO/World Obesity: +2 SD above the median [99][101]. BMI-versus-fat classification is 95–100% specific but 36–66% sensitive; at ages 6–12, BMI explained 54% of girls’ and 69% of boys’ fat variation (error 20.3% and 22.5%) [99]. Waist (tape; 0.1 cm; ≥8 y) is a central-fat surrogate; skinfolds estimate %fat; BIA estimates fat/lean mass but growth limits pediatric validity; DXA estimates total/regional fat and lean mass; ADP estimates density/fat (BOD POD ≥6 y, 35–200 kg) [99][100]. No direct full-guideline association is reported here [100][101].

- **Sleep-duration evidence is component-only, not evidence of full 24-h adherence.** A systematic review of nonclinical primary-school children aged 5–13 years included 112 articles; among 103 sleep-duration articles, 86 (83%) reported a significant inverse association with measured weight status and 15 (14%) reported no significant association. One study found higher overweight/obesity among children sleeping >10 hours/night, and another reported sex-specific mixed results; these figures must not enter the full-guideline denominator [102].

- **The adiposity outcomes were heterogeneous.** Reported indicators included BMI >85th percentile for overweight/obesity, BMI z-scores using CDC, IOTF, WHO or national charts, waist circumference, body-fat percentage, DXA fat, waist-to-height ratio and visceral fat. No single cutoff, reference standard or assessment period can therefore be assumed across studies [102].

- **The Swiss adolescent study reported an inverse relationship, but its combined exposure was not the sleep-inclusive construct.** In the 2006 Swiss HBSC survey, adolescents aged 11–15 years numbered 9,791 before exclusions, 9,211 after imputation, and 8,130 in the normal-weight/overweight analytic sample; fewer than 2% met all three reported recommendations and more than half met none. Overweight used age- and sex-specific IOTF BMI cutoffs from self-reported height and weight; 95 participants (1.0%) classified as obese were grouped with overweight, while underweight participants were excluded [103]. Overweight boys were less likely to follow recommendations; adjusted odds reached 2.10 for physical activity less than weekly and 1.83 for screen time of at least 6 h/day versus 2 h/day. Among girls, the significant adjusted difference was lower adherence to 0–2 nutritional recommendations (AOR 0.54 versus 6–9). The authors judged the hypothesis only partially supported; cross-sectional data, self-reported BMI and incomplete nutrition scoring prevent causal interpretation [103]. Because the reported combination was nutrition, screen time and physical activity—not sleep—it should not be counted as an unqualified full 24-h result.

**Roster boundary and table-cell verification**

For this table, a row represents one independent eligible primary study or cohort available by **31 May 2023**. A review, component-only analysis, duplicate publication, or unverified citation lead is not itself a verified row. The evidence in this source group supports an **open roster**, not a completed named roster or final study count; participant age, full-guideline exposure, outcome definition, and study independence still require report-level verification [129][131].

- **Eligibility and screening:** Candidate reports should be screened against prespecified, documented criteria; reviewers should have accessible instructions, pilot the screening process, retrieve the full text, and use full-text information to check eligibility, outcomes, and methods [134]. In a methodological example, **29,846 abstracts** representing **14,923 unique citations** were independently double-screened and reconciliation ended at **0% disagreement**; these figures describe that example, not this evidence roster [129]. Mixed-age candidates therefore remain pending until full text establishes a separable school-aged or adolescent result [134].

- **Duplicates and study identity:** Different databases may return multiple records for the same reference. The table must distinguish duplicate records from multiple publications arising from one study, and count the study or cohort once rather than counting each publication, outcome, or model [134]. Each exclusion should retain an explicit reason, while unresolved records remain marked as pending rather than silently removed [134].

- **Search and completeness:** A defensible search uses multiple relevant information sources; MEDLINE alone can miss eligible studies, so supplementary reference-list checking, author contact, database searching, and similar-article tools are required [131]. Search strategies should be checked against known relevant records; one methodological benchmark suggests retrieving at least **80%–90% of known studies**, assuming that the known set represents the relevant literature [131]. Backward citation searching examines references cited by a source, whereas forward searching identifies later papers that cite it; Google Scholar, Scopus, and Web of Science have different coverage—for one example, they returned **65, 37, and 29 citations**, respectively [132]. Citation tracking should supplement, not replace, database searching, because selective citation of positive findings can bias the roster [132][135].

- **Search expansion can change the candidate set:** Recommended supplementary methods include repeating database searches, checking references in included studies and reviews, searching relevant journals, and contacting authors; one example found **four additional papers** after a repeated search [143]. A pre-cutoff study of qualitative-review searches found that supplementary methods identified **25 of 37** publications missed by traditional database searching (**67.6%**) and raised overall retrieval to **87.1% of 101 publications**; because that evidence concerns qualitative reviews, it demonstrates the rationale for an open search but does not establish the present movement-guideline roster [144]. Search results should be deduplicated before screening and, when new eligible records appear, used as seeds for another citation-search iteration [135].

- **Table-cell rule:** The study-name cell will use the verified first author and publication year. The population cell will state the recruitment or assessment country and exact reported age range. The obesity-indicator cell will preserve the metric, reference standard, cutoff, unit, ascertainment method, and assessment period; BMI, BMI z-score, percentile, waist circumference, body fat, and prevalence are not interchangeable. The core-finding cell will state the full-adherence definition, outcome, direction, estimate or significance, comparator, and qualification. If a value cannot be established, the cell will say **not reported**, **not verifiable**, **not accessible**, or **pending verification** rather than infer it.
