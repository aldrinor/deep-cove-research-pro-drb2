# Physical Activity and Health in Chinese Children and Adolescents Aged 6–17: Evidence Through Early 2020

**The overall conclusion is clear.** By early 2020, scientific evidence strongly supported regular physical activity for improving children’s cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular fitness, and skeletal health. Benefits for mental health and executive function were generally small but favorable; effects on overweight and obesity depended on the intervention, and improvements in academic performance were less consistent. Chinese national school surveys reported activity-compliance estimates spanning **13.1%–34.1%**, while the exact-age national nutrition survey found substantial sedentary exposure among 6–17-year-olds. These figures describe different measurements, not a single national trend. [1][2][3]

**The practical benchmark was daily activity.** China’s December 2017 guideline recommended at least 60 minutes of accumulated moderate-to-vigorous physical activity each day, including vigorous and muscle- and bone-strengthening activities on at least three days per week, together with limiting screen time and interrupting prolonged sitting. The strongest justification was broader health and fitness—not a promise that exercise alone would normalize weight or raise examination scores. [4]

**The review follows those distinctions.** It first establishes China’s activity, sedentary-behavior, and weight-status baseline, then explains intensity measurement. It subsequently examines overall health, cardiorespiratory and metabolic health, bone and muscle, brain and learning, and overweight and obesity, before drawing an evidence-weighted judgment for a Chinese adolescent-health initiative.

## Current status and key data

### Physical-activity compliance was low across national school surveys

**Most students fell below recommendations.** The clearest national school-survey estimates available before the cutoff were 29.9% in the 2016 Physical Activity and Fitness in China—The Youth Study, or PAFCTYS; 13.1% in the China 2018 Report Card using 2016 surveillance; and 34.1% in the 2017 PAFCTYS update. Their common message is widespread insufficient activity, but their definitions and analytic populations differ.

| China 2018 Report Card, autumn 2016 data | Ages 9–17; 125,281 students | ≥60 min daily during previous 7 days | Student questionnaire | 13.1% |

**The defensible summary is a proxy range.** For an initiative targeting ages 6–17, 13.1%–34.1% is a descriptive span of near-matching national school-survey estimates. It is neither a confidence interval nor an age-standardized estimate for precisely that population. Nevertheless, even the highest estimate indicates that approximately two-thirds of students fell below the survey’s activity benchmark.

**Daily and average compliance differ.** The 2016 and 2017 IPAQ-based analyses summed reported moderate and vigorous activity over seven days and divided by seven. The Report Card described achieving 60 minutes daily during the previous week. A weekly average can conceal individual days below the target, so the two approaches do not measure exactly the same behavior. The IPAQ analyses also counted activities lasting at least ten minutes, potentially omitting short bursts of children’s play. [1][5]

**Average activity was also insufficient.** In the valid 2016 PAFCTYS sample, mean self-reported MVPA was 45.4 minutes/day: 47.2 minutes for boys and 43.7 minutes for girls. Compliance was 31.8% among boys and 28.2% among girls. These sex differences were consistent with the Report Card, which reported daily compliance of 14.5% and 11.7%, respectively. [1][2]

**Older students were less active.** Both reporting approaches showed progressively poorer activity participation toward upper-secondary school. The comparison is especially relevant for adolescent-health initiatives because it identifies a developmental and institutional transition at which activity opportunities appear to contract.

**Residence showed a more complicated pattern.** Urban students reported more MVPA minutes in 2016, but the adjusted overall urban–rural difference in compliance was not significant. The 2017 update likewise found no overall urban–rural difference in MVPA compliance. Residence therefore should not be treated as a simple substitute for activity exposure: transport, school provision, household resources, and leisure opportunities can operate differently. [1][5]

### Exact-age national data show activity across several settings

**The strongest age-matched baseline was CNNHS.** The 2010–2012 China National Nutrition and Health Survey included 38,744 children aged 6–17 across all 31 mainland provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities, excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan. Trained interviewers collected information about the previous semester; parents or caregivers assisted children younger than ten. [6]

**School time was not total MVPA.** The 61.6-minute school-time mean and the 52.5% participation threshold concern school activity of varying intensity. They should not be relabeled as proof that half of Chinese children met a daily 60-minute MVPA recommendation. Similarly, the 44.9-minute leisure-time figure describes participants in that domain, rather than every child’s total daily activity.

**Transport was a substantial opportunity.** Walking accounted for 50.9% of school commuting and cycling for 13.3%, together producing the 64.2% active-transport share. The reported 38.5-minute average commuting duration included motorized journeys as well, so all of it should not be counted as exercise. These findings nonetheless show why school travel belongs alongside PE and sport in an adolescent-health strategy. [6]

**Activity patterns varied by domain.** Boys reported more school-time and leisure-time activity, while girls spent more time on domestic activity and homework. Older children reported more leisure-time exercise in CNNHS even though later school surveys showed lower overall MVPA compliance at higher grades. This is understandable: participation in one activity domain and achievement of an all-day intensity-and-duration target are different outcomes.

### Sedentary exposure was high, but its definition matters

**The age-matched sedentary burden was substantial.** CNNHS participants averaged 2.9 hours/day of leisure-time sedentary behavior, and 85.8% exceeded two hours/day. The measure included television, computer use, video games, reading, and homework outside school. Homework alone occupied nearly 1.5 hours/day—approximately half of that reported sedentary exposure. [6]

Sources: CNNHS [6], 2016 screen-time analysis [7], Report Card [2], 2017 update [5], and accelerometer meta-analysis [8].

**These figures describe different exposures.** A screen-only estimate should be much lower than a measure that also includes homework, classroom sitting, and other stationary activities. Thus, the Report Card’s 92.9% and PAFCTYS’s 36.8% do not imply an enormous change in screen behavior. Their denominators and behavioral definitions differ.

**Objective measurements reinforce the concern.** The February 2020 accelerometer meta-analysis estimated 37.66 minutes/day of MVPA and 539.61 minutes/day of sedentary behavior. The latter equals approximately 9.0 hours/day, calculated as 539.61 ÷ 60. These are pooled device-based estimates from Chinese youth samples, rather than a nationally representative exact-age prevalence estimate. [8]

**A population mean is not compliance.** The pooled MVPA mean was 22.34 minutes below the 60-minute benchmark, calculated as 60 − 37.66. That describes the difference between two averages; it does not reveal how many children met the recommendation or imply that every child needed precisely 22.34 additional minutes. Nevertheless, agreement between device-based and questionnaire evidence strengthens the conclusion that insufficient activity was common.

**Sedentary exposure also differed by age.** In the Report Card, the proportion reporting at least two hours/day of combined sedentary activities rose from 86.8% in primary school to 96.1% in both secondary-school groups. CNNHS similarly found greater sedentary time among older children and children in large cities. The school-stage pattern therefore involved both less MVPA and more sitting. [2][6]

**Earlier behavioral trends were unfavorable.** A pre-2020 systematic review reported that rural children’s television viewing increased from 0.7 to 1.7 hours/day between 1997 and 2006—a calculated increase of one hour/day. This supports a historical shift toward sedentary leisure, while remaining a narrower indicator than total sedentary time. [9]

### Overweight and obesity had risen substantially

**Both conditions exceeded earlier national levels.** A weighted CNNHS analysis of 36,285 children aged 6–17 estimated overweight prevalence at 9.6% and obesity at 6.4% in 2010–2012. The cited 2002 comparison, covering ages 7–17, was 4.5% overweight and 2.1% obese. [10]

**The longer-term direction was upward.** Relative to the cited 2002 figures, the 2010–2012 estimates were higher by 5.1 percentage points for overweight and 4.3 percentage points for obesity. These calculated differences establish the scale of the contrast, but the one-year age-band difference means they are not a fully age-standardized trend.

**The one-year pattern was mixed.** The 2017 update compared its results with cited 2016 values of 14.0% overweight and 11.9% obesity, describing an increase to 15.1% and a decrease to 10.7%, respectively. Its overweight comparator differs slightly from the 14.4% reported in the separate 2016 analyses. Consequently, the appropriate judgment is a persistently high burden, rather than a firm claim that obesity had entered sustained decline. [5]

**Weight categories were pediatric categories.** In PAFCTYS, overweight meant BMI at or above the Chinese age- and sex-specific 85th percentile but below the 95th percentile; obesity meant BMI at or above the 95th percentile. They were separate categories, not adult BMI thresholds of 25 and 30 kg/m². In 2016, the reported confidence intervals were 13.8%–15.0% for overweight and 11.0%–13.0% for obesity. [7]

**Boys and urban children had higher prevalence.** In CNNHS, boys had overweight and obesity prevalences of 10.9% and 7.8%, compared with 8.2% and 4.8% among girls. Urban estimates were 11.0% and 7.7%, compared with 8.4% and 5.2% in rural children. These patterns are important counterexamples to a simplistic explanation based only on activity: boys generally reported more MVPA, yet also had more excess weight. [10]

**The baseline judgment is therefore robust.** Chinese youth commonly accumulated too little MVPA and substantial sedentary exposure, against a backdrop of elevated overweight and obesity; no single behavioral statistic explains the whole weight-status pattern.

The underlying 2017 systematic review included **42 English-language papers published during 2002–2016**; **79% were cross-sectional**, only **9 studies were longitudinal**, **35 used questionnaires**, and **9 used objective methods** such as accelerometers, pedometers, or heart-rate monitoring [33]. Questionnaire validity was considered limited in most studies. The review generally found low activity, particularly in larger studies. Boys were consistently more active than girls; suburban or rural children generally reported less activity than urban children; parental activity was positively associated with children’s activity; and adolescent self-efficacy was positively associated with activity. Family socioeconomic status and parental education were not consistently associated [33]. These are exposure-status findings, not quantified evidence of health effects.

**Weight status and trends**

An older Chinese BMI norm used **P85 for overweight and P95 for obesity**, based on more than **244,200 Han students aged 7–18 years** in the 2000 Chinese National Survey on Students’ Constitution and Health. Its validation samples included Beijing, Henan, and Sichuan; at age 18, the corresponding BMI cut-points were **24 for overweight and 28 for obesity** [35]. In the reported 2000 regional samples, male/female overweight prevalence was **17.00%/9.46% in Beijing**, **10.86%/6.64% in Henan**, and **6.95%/4.23% in Sichuan**; obesity prevalence was **9.99%/6.47%**, **4.27%/3.07%**, and **2.84%/2.09%**, respectively [35]. These results cover ages 7–18 and are geographically stratified validation data, not an exact national 6–17 estimate.

**Scope and unresolved status fields**

The activity-factor review used an inclusion rule of Chinese children or adolescents living in China aged **3–18 years**, or with a mean age in that range; its child and adolescent categories were therefore broader than the requested operational 6–11 and 12–17 subgroups [33]. The planned 6–11 and 12–17 rows should remain **not separately reported**, rather than being filled from the broader 3–12 and 13–18 categories. A mainland-China intervention review included **76 studies and 72,620 students aged 6–19 years**; interventions lasted at least three months, and Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan were excluded [36]. A 2017 abstract identifies associations among physical activity, sedentary behavior, and fitness in Chinese children and adolescents, but the supplied record gives no exact age range, geography, sampling frame, or estimate [37].

Later records do not repair the pre-2020 gaps. A July 2020 accelerometer meta-analysis identifies the topic but does not specify the requested age range or inclusion rules and is outside the cutoff [31]. A 2024 urban-Chongqing validation study involved adolescents aged 12–19 years recruited in 2023; it retrospectively reports **13% meeting guidelines in 2018 and 14% in 2022 among ages 9–17**, plus PAQ-A instrument details, but it is post-cutoff and not an exact 6–17 status source [32]. A separate 2024 policy review describes Chinese youth as roughly 6–19 years and selected 384 policy documents, but its policy synthesis and reported obesity changes among ages 7–22 are also outside the historical evidence boundary [38]. The separate Chinese physical-activity guideline’s target ages, policy status, and domain-specific recommendations are not established by the eligible extracts here; only the frequently used **≥60 min/day MVPA** criterion and the Chinese BMI classification guidance are directly documented [3,4].

**Measurement-sensitive status estimates**

Earlier estimates are not directly comparable: a 2010 national survey of 166,812 children found 22.7% reporting any physical activity for ≥60 min/day; accelerometry found 9% of urban boys, 2% of urban girls, and 6% of urban 9–17-year-olds meeting 60 min/day. [42] A 2010–2012 national nutrition survey directly covered ages 6–17, but the supplied record reports neither daily MVPA or sedentary time nor its denominator. [39] Self-report may overestimate activity through recall and social-desirability bias; PAFCTYS was cross-sectional, single-season and non-probability sampled, with in-school and out-of-school MVPA not separated. [42] The meta-analysis describes status, not causation. [40]

A China-focused review searched child/adolescent/student/minor and China/Chinese terms; it excluded several cross-sectional reports without free text, including Shanghai transport activity (2017), a national student survey (2010), and a 9–22-year study (2014), and supplied no obesity trend. Named activities included basketball, soccer, baseball, dodgeball, football, volleyball, rhythmic gymnastics and jump-rope. [41]

**Weight-status definitions and prevention signal**

China’s WS/T 586—2018 standard applies nationwide to children and adolescents aged 6–18 and uses sex- and age-specific BMI cutoffs [226]. Overweight is BMI at or above the overweight cutoff but below the obesity cutoff; obesity is BMI at or above the obesity cutoff. Height and weight must be measured rather than self-reported, BMI is calculated as kg/m² and retained to one decimal place, and age is recorded in half-year increments [226]. Combined “overweight/obesity” therefore cannot substitute for either separate endpoint.

Pre-cutoff Chinese evidence mainly supports an association, not causal prevention. A prevalence review reported that 29.9%–34.1% of Chinese youth met a threshold of at least 60 minutes of MVPA per day, while 65.4% reported no more than 2 hours of sedentary behavior daily; estimates varied substantially with sample, instrument, and cut-point [165]. The same sample produced MVPA-compliance estimates of 29.9% and 13.1% when IPAQ and HBSC questionnaires were used, respectively; IPAQ was validated in Chinese people older than 15 years, making recall bias in younger children a concern [165]. Most included Chinese studies were cross-sectional, so favorable activity–adiposity patterns do not demonstrate that activity prevented overweight or obesity [165].

For context, five national survey cycles covering 29 provinces and 1,054,602 Chinese participants aged 7–18 years used WHO classifications; combined overweight-plus-obesity prevalence rose from 5.3% (95% CI 5.2–5.4) in 1995 to 20.5% (20.4–20.7) in 2014—an observed increase of 15.2 percentage points [227]. The 2010–2012 CNNHS estimated overweight prevalence at 9.6% among Chinese 6–17-year-olds, higher than in 2002, although the earlier comparator is not stated in the supplied span [46]. These trends establish need, not an independent physical-activity effect.

The result-bearing trials therefore address **improvement or containment of existing excess weight**, not primary prevention in initially healthy-weight children. Their measurable pathways were central adiposity and metabolic markers; no supplied study tested mediation, adverse events, equity effects, or long-term maintenance beyond the short follow-up. A later Chinese 24-hour-movement study reported 5.12% guideline attainment and 22.44% combined overweight/obesity, with lower odds among adherent participants, but it was published in May 2021 and cross-sectional; it is excluded from the early-2020 synthesis [216]. Likewise, later reviews and a preschool thesis are contextual leads only, not eligible evidence for Chinese 6–17-year-olds [20, 298, 560].

**Decision:** physical activity is a plausible and sometimes effective component of weight improvement, especially when supervised and sustained, but early-2020 evidence does not establish a single preventive dose or a reliably superior intervention type.

An urban Chinese 10-month school program (2015; Tianjin, Neijiang, Dalian) randomized 1,491 junior-high students (PA+health education n=720; education-only n=771). In obese students, BMI fell from 30.1±5.8 to 28.9±3.4 kg/m² (−1.2), versus −0.5 kg/m² in controls; sedentary time fell 0.1 h/weekday. Benefit was reported for boys, not girls; this was multicomponent, not PA-only evidence [255].

A 2019 Youth Study update found fewer than one-third meeting MVPA recommendations and high overweight/obesity, varying by sex, grade, and residence; a longitudinal study linked sedentary behavior to BMI increases at the 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles from ages 9–15 independently of MVPA, although geography was unstated [220].

Later evidence is excluded: the national study published 10 August 2020 (15,100 normal-weight ages 7–18) reported 13.2–15.6% lower incident overweight/obesity risk per added 30 min/day VPA and 16.1% lower risk when sedentary time was replaced by VPA [256]. The 2022–26 reports are likewise post-cutoff [68][231][242][221].

## Definition of physical-activity intensity

### Conventional MET categories provide a useful starting point

**Low, moderate, and vigorous describe effort.** The conventional categories used in the 2017 Chinese guideline were approximately 1.5–2.9 METs for low-intensity activity, 3.0–5.9 METs for moderate activity, and at least 6.0 METs for high-intensity or vigorous activity. “Light” and “low-intensity” are generally equivalent terms here; “vigorous” is the usual English counterpart of “high-intensity.” [4]

| Category | Conventional operational band | Typical perceived effort | Examples |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Sedentary behavior | ≤1.5 METs, while sitting/reclining awake | Very little effort | Seated television viewing or homework |
| Low/light activity | >1.5 to <3 METs | Easy; slight breathing increase | Slow walking, gentle household activity |
| Moderate activity | 3 to <6 METs | Noticeable effort; conversation remains possible | Brisk walking, ordinary cycling |
| High/vigorous activity | ≥6 METs | Markedly increased breathing and heart rate | Fast running, energetic football or basketball |

**A MET expresses relative energy cost.** Conventionally, one adult metabolic equivalent represents resting oxygen consumption of 3.5 mL of oxygen per kilogram of body mass per minute, approximately equivalent to 1 kcal/kg/hour. An activity assigned four adult METs therefore has an estimated metabolic rate four times that conventional resting reference. [11]

**Intensity is not an activity name.** Walking can be light or moderate depending on speed and circumstances; cycling and ball games can span moderate and vigorous levels. Likewise, vigorous activity does not necessarily mean an all-out sprint or a formal high-intensity interval-training protocol. The relevant exposure is the effort and duration actually experienced by the child.

### Youth METs address children’s changing metabolism

**Children are not small adults.** Resting metabolism per kilogram is higher in young children and declines with growth. The Youth Compendium gives an approximate basal oxygen-consumption rate of 6.5 mL/kg/min in a typical six-year-old, approaching the adult reference of 3.5 mL/kg/min around age eighteen. Applying the adult reference unchanged can therefore misrepresent a child’s relative metabolic demand. [11]

**METy uses a pediatric denominator.** The youth metabolic equivalent divides an activity-specific metabolic rate by estimated pediatric basal metabolic rate, using compatible units:

\[
\mathrm{MET_y}=\frac{\text{activity-specific metabolic rate}}{\text{estimated pediatric basal metabolic rate}}
\]

**Age-specific values improve interpretation.** The 2018 Youth Compendium covered 196 activities in 16 categories, drawing on 137 pediatric studies and more than 37,000 observations. It provided values for ages 6–9, 10–12, 13–15, and 16–18. Basal metabolism was estimated using age-, sex-, and body-mass-specific Schofield equations. [11]

**The categories remain operational conventions.** Applying light 1.51–2.99, moderate 3.00–5.99, and vigorous at least 6.00 to youth-adjusted values gives a clear working scheme corresponding to the familiar conventional bands. It should not be mistaken for a universally validated pediatric physiological boundary. The early-2020 evidence supported age-sensitive energy-cost estimation more securely than one universally agreed set of youth cut points.

**Absolute and relative intensity differ.** MET-based classification concerns metabolic cost relative to a reference. Relative intensity concerns how demanding an activity is for a particular child. The same running task can represent a different fraction of maximum capacity in a highly trained adolescent and an initially inactive child. This distinction helps explain why studies sometimes prescribed exercise using heart rate, oxygen uptake, or perceived exertion instead of METs.

### Heart rate and perceived exertion help operationalize intensity

**Perceived effort offers practical guidance.** The 2017 Chinese guideline associated light activity with approximately 10–11 on the Borg 6–20 rating-of-perceived-exertion scale, moderate activity with 12–14, and vigorous activity with 15 or above. Descriptions of breathing and ease of speaking can help children and teachers interpret these categories without laboratory equipment. [4]

**Heart-rate rules are study-specific.** For example, the Beijing school intervention operationalized supervised MVPA as 64%–94% of age-predicted maximum heart rate, using maximum heart rate = 220 − age. The Chinese guideline also supplied approximate heart-rate and exertion categories. These approaches describe practical exercise prescriptions; they are not validated one-to-one conversions into METy. [12]

**Energy cost does not capture everything.** A muscle-strengthening exercise can provide substantial local resistance without maintaining a high whole-body oxygen demand. Similarly, jumping can load bone strongly despite occupying only a short fraction of the day. A rigorous report therefore specifies activity type as well as intensity, frequency, and duration.

**The intensity judgment is straightforward.** Use the conventional light, moderate, and vigorous bands to communicate clearly, but use pediatric calibration and the original study’s measurement rule when interpreting scientific results.

## Overall physical and mental health

### Physical benefits extend beyond body weight

**Physical activity improves functional health.** The broad pediatric evidence available by early 2020 supported better cardiorespiratory fitness, stronger muscles, greater bone mineral accumulation, and smaller increases in adiposity with higher activity. These are distinct benefits: an intervention can improve endurance or muscular performance even when BMI changes little. [3]

**Chinese experiments support that distinction.** The Chinese Childhood Health, Activity and Motor Performance Study—Chinese CHAMPS—demonstrated improvements in endurance and several other fitness outcomes after an eight-month school intervention. Its contribution was stronger than a simple comparison between active and inactive students because schools were randomized to different intervention conditions. [13]

**Several mechanisms operate simultaneously.** Repeated aerobic activity challenges oxygen delivery and utilization; resistance and movement practice train muscular force and coordination; impact and weight-bearing activities load the skeleton. Activity also contributes to energy expenditure and can alter body composition. These mechanisms explain why one general activity exposure can influence several health outcomes, while also explaining why different activities are not interchangeable.

**Health should not become a weight score.** A child who runs farther, performs movements more competently, or develops greater muscular endurance has gained function even if body mass rises during normal growth. Conversely, a lower BMI does not by itself demonstrate improved strength, fitness, or psychological well-being. The appropriate overall-health judgment considers these outcomes separately.

### Mental-health benefits were favorable but modest

**Intervention effects were small on average.** A 2019 systematic review included 114 articles: four randomized trials, fourteen nonrandomized intervention studies, twenty-eight prospective studies, and sixty-eight cross-sectional studies. Twelve intervention studies entered its meta-analysis, producing a standardized mental-health effect of 0.173, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.106–0.239. [14]

**This was not a percentage improvement.** A standardized effect of 0.173 describes a small difference relative to variation in the measured outcomes; it does not mean that depression fell by 17.3% or that 17.3% of participants recovered. Between-study heterogeneity was relatively low, with I² = 11.3%; I² describes the proportion of variation in study estimates attributed to heterogeneity beyond sampling variation.

**Age-specific findings were important.** The intervention effect was statistically significant among adolescents aged 12–18 but not among children aged 6–11. This supports a more confident claim of modest adolescent benefit than of an equally established effect in younger children. However, significance in one subgroup and not another does not itself prove that the subgroup effects differ. [14]

**Observational findings covered several outcomes.** Greater activity was associated with less depression, stress, negative affect, and psychological distress, and with better self-image, life satisfaction, happiness, and well-being. Greater sedentary behavior showed the opposite pattern for depression and several positive well-being measures. These observations are consistent with the intervention signal, although they do not establish that activity alone produced the differences. [14]

**Psychosocial mechanisms are plausible.** Enjoyable activity can create opportunities for mastery, social contact, cooperation, and a sense of competence. The Chinese guideline emphasized self-esteem, relationships with peers and adults, and reductions in anxiety, tension, and depressive symptoms. These pathways are especially relevant to school programs, but their contribution depends on how activity is delivered: a supportive game and a humiliating fitness test are not psychologically equivalent. [4]

**Chinese relevance requires careful attribution.** Chinese surveillance established the behavioral setting—low activity, high sedentary exposure, and academic pressures—while the pooled numerical mental-health estimate came from the broader international pediatric literature. It is best interpreted as evidence of a likely modest co-benefit, not as a measured national Chinese treatment effect.

### Insufficient activity and prolonged sitting are distinct concerns

**Inactivity means insufficient recommended activity.** A child can be classified as insufficiently active despite walking and moving during the day if those activities do not provide the recommended MVPA. Sedentary behavior, by contrast, means waking activity performed sitting, reclining, or lying with very low energy expenditure. These definitions describe different dimensions of the day.

**An active child can still sit extensively.** Meeting a 60-minute MVPA target does not describe the remaining waking hours. Equally, replacing sitting with light movement may reduce sedentary exposure without satisfying the MVPA recommendation. Consequently, promoting activity and reducing prolonged sitting are complementary rather than redundant objectives. [4]

**Screen exposure carried the clearest concerns.** The Chinese guideline summarized a review of 232 studies involving 983,840 young people that associated more than two hours/day of television with unfavorable body composition, lower fitness, lower self-esteem, and poorer behavioral or academic indicators. The early-2020 international guideline review likewise found more consistent adverse evidence for screen time than for all objectively measured sedentary time. [4][15]

**Context changes the interpretation of sitting.** Homework, reading, and recreational television all involve little movement, but their educational and psychological meanings differ. A two-hour recreational-screen recommendation should therefore not be applied as a two-hour ceiling on all classroom sitting or reading. The Chinese context particularly supports movement breaks during prolonged study, alongside protecting time for MVPA.

**Benefits outweigh ordinary exercise risks.** The 2017 guideline recommended gradual increases in activity for inactive children, age-appropriate instruction, suitable equipment, and safe environments. This supports participation while recognizing injury prevention and individual capability. Its guidance also accommodated environmental conditions such as poor air quality rather than assuming that all activity must occur outdoors.

**The overall-health judgment is positive.** Regular, enjoyable activity is a broad health resource, with modest mental-health benefits and substantial functional benefits; neither low body weight nor one daily exercise session captures the whole objective.

By early 2020, evidence supported a generally favorable activity–health relationship, but not a precise China-wide causal effect for ages 6–17. Mental health. A Hong Kong Chinese adolescent study examined whether activity improves mental health through resilience, but the supplied record gives no magnitude; it supports a plausible pathway, not a quantified causal effect [33]. Sedentary behavior is distinct from low activity. ## Cardiorespiratory and cardiometabolic health

### Cardiorespiratory fitness has the strongest intervention evidence

**Increasing MVPA improves aerobic fitness.** The 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee synthesized fifteen reviews of cardiorespiratory fitness and rated the evidence strong that increased MVPA increases fitness in children and adolescents. The Chinese guideline summarized endurance-training improvements in maximal oxygen uptake of approximately 5%–15%. [3][4]

**Chinese randomized evidence is directly relevant.** Chinese CHAMPS randomized twelve middle schools to usual practice, an after-school program, enhanced school PE, or the combination. The intervention ran for eight months in 2015–2016. Of 758 enrolled seventh-grade students, 680 completed both assessments; their mean baseline age was 12.66 years. [13]

| CHAMPS condition | Completers | Scheduled activity target | Change in 20-m shuttle run |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Usual-practice control | 170 | 90 min/week | +3.52 laps |
| After-school program | 180 | 180 min/week | +12.38 laps |
| Enhanced school PE | 162 | 210 min/week | +17.85 laps |
| Enhanced PE plus after-school activity | 168 | 300 min/week | +25.78 laps |

Source: Chinese CHAMPS intervention and fitness results [13]. Scheduled targets represent the intervention design, not directly measured MVPA achieved by every participant.

**The adjusted comparison favored intervention.** Pooling the three intervention conditions, the trial estimated 15.2 additional shuttle-run laps relative to control, with a 95% confidence interval of 12.3–18.2 laps. The combined program had the largest observed improvement, followed by enhanced PE and then after-school activity alone. This establishes a useful Chinese example of program efficacy rather than merely an association between active students and fitness.

**Program quality mattered alongside time.** Enhanced PE included curriculum redesign, teacher training, portable equipment, game-based instruction, and intermittent conditioning exercises. Observations showed more active class time and less time spent managing classes. Thus, the trial supports improving the content and delivery of activity opportunities, not simply adding nominal lessons to a timetable. [13]

**Fitness is an adaptation, not a behavior.** Cardiorespiratory fitness describes the cardiovascular and respiratory systems’ capacity to supply oxygen during sustained exercise. Maximal oxygen uptake, usually expressed in mL/kg/min, is a physiological measure; a 20-m shuttle run is a field-performance proxy. Activity is the exposure that can improve fitness, but growth, body size, inherited characteristics, skill, and motivation also influence the measured result.

**Repeated aerobic demand explains the benefit.** Running, cycling, swimming, and sustained active games repeatedly challenge oxygen transport and muscular oxygen utilization. Adaptation improves the capacity to perform prolonged work. This mechanism makes endurance outcomes particularly responsive when programs deliver adequate effort consistently.

**Training doses were effective, not uniquely optimal.** The Chinese guideline summarized twenty-two controlled studies using three to four sessions/week, thirty to sixty minutes/session, above 80% of maximum heart rate, for one to three months. These protocols demonstrated that fitness could improve; they did not establish that every child needs that exact prescription or should begin at that intensity. The general daily recommendation also includes accumulated activity outside formal training.

### Blood pressure responds differently across populations

**Blood-pressure benefits were context-dependent.** Broader pediatric evidence supported favorable blood-pressure effects, especially in some higher-risk children, but universal school interventions often produced little average change. A Cochrane review summarized in the advisory evidence portfolio found fitness improvements more consistently than reductions in systolic or diastolic pressure. [3]

**A targeted trial demonstrates physiological potential.** In Geneva, forty-four prepubertal children aged 6–11 with obesity were randomized to exercise or control. The program provided three supervised sixty-minute sessions/week for three months, combining aerobic activity, strengthening, and cool-down. Between-group changes in office and 24-hour blood pressure favored exercise. [16]

**The reported changes were clinically meaningful.** Within the exercise group, office systolic pressure fell 2.0% and diastolic pressure 4.1%; 24-hour systolic and diastolic pressure fell 4.9% and 3.2%. The authors reported three-month treatment effects of −1.2 and −0.5 standard-deviation scores for 24-hour systolic and diastolic pressure. These are results from a small Swiss obesity-clinic population, not national Chinese estimates.

**Baseline risk helps explain differing results.** Children with elevated pressure have more scope for reduction than children beginning within a healthy range. Exercise may also affect vascular regulation and autonomic function as well as adiposity. The Geneva investigators found that the intervention remained associated with blood-pressure changes after accounting for changes in body weight or fat, supporting pathways beyond weight reduction.

**Chinese evidence supports a qualified conclusion.** The Chinese guideline included improved systolic pressure among expected cardiovascular benefits, while Chinese observational work examined pressure within clustered metabolic risk. The appropriate early-2020 judgment is that blood pressure is a plausible beneficial outcome, particularly in higher-risk youth, but a uniform numerical reduction should not be assigned to all Chinese 6–17-year-olds.

### Blood-lipid evidence was favorable overall but not uniformly positive

**The strongest broad signal concerned triglycerides.** The advisory committee synthesized nine cardiometabolic reviews, rating the overall evidence moderate but the evidence for triglycerides and insulin strong. The Chinese guideline also summarized favorable findings for triglycerides, cholesterol, and HDL cholesterol. [3]

**The Beijing intervention showed no lipid separation.** Its twelve-week program measured total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides. None showed a statistically significant between-group difference, despite improvements in activity, BMI, skinfolds, and fasting glucose. [12]

| Lipid measure | Generally favorable direction | Beijing trial, twelve weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Triglycerides | Lower concentration | No significant between-group change |
| Total cholesterol | Lower elevated concentration | No significant between-group change |
| LDL cholesterol | Lower elevated concentration | No significant between-group change |
| HDL cholesterol | Higher concentration | No significant between-group change |

**Lipid changes are not interchangeable.** A decrease in LDL and an increase in HDL generally point in favorable directions, so saying that “cholesterol fell” can conceal important distinctions. In the Geneva trial, HDL fell within the initial exercise arm rather than rising, and between-group biochemical differences were not significant. Fitness improvement therefore should not be described as proof that every lipid fraction improved. [16]

**The implication is marker-specific interpretation.** Activity remains justified for cardiovascular health, but lipid outcomes depend on baseline concentrations, intervention content, and duration. The Beijing findings particularly argue against promising detectable lipid improvement from every short school program.

### Glucose and insulin outcomes provide a Chinese intervention signal

**Fasting glucose improved in Beijing.** The nonrandomized trial included 921 children aged 7–15 in four public schools, comparing a multicomponent activity program with usual practice. After twelve weeks, the adjusted between-group difference in fasting glucose was −0.19 mmol/L, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.24 to −0.15 mmol/L. [12]

**The contrast included control-group deterioration.** Fasting glucose changed by −0.03 mmol/L in the intervention group and +0.17 mmol/L in controls. The adjusted −0.19 mmol/L estimate should therefore be understood as a relative difference in change, not a claim that intervention participants individually reduced glucose by that amount.

**Muscle activity supports glucose handling.** Contracting muscles use glucose, while repeated training can improve insulin sensitivity. This gives activity a pathway to better glycemic regulation that does not require a large change in BMI. The Chinese trial is consistent with that pathway, although its combined program and nonrandomized allocation do not isolate one mechanism.

**Glucose and insulin are different endpoints.** Normal fasting glucose can coexist with compensatory insulin secretion, so unchanged glucose does not necessarily mean unchanged insulin sensitivity. Conversely, a small fasting-glucose change alone does not establish reduced future diabetes incidence. The broader evidence’s stronger insulin signal is therefore complementary to, rather than identical with, the Beijing glucose finding.

**Counterexamples prevent overgeneralization.** The Geneva randomized phase showed no reduction in glucose, insulin, or the HOMA-IR insulin-resistance index during its first three months. Changes emerged later during a modified crossover phase. Together, the Beijing and Geneva results show that metabolic markers can respond on different schedules and need not improve simultaneously. [16]

### Fitness and body fat jointly shape metabolic risk

**Chinese observational results show overlapping influences.** Wang and colleagues studied 676 Wuhan schoolchildren, mean age 9.6 years, in 2010. Fitness was assessed using a 20-m multistage test, and a metabolic-risk score combined mean arterial pressure, HDL, triglycerides, and glucose. Fitness was inversely associated with risk in a separate regression model, but only body fat remained significant when both were included together. [17]

**That result has competing explanations.** Adiposity may account for part of the apparent fitness association; it may also lie on a pathway through which activity affects health. Additionally, expressing oxygen uptake relative to body weight creates mathematical overlap with adiposity measures. The study therefore does not establish that fitness is irrelevant or that fat reduction is the only useful goal.

**The cardiovascular judgment favors fitness first.** Aerobic fitness improvement is strongly established and directly supported by Chinese randomized evidence; favorable blood pressure, lipid, and glycemic changes are important additional outcomes whose magnitude depends on the population and intervention.

## Musculoskeletal health

### Bone-loading activity increases mineral accumulation

**Exercise produces measurable skeletal gains.** Specker, Thiex, and Sudhagoni’s 2015 review included twenty-two controlled trials, fifteen randomized and seven observational, involving healthy children aged 3–18. Interventions lasted three to thirty-six months. Exercise groups accumulated more bone mineral content and areal bone mineral density than controls at several sites. [18]

The pooled exercise–control differences in percentage change were +0.8% for total-body bone mineral content, +1.5% for femoral-neck bone mineral content, and +1.7% for spine bone mineral content; corresponding areal bone-mineral-density differences were +0.6% at the femoral neck and +1.2% at the spine. These data concerned healthy children aged 3–18.

| Bone outcome | Exercise–control difference in percentage change | 95% confidence interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total-body bone mineral content | +0.8 percentage points | +0.3 to +1.3 |
| Femoral-neck bone mineral content | +1.5 percentage points | +0.5 to +2.5 |
| Spine bone mineral content | +1.7 percentage points | +0.4 to +3.1 |
| Femoral-neck areal bone mineral density | +0.6 percentage points | +0.2 to +1.1 |
| Spine areal bone mineral density | +1.2 percentage points | +0.6 to +1.8 |

**These measures answer different questions.** Bone mineral content is the amount of mineral measured at a skeletal site. Areal bone mineral density, commonly obtained using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, expresses mineral relative to projected bone area. Bone geometry and resistance to bending add further information about strength. An improvement in one measure is not automatically an equivalent improvement in every other skeletal property.

**The reported gains exceed normal growth.** Children in both exercise and control groups naturally accumulated bone. The table describes the additional difference in percentage change between groups, not their entire gain and not a change of 0.8–1.7 g/cm². These results provide international quantitative reference points for Chinese programs, rather than a Chinese pooled effect estimate.

**Mechanical loading explains the specificity.** Bone adapts to forces generated by impact, weight-bearing, and muscular contraction. Running, landing, jumping, and changing direction create loading patterns that differ from swimming. This is why an activity’s cardiovascular intensity alone does not fully predict its skeletal effect. [18]

### Activity types differ in their skeletal effects

**Jumping had a broadly favorable record.** Gómez-Bruton and colleagues’ review included twenty-six jumping-intervention studies in participants younger than eighteen. Twenty-four reported favorable bone mineral density, mineral content, or structural outcomes; two reported no bone-mass effect after interventions lasting ten weeks and nine months. The result supports jumping as an effective loading approach without implying universal success. [19]

| Activity type | Dominant skeletal stimulus | Evidence through early 2020 | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jumping and plyometric exercise | Repeated landing impacts | Mostly favorable mineral outcomes | Useful targeted bone-loading activity |
| Gymnastics | Impact and varied weight-bearing | Favorable bone-geometry comparisons | Strong loading stimulus |
| Football/soccer | Running, impacts, directional changes | Generally favorable mineral and geometry findings | Combines aerobic and skeletal demands |
| Swimming | Muscular work with reduced weight-bearing | Less favorable skeletal comparisons | Valuable aerobic activity, not an equivalent impact substitute |
| Resistance exercise | Muscle-generated loading | Supports muscular fitness and can load bone | Complements impact activity |

Sources: Jumping review [19], sports and bone-geometry review [20], and pediatric bone and muscular-fitness synthesis [3].

**Swimming illustrates outcome-specific benefit.** A swimmer may have excellent aerobic fitness without the skeletal loading produced by football or gymnastics. The sports review found swimmers’ bone results less favorable than those of several weight-bearing sports, with some comparisons favoring controls. This does not establish that swimming damages bone; it shows why swimming alone should not be assumed to deliver an equivalent bone-strengthening stimulus.

**Geometry findings were less uniform.** A 2018 review of twenty-one articles found generally favorable active–inactive differences, but its two jumping studies showed no bone-geometry benefit. This is not necessarily inconsistent with the twenty-four positive jumping studies: mineral accumulation and geometric adaptation are related but distinct endpoints, assessed in different studies. [20]

**Pubertal stage altered the response.** In the 2015 pooled analysis, significant mineral-content benefits were concentrated in prepubertal children; the additional spine gain was 3.7 percentage points, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.8–6.6. Some individual studies favored early puberty, and later-pubertal pooled results were not significant. The practical implication is to establish varied loading activity before and through puberty, not to regard a particular birthday as a universal biological deadline. [18]

**Nutrition can modify loading benefits.** All three factorial trials specifically examining calcium–exercise interaction found greater leg-mineral gains when exercise was combined with calcium supplementation than with placebo. Exercise–control differences ranged from 1.5% to 3.7% in those comparisons. These findings support attention to nutritional adequacy alongside activity, rather than a general instruction that all children require supplements. [18]

**Bone accrual is not fracture prevention.** Some jumping-related gains persisted for several years, but those results concerned maintained bone measurements. They should not be converted into a numerical reduction in childhood fractures or later osteoporosis. The immediate conclusion is improved skeletal development, with longer-term clinical benefit depending on persistence and subsequent exposure.

### Resistance activity improves muscular performance

**Muscular fitness is a separate benefit.** The broad pediatric evidence rated increased resistance exercise as strongly beneficial for muscular fitness. The Chinese guideline identified strength, endurance, muscle development, coordination, and motor skills among expected benefits and recommended strengthening activity on at least three days per week. [3]

**Chinese trial results quantify functional gains.** Chinese CHAMPS provides direct evidence for improvements in muscular-performance proxies. Compared with control, the pooled intervention conditions produced the following adjusted differences after eight months:

| Functional outcome | Additional intervention improvement | 95% confidence interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Standing broad jump | +17.0 cm | +12.8 to +21.3 |
| Plank support | +31.8 seconds | +22.4 to +41.2 |
| One-minute sit-ups | +5.1 repetitions | +3.3 to +6.9 |

Source: Chinese CHAMPS muscular-performance results [13].

**Performance does not equal muscle mass.** Broad-jump distance reflects lower-limb power and coordination, while plank duration and sit-ups reflect aspects of endurance and task performance. These are meaningful functional gains, but they do not measure kilograms of new muscle or isolated maximal strength. A separate sixteen-trial school-exercise review found a lean-mass effect of SMD 0.159, with a confidence interval from −0.076 to 0.394, which crossed zero. [21]

**Strength can improve through several adaptations.** Better coordination and neuromuscular control can improve performance alongside changes in muscle tissue. Appropriate activities include body-weight movements, climbing, push-ups, elastic-band exercises, and progressively supervised resistance work. The objective is age-appropriate challenge and movement competence, not simply lifting the heaviest possible load.

**The musculoskeletal judgment favors a mixture.** Aerobic activity should be complemented by impact or weight-bearing movement and progressive strengthening, because cardiovascular effort, bone loading, muscular endurance, and muscle mass are not interchangeable outcomes.

## Brain, cognition, memory, and academic performance

### Brain-structure findings establish plausibility, not a universal effect

**Fitter adolescents showed structural differences.** Herting and Chu’s review described MRI studies in adolescents, principally ages 15–18, associating greater aerobic fitness with larger hippocampal and rostral middle-frontal volumes. A positive association with lingual-gyrus surface area varied according to BDNF genotype. These were predominantly small cross-sectional studies. [22]

**The hippocampus makes the finding relevant.** This brain region contributes to learning and memory, so its association with fitness provides a plausible connection between physical and cognitive development. However, a larger regional volume is not itself a standardized measure of better learning, and cross-sectional differences do not show that exercise enlarged that region.

**Different MRI measures require different interpretations.** The review also described white-matter diffusion and connectivity findings. Such measures should not be reduced to a rule that “more activation” or “a higher imaging value” always indicates better brain health. Development changes multiple properties simultaneously, and their meaning depends on the task, region, and imaging method.

**Biological mechanisms remained proposed pathways.** Reviews discussed neurogenesis, angiogenesis, central-nervous-system metabolism, and growth factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor, insulin-like growth factor-1, and vascular endothelial growth factor. Animal and experimental findings make these mechanisms plausible, but the pediatric intervention literature did not establish each pathway as the cause of improved school performance. [23]

**Chinese interpretation should remain outcome-specific.** For Chinese 6–17-year-olds, the international MRI findings support biological plausibility. The stronger direct Chinese evidence concerned physical fitness, while structural brain claims depended on extrapolation from other populations and, for some adolescent samples, an age range extending to eighteen.

### Executive function improves modestly in randomized trials

**Executive function has a positive intervention signal.** Xue, Yang, and Huang’s 2019 meta-analysis included nineteen randomized or cluster-randomized studies with 5,038 participants. Eligible ages were 6–12 for children and 13–17 for adolescents, matching the requested span. Chronic exercise improved overall executive function by SMD 0.20 and inhibitory control by SMD 0.26. [24]

| Outcome or intervention format | Standardized mean difference | 95% confidence interval | Result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Overall executive function | +0.20 | +0.09 to +0.30 | Small positive effect |
| Inhibitory control | +0.26 | +0.08 to +0.45 | Small positive effect |
| Sports and PA programs | +0.21 | +0.12 to +0.31 | Positive pooled effect |
| Curricular physical activity | +0.39 | +0.08 to +0.69 | Positive pooled effect |
| Integrated physical activity | +0.02 | −0.05 to +0.09 | No clear pooled improvement |

**Executive function is not one ability.** It includes processes such as inhibiting an inappropriate response, shifting between tasks, maintaining relevant information, and directing attention toward a goal. A benefit on inhibitory control should not automatically be described as increased intelligence, better long-term memory, or improved grades.

**Program content may influence the result.** Curricular activity and sports programs had favorable pooled estimates, whereas integrated activity did not show a clear effect. However, these were subgroups across different studies, not a single randomized head-to-head comparison. The results support attention to program format without proving that one format is universally superior.

**Cognitive demands offer a plausible explanation.** Singh and colleagues described an intervention in 10–12-year-olds in which cognitively demanding team games improved cognitive flexibility more than lower-cognitive-demand aerobic exercise. Updating and inhibition did not differ between groups. The contrast suggests that physical exertion and decision-making demands can contribute differently to specific cognitive outcomes. [23]

**Longer sessions were not consistently better.** In the executive-function meta-analysis, sessions shorter than ninety minutes had an effect of SMD 0.24, whereas sessions of at least ninety minutes had an estimate of 0.05 with a confidence interval crossing zero. This subgroup pattern does not establish a ninety-minute biological threshold, but it argues against assuming that longer sessions necessarily produce larger cognitive benefits. [24]

**Adolescent trials also showed counterexamples.** One four-month PE study in seventy 12–14-year-olds favored a more intensive program, while a separate twenty-week randomized school intervention found no inhibitory-control improvement. Device data suggested poor adherence in the latter. The contrast points toward the importance of delivered activity, but it does not isolate intensity from adherence or other program differences. [22]

### Memory findings were mixed

**Learning and memory should be separated.** Some adolescent studies associated exercise or aerobic fitness with faster spatial learning. Yet two studies found no corresponding difference in spatial-memory performance. Learning a task more quickly and retaining information after learning are related but distinct outcomes. [22]

**Brain activation did not guarantee better performance.** During an associative-learning fMRI task, lower-fit adolescent boys showed greater hippocampal activation than higher-fit boys despite equivalent task performance. The authors interpreted this as possibly requiring additional neural resources. It supports a hypothesis about processing efficiency, not a demonstrated improvement in remembered information.

**Working memory is another distinct endpoint.** It concerns holding and manipulating information over short periods, whereas spatial-memory and associative-learning tasks test different processes. Positive executive-function findings therefore cannot simply be substituted for a claim that every form of memory improves.

**Chinese CHAMPS contributes a useful distinction.** Its 2018 protocol specified fluid intelligence, attention control, and executive-function assessments. A protocol records intended measurements, not evidence that those outcomes improved. The 2019 report provides a strong Chinese physical-fitness example, while the numerical executive-function conclusions above come from the broader randomized-trial synthesis. [25][13]

### Academic outcomes were more heterogeneous

**Better grades were not a settled consequence.** Marques and colleagues reviewed fifty-one articles involving ages 6–18: forty-one cross-sectional, eight longitudinal, and two intervention studies. Fitness showed a more consistently favorable association with achievement than objectively measured activity did. [26]

| Exposure examined | Evidence summarized | Academic finding |
|---|---|---|
| Self-reported physical activity | 16 articles | 10 reported positive associations |
| Objectively measured activity | 11 studies | Inconsistent associations |
| Cardiorespiratory fitness | 22 studies | Generally favorable associations |

**Fitness and activity measure different things.** Fitness can reflect accumulated training and development over longer periods, whereas a short activity-monitoring window may not represent habitual exposure. Fitness also shares determinants with school performance, including health and social circumstances. Its stronger association therefore does not prove that increasing activity by a specified amount causes a corresponding grade increase.

**Higher-quality intervention findings remained mixed.** Singh and colleagues identified fifty-eight intervention studies, but only eleven met their high-quality threshold. Among analyses from those studies, fifteen of twenty-five academic outcomes showed significant benefit. Mathematics was more encouraging: six of seven outcomes across five studies favored activity. The review nevertheless judged overall academic evidence inconclusive. [23]

**A narrower benefit can coexist with uncertainty.** An activity program may improve attention or mathematics performance without changing reading scores, overall grade-point average, or examination rankings. Counting several positive outcomes from one study also does not turn them into independent replications. The strongest interpretation is domain-specific potential rather than a universal academic effect.

**Chinese school evidence was mainly associative.** The adolescent brain review described favorable activity–achievement relationships among Hong Kong high-school students. These observations are relevant Chinese-context evidence, but they are not equivalent to a mainland-China randomized estimate, and they do not establish that taking time from study for exercise necessarily raises marks. [22]

**Protecting PE remains scientifically reasonable.** Donnelly and colleagues’ 2016 systematic review characterized the overall academic effect of PE and activity as broadly neutral, while finding more favorable cognitive evidence. Together with the positive mathematics findings, this argues against removing activity solely to maximize classroom minutes, while avoiding a guarantee of higher grades. [27]

**The brain-and-learning judgment is calibrated.** Small executive-function benefits are supported by randomized evidence, structural and memory findings provide promising but mixed signals, and academic improvement should be treated as a possible co-benefit rather than the principal justification for activity.

## Overweight and obesity

### Prevention and treatment show different effects

**Physical activity helps, but context matters.** The broad evidence supports an association between greater activity and smaller increases in weight and adiposity during childhood. Intervention effects are less uniform, especially when the objective is preventing excess weight across an entire school rather than treating children who already have overweight or obesity. [3]

**Mainland-China evidence distinguishes those objectives.** Feng and colleagues reviewed seventy-six controlled school interventions involving 72,620 students aged 6–19. Forty studies targeted children with overweight or obesity; thirty-six enrolled children without restricting baseline weight status. Treatment studies more often showed a significant anthropometric benefit than prevention studies: 34/40, or 85.0%, versus 21/36, or 58.3%. [28]

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Prevention: PA only | 4 | +0.05 kg/m²; CI −0.04 to +0.15 | 2/7; 28.6% |
| Prevention: PA plus health education | 3 | −0.19 kg/m²; CI −0.27 to −0.11 | 15/19; 78.9% |
| Treatment: PA only | 8 | −0.91 kg/m²; CI −1.15 to −0.67 | 12/15; 80.0% |
| Treatment: PA plus health education | 5 | −1.80 kg/m²; CI −2.15 to −1.44 | 16/18; 88.9% |

Source: Feng and colleagues’ meta-analyses [28]. Confidence intervals are 95%; pooled estimates concern short-term interventions lasting three to twelve months.

**“Effective” described studies, not pupils.** The review classified a study as effective if at least one anthropometric outcome improved significantly relative to control. Thus, 78.9% does not mean that 78.9% of children lost weight or avoided obesity. The pooled BMI estimates are a different, more quantitative measure, and come from smaller subsets of the studies.

**Treatment offers greater scope for change.** Children beginning with excess adiposity may have more opportunity for measurable BMI reduction and may receive a more targeted exercise dose. Whole-school prevention includes many children growing normally, for whom stable healthy growth rather than weight loss is the goal. These differences help explain larger treatment effects without proving that treatment is the more important population strategy.

**Combined programs had stronger Chinese signals.** PA plus health education showed more favorable pooled estimates than PA alone in both prevention and treatment. However, these comparisons combined different studies, populations, and delivery methods. They support a preference for comprehensive programming, not a precise causal estimate of the additional benefit of education.

### A Beijing controlled trial separates incidence from remission

**The Beijing results favored prevention more clearly.** Li and colleagues studied 921 children aged 7–15 in four public schools, comparing a twelve-week multicomponent program with usual practice. The intervention included enhanced PE, extra activity for students with overweight or obesity, home activity, and health education for students and parents. [12]

|---|---:|---:|---|
| New combined overweight/obesity among initially unaffected students | 2.7% | 7.1% | Adjusted OR 0.34; CI 0.15–0.80 |
| Remission among initially overweight/obese students | 12.5% | 6.6% | p = 0.370 |
| BMI change | −0.02 kg/m² | +0.41 kg/m² | Adjusted difference −0.43 kg/m² |

Source: Beijing intention-to-treat results [12].

**Overweight prevention had a favorable signal.** The combined incidence endpoint was lower in the intervention group. The calculated absolute contrast was 4.4 percentage points, from 7.1 − 2.7. The adjusted odds ratio of 0.34 describes lower odds of the combined event; it should not be relabeled as an overweight-only incidence rate.

**Obesity prevention shares that combined endpoint.** The same result supports prevention of excess-weight onset generally, but it does not separate new obesity from new overweight or measure progression from overweight to obesity independently. The scientific conclusion is therefore favorable for the combined prevention outcome, with the category boundaries kept intact.

**Overweight improvement includes reduced adiposity gain.** BMI was 0.43 kg/m² lower relative to control after adjustment, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.63 to −0.23. Skinfold changes also favored intervention: −1.40 mm at the triceps, −1.26 mm subscapularly, and −0.69 mm abdominally. These measurements support improved adiposity-related outcomes, rather than relying only on crossing a diagnostic threshold. [12]

**Obesity improvement is not synonymous with remission.** Remission of the combined overweight/obesity category occurred in 12.5% versus 6.6%, but the difference was not significant. A child can reduce fatness or improve fitness while remaining above an obesity threshold, so nonsignificant remission does not negate all benefit. Conversely, the favorable BMI result should not be presented as proof of reliable normalization.

**The intervention changed several settings.** Schools provided three forty-five-minute PE lessons/week, targeting at least thirty minutes of MVPA per lesson. Students with overweight or obesity were encouraged to undertake additional supervised activity on at least three days/week, while home activity and parent engagement supported the daily target. Health education also included healthy eating; this was not an isolated test of exercise energy expenditure.

**Delivered activity explains part of the contrast.** Intervention students increased reported MVPA by 8.9 minutes/day while controls decreased it by 13.8 minutes/day. The adjusted +22.7-minute relative difference therefore reflected both improvement and protection against decline. This is practically important: maintaining activity during a school term can be beneficial even when the intervention group’s absolute increase is modest.

**Curricular structure and enjoyment were promising.** Programs incorporating curricular PA or emphasizing enjoyment had more favorable pooled estimates. Plausible explanations include protected activity time, improved participation, and greater adherence. Because these were cross-study subgroup comparisons, they do not establish that inserting an “enjoyment” component alone would reproduce the numerical difference.

**The Chinese and international findings can coexist.** The mainland review favored PA plus education, while the randomized international review did not demonstrate statistically significant superiority of all multicomponent programs over all single-component programs. The comparisons are not identical: “multicomponent” covers diverse combinations, and the studies differed in baseline risk and delivery. The shared conclusion is that effective content and implementation matter more than the component count alone.

**Quality-sensitive analyses temper large expectations.** In Feng and colleagues’ review, restricting multicomponent prevention analysis to moderate-quality studies produced an estimate of −0.50 kg/m², but its confidence interval, −1.14 to +0.14, crossed zero. This does not establish no effect; it shows that confidence in a stable prevention magnitude was weaker than the main pooled estimate might suggest. [28]

### Heterogeneity explains conflicting weight-intervention conclusions

**One large pooled estimate was unstable.** Mei and colleagues’ 2016 meta-analysis of eighteen school-based trials reported an overall estimate of −2.23, with a 90% confidence interval of −2.92 to −1.56 and I² = 99.8%. Its labeling mixed “standardized mean difference” with kg/m² units, so that figure should not be interpreted as a dependable 2.23 kg/m² treatment effect. [30]

**Sensitivity results were considerably smaller.** After excluding seven influential studies, the review reported a BMI-change difference of −0.13 kg/m² for PA alone, with a 90% confidence interval of −0.29 to +0.04, and −0.26 kg/m² for PA plus nutrition, with an interval of −0.30 to −0.09. Residual heterogeneity remained high, at I² = 89.8% and 92.1%, respectively.

**More scheduled minutes did not ensure more weight change.** Weekly doses of 36–100 minutes had a reported standardized estimate of −0.22, compared with −0.18 for doses above 100 minutes. This was not a simple longer-is-better gradient. Differences in actual intensity, attendance, compensatory behavior, and accompanying components can make scheduled duration an imperfect measure of effective exposure. [30]

**Null reviews are important counterevidence.** Mei and colleagues also discussed earlier reviews finding little BMI effect or no body-composition improvement despite activity programs. These findings do not undermine exercise’s fitness benefits. They show that increasing activity is not automatically sufficient to produce a detectable average weight change in every school population.

**Energy balance is dynamic during growth.** Activity raises energy expenditure, but food intake, activity elsewhere in the day, and growth can change simultaneously. Training can also increase lean tissue while reducing fat. Therefore, an exercise dose does not translate mechanically into a fixed number of kilograms lost.

**Chinese CHAMPS illustrates body-composition value.** Its combined PE-plus-after-school group reduced body-fat percentage by 1.9 percentage points, while controls increased by 1.3 percentage points over eight months. The adjusted pooled intervention contrast was −1.6 percentage points versus control. These results provide direct Chinese evidence beyond BMI, although the program also included education and family support. [13]

### Named intervention formats offer practical comparisons

**Several Chinese formats were already studied.** Their value lies in how they create and sustain appropriate activity, rather than in a universally proven ranking.

| Named format | Main activity approach | Reported schedule | Evidence-supported role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enhanced PE in the Beijing trial | More active lessons plus home and family support | 3 × 45-min lessons/week | Improved MVPA, BMI, skinfolds, and glucose |
| School Sports Prescription | Moderate aerobic activity plus strength and fitness work | 40–60 min, 2–5 times/week | Targeted overweight/obesity treatment |
| Happy 10 | Recreational activity during class breaks | Two 10-min sessions/day | Activity component in comprehensive prevention |
| Chinese CHAMPS | Enhanced PE, conditioning, games, and after-school activity | Eight months; after-school sessions 2 days/week | Improved aerobic and muscular fitness |

**School provision offers reach and structure.** PE and activity breaks can include children who would not independently join sports clubs. After-school sessions can supply additional supervised activity, particularly for children requiring a greater training dose. Family involvement can support activity beyond school, but the independent contribution of each setting is not established by a program that changes them together.

**Treatment should preserve growth and dignity.** For children with overweight or obesity, success can include slower excess-weight gain, lower fatness, improved metabolic markers, and better function—not only rapid weight loss. Universal activity opportunities also reduce the need to single out heavier students publicly, a concern explicitly recognized in the Beijing study’s discussion.

**The obesity judgment favors comprehensive support.** Physical activity is an essential part of prevention and treatment, with stronger average effects in targeted treatment and promising Chinese results for PA plus health education; neither exercise alone nor adding more components guarantees weight normalization.

## Scientific consensus and implications for a Chinese health initiative

### Confidence differs by outcome

**The consensus is outcome-specific, not uniform.** By early 2020, the strongest evidence concerned cardiorespiratory fitness and skeletal health, with muscular-fitness benefits also well supported. Greater activity was strongly associated with smaller increases in adiposity, but school-intervention BMI effects were heterogeneous. Mental-health and executive-function benefits were generally modest, while broad academic improvement remained an uncertain additional benefit. [3][24][23]

| Health target | Evidence-weighted judgment | Activity priority | Appropriate outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall physical health | Broad, consistent benefits | Daily accumulated activity | Participation and functional fitness |
| Mental health | Small average benefit; clearer adolescent signal | Enjoyable, supportive activity | Validated symptoms and well-being |
| Cardiorespiratory fitness | Strong; supported by Chinese randomized evidence | Progressive aerobic MVPA | Shuttle-run performance or measured oxygen uptake |
| Cardiometabolic health | Favorable but marker- and risk-dependent | Aerobic activity with broader healthy-lifestyle support | Blood pressure, lipids, glucose, insulin |
| Bone and muscle | Strong broader evidence; Chinese functional gains | Impact, weight-bearing, and resistance activity | Bone accrual and muscular performance |
| Cognition and learning | Small executive-function benefit; mixed academic effects | Sustained, engaging curricular or sports activity | Domain-specific tests and academic outcomes |
| Overweight and obesity | Prevention modest; treatment effects often larger | PA combined with education and appropriate dietary support | Growth-adjusted BMI, fatness, incidence, remission |

**Chinese causal evidence was stronger than surveillance alone.** Chinese CHAMPS demonstrated that a randomized school program could improve fitness. The Beijing study provided controlled evidence of program-associated improvements in BMI, skinfolds, and glucose. Neither result justifies attributing every change to activity alone, because both programs altered several features of children’s environments.

**Study design controls causal wording.** National cross-sectional surveys establish prevalence and associations. Prospective studies can establish temporal ordering but remain vulnerable to confounding. Randomized trials provide stronger causal evidence for the intervention actually delivered, while nonrandomized controlled studies provide a less secure comparison. Meta-analysis strengthens inference only to the extent that its included studies and outcome definitions support it.

**A common biological direction is credible.** Oxygen-delivery adaptation, muscle training, skeletal loading, and glucose use are not uniquely Chinese processes, so international pediatric evidence is relevant. However, school organization, pubertal timing, diet, baseline fitness, activity preferences, and environmental conditions can change the magnitude and practicality of the response. International estimates are therefore reference evidence, not automatic forecasts for Chinese students.

### The practical conclusion is to protect meaningful activity

**The recommendation is justified without academic promises.** At least sixty minutes/day of accumulated MVPA, with vigorous and strengthening activity on at least three days/week, was a defensible early-2020 public-health standard. It describes a target for healthy development, not a threshold below which activity has no benefit. Initially inactive children can benefit from gradual increases toward it.

**Quality should accompany protected time.** Chinese CHAMPS showed why trained teachers, active lessons, varied games, and after-school opportunities matter. A scheduled PE period is useful only insofar as it produces suitable participation and challenge. At the same time, skeletal and muscular goals require specific loading and resistance rather than aerobic minutes alone.

**Weight should be one outcome among several.** A health initiative should value increased activity, better endurance, improved strength, healthier body composition, and psychological well-being. This follows directly from the evidence that fitness gains can be clearer and more consistent than BMI changes. It also avoids judging normally growing children by an inappropriate expectation of weight loss.

**Stronger claims have identifiable conditions.** Claims of reliable weight-category remission, durable academic improvement, Chinese-specific brain-structure change, or a fixed reduction in every cardiovascular marker would require those particular outcomes to change consistently under comparable controlled conditions and persist beyond the intervention. Favorable fitness results alone do not establish those additional effects.

**The evidence should be read proportionately.** This synthesis includes evidence available through early 2020, including February publications; later-2020 and 2021 findings do not determine its conclusions. Age-band differences, self-report and device definitions, seasonal sampling, confounding, short follow-up, and intervention heterogeneity affect precision and transferability. Feng and colleagues rated 80.3% of mainland obesity-intervention studies weak quality. Standardized effects are dimensionless, BMI differences use kg/m², and bone results above are differences in percentage change; calculated contrasts and proxy ranges are identified explicitly. [9][28]

**The final judgment favors sustained participation.** Physical activity was already a well-supported investment in Chinese children’s and adolescents’ health by early 2020. Its strongest promise was better physical capacity and healthier development, with additional mental, metabolic, cognitive, and weight-related benefits that should be described and assessed on their own terms.
