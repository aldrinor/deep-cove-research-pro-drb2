# Memory, Trauma, and Postcolonial Politics in Eastern Europe: Kõresaar, Aleksievich, and Husanović

**Memory is a struggle over authority.** Ene Kõresaar, Svetlana Aleksievich, and Jasmina Husanović show how accounts of historical violence establish identities, authorize political claims, and determine whose suffering receives recognition. Kõresaar’s Boris Takk case reveals how Waffen-SS service, anti-Soviet resistance, and subsequent Communist Party membership can be narrated as a continuous commitment to Estonian freedom. His acknowledgment of Jewish persecution coexists with a Soviet-centered interpretation that limits the Holocaust’s independent significance within his life story. [1]

**The alternatives are not interchangeable.** Aleksievich’s *War's Unwomanly Face* reconstructs women’s wartime experiences through an edited chorus of testimonies that challenges the masculine Soviet victory narrative. Husanović’s “politics of witnessing and hope” moves from recovering testimony to asking whether survivors can retain political agency and create solidarity, equality, and different futures. Together, these perspectives make postcolonial analysis useful not simply for identifying domination, but also for examining what narratives of liberation recognize, subordinate, or exclude. [2][3]

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Takk through Kõresaar; Estonia | Veteran organizing; 1993 memoir; public advocacy | Biography reconciled through “prolonged rupture” | National continuity across Nazi and Soviet domination | Recognition preserves ethical contradictions |
| Aleksievich; Soviet World War II | Interviews assembled into a 1985 polyphonic book | Gendered, belated, mediated recollection | Counter-memory within and against Soviet identity | More voices do not ensure equal recognition |
| Husanović; postwar Bosnia | Witnessing, memorialization, art, education, activism | Embodied testimony contested by institutions | Resistance to ethnonational and governing hierarchies | Hope requires continuing political agency |

**The report proceeds in two parts.** Part One follows Takk’s life, his memory entrepreneurship, his freedom narrative, his treatment of the Holocaust, and Kõresaar’s interpretation of the dominant postcolonial framework. Part Two examines Aleksievich’s testimonial method and Husanović’s connected arguments about witnessing and hope, before comparing their approaches. Throughout, memory politics means the struggle over public interpretations of the past; trauma theory addresses how violence disrupts experience and its communication; postcolonial analysis examines domination and the inequalities that can survive its formal ending.

## Part One — Boris Takk in Ene Kõresaar’s analysis

### Boris Takk’s life trajectory: war, Soviet life, and restored independence

**Takk crossed competing political worlds.** His trajectory moved from youth under successive occupations to forced mobilization into an Estonian Waffen-SS unit, clandestine anti-Soviet resistance, settlement within Soviet society, Communist Party membership, service as a Supreme Council delegate, and participation in the recovery of previously suppressed veterans’ memories. The central biographical problem is therefore not simply how a soldier remembered war. It is how someone whose activities acquired sharply different meanings under different regimes made those activities intelligible as one life. [1]

**His youth supplied contrasting reference points.** Takk identified with the generation formed before the destruction of independent Estonia. Kõresaar explains that he associated this upbringing with diligence, responsibility, and an Estonian work ethic, which subsequently became standards for judging Soviet society. His childhood also included frequent contact with minority communities through family relationships; his sympathetic recollections of Jewish people later complicate any reading of his memoir as a uniformly exclusionary national account. [1]

| Period | Life phase | Experiences or affiliations | Later narrative significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prewar youth | Formation before Soviet rule | National identification; contact with minorities | Ethical benchmark for later life |
| 1940–1941 | First Soviet occupation | New rituals, arrests, disappearances, deportation | Beginning of national rupture |
| 1941–1943 | German occupation | Recognition of Nazi coercion and brutality | Disillusionment with another “liberator” |
| Autumn 1943 | Forced military mobilization | Estonian unit of the Waffen-SS | Coercion, survival, and national purpose |
| 1943–1944 | Military service and movement | Encounters in occupied Poland and Lithuania | Tension between occupied person and occupier |
| February 1944 | Return through Estonia | Train passage through the Klooga camp | Fragmentary recognition of Jewish suffering |

**The first occupation became formative.** In his recollections of 1940–1941, Takk initially uses ridicule and anecdotes about Soviet representatives, before moving toward accounts of arrests, the destruction of an Estonian War of Independence monument, disappearances, and the 1941 mass deportation. This movement from mockery to tragedy establishes the Soviet regime as both culturally alien and violently destructive. Kõresaar treats this progression as a deliberate narrative construction: the occupation supplies the interpretive beginning of the rupture that will organize the rest of his story. [1]

**German rule produced another disillusionment.** Takk entered the Waffen-SS through forced mobilization in autumn 1943, after, according to his account, already recognizing crimes and inhumanity under Nazi occupation. His judgment that the supposed liberators were alike undermines a simple story of voluntary ideological commitment to Germany. At the same time, conscription explains entry into a military institution without determining the meaning or responsibility of every subsequent action within it. [1]

**Survival became a national obligation.** Experienced soldiers whom Takk knew had become disillusioned with the prospect that German victory would restore Estonia. Their advice, as he remembered it, was to survive the struggle between Hitler and Stalin because Estonia might need them later. This understanding gave personal self-preservation a patriotic meaning: remaining alive could be represented as preparation for a future national opportunity, rather than abandonment of the national cause. [1]

**His wartime experiences resisted simplification.** Takk recalled sympathy for Polish people resisting occupation, compassion toward Italian prisoners of war, and embarrassment about confiscating horses from Lithuanian civilians. He also described involvement in a manhunt at Torma, during which he helped capture men whose subsequent execution he indirectly witnessed. These episodes place him in several positions at once: a conscript from an occupied country, a participant in an occupying military, a person capable of solidarity, and someone troubled by his involvement in coercive violence. [1]

**Soviet life introduced further reversals.** After the Soviet return in 1944, Takk’s German military service became a stigmatizing past. His activities as a forest brother—an anti-Soviet resistance participant living clandestinely—belonged to a biography that could not safely become public. He hid from the NKVD, the Soviet security apparatus, and experienced employment difficulties in the late 1940s and early 1950s. [1]

|---|---|---|---|

| Mid-1950s | Settlement and family formation | Kolkhoz residence, marriage, pursuit of better-paid work | Ordinary aspirations within an unwanted order |
| Late 1950s onward | Work and observation | Work trips to Soviet Russia | Material for criticism of Soviet life |

**Domestic aspirations complicated the rupture.** In the mid-1950s, Takk settled at a kolkhoz, or collective farm, married, and sought profitable employment to realize his ambition of building a house. These were substantial projects of ordinary life, not simply episodes of persecution or resistance. Their presence matters because his later interpretation of the entire Soviet period as an abnormal interruption had to accommodate experiences of work, family, aspiration, and achievement within that period. [1]

**Party membership changed his public position.** Around the early 1970s, Takk accepted an invitation to join the Communist Party and become a Supreme Council delegate. He subsequently explained this decision through national service rather than communist identification. Kõresaar describes his work “on the cultural front” as a route to recovering a publicly legitimate biography: the former German soldier and forest brother could now participate in officially recognized activity, although the earlier life remained politically dangerous. [1]

**The independence movement reversed these valuations.** Takk regarded the Baltic Chain of 1989 as one of his life’s most important events. From that period, he became involved in organizing veterans who had served in German forces and publicly recovered his forest-brother identity. Following the restoration of Estonian independence in 1991, experiences previously treated as discrediting could support a claim to patriotic recognition, while Communist Party membership now required explanation. [1]

**Writing connected mourning and representation.** Takk completed his life story in 1993, motivated in part by his mother’s death and reflection on mortality. In November 1996, he sent the 205-page computer-printed manuscript to the Estonian Life Stories Association in response to its public appeal. Kõresaar later interviewed him at home in July 2006, examining both his wartime service and his account of resistance during Soviet rule. [4]

**The biographical judgment is therefore mixed.** Takk’s life demonstrates both constrained choices under successive regimes and active efforts to influence his surroundings; neither an uncomplicated heroic biography nor a purely passive history of victimization captures its major transitions.

### Memory entrepreneurship after Estonian independence

**Takk did more than remember.** Describing him as a “memory entrepreneur” identifies his efforts to make a particular interpretation of experience publicly consequential. He organized fellow veterans, produced extensive testimony, addressed future readers as a representative of his generation, defended the legitimacy of German-uniformed soldiers’ choices, and advocated commemorative recognition. The term concerns the mobilization of memory and authority, not commercial profit. [1]

**His activism bridged the independence transition.** The organizational turn began around 1989, before the restoration of independence, and supplied the setting for his subsequent interventions. Previously suppressed military and resistance experiences could now enter public discussion. Takk’s memoir and later interview helped transform that opening into an argument about how independent Estonia should understand the men who had served in German forces. [1]

|---|---|---|---|
| From 1989 | Organizing former German-army soldiers | Veterans and emerging veteran networks | Collective recognition |
| 1993 | Completing a 205-page life story | Intended readers; his generation | Establishing testimonial authority |
| November 1996 | Submitting the manuscript | Estonian Life Stories Association | Entering an institutional memory collection |
| July 2006 | Interview and commemorative advocacy | Kõresaar; Estonian and Western publics addressed through her | Defending complex choices and remembrance |

**The manuscript claimed collective significance.** Takk did not present his experiences solely as private recollection. Kõresaar’s introduction describes him as assuming the roles of historical witness and spokesperson for a generation of Estonian men. His personal story thus supplied an experiential warrant for a larger claim: understanding his life would help readers understand a collective predicament. [4]

**Institutional submission changed the story’s status.** The Estonian Life Stories Association’s 1996 campaign, “My Destiny and the Destiny of Those Close to Me in the Labyrinths of History,” invited accounts of domestic life disrupted by war, political upheaval, deportation, and other violence. Takk’s submission placed his recollections within a nationally meaningful collection. An account originating partly in family mourning could thereby become material for cultural memory and historical interpretation. [5]

**Veteran organization created a collective claimant.** A former soldier recounting an isolated experience occupies a different public position from veterans organized around a shared demand for recognition. Takk’s involvement helped make German military service discussable as an Estonian experience rather than only through the Soviet category of service to the enemy. The political implication was a claim that the restored state should distinguish soldiers’ national aspirations and circumstances from the objectives of the power whose uniform they wore. [1]

**Commemoration exposed the stakes of classification.** In the 2006 interview, Takk expressed disappointment that German-uniformed veterans had not secured the monument to their fallen comrades that he considered appropriate. He contrasted their position with recognition afforded to veterans of the Red Army and Finnish Army. Kõresaar situates this complaint after the removal of the Lihula monument in 2004, showing how personal recollection interacted with public disputes over monuments and legitimate remembrance. [1]

**His objection concerned more than symbols.** Takk opposed blanket descriptions of men in German uniforms as “SS volunteers,” because such wording erased forced mobilization and the diversity of wartime choices. A monument would therefore have done more than honor the dead: it would have publicly validated an interpretation of their service. Recognition was simultaneously commemorative, biographical, and moral. [1]

**The interview became another public platform.** Kõresaar observes that Takk used their conversation to reaffirm his political views, not merely to supply additional factual detail. He also treated her as a “microphone” through which he could address Western countries. This continued an earlier practice, described in his memoir, of using informal conversations during foreign travel to explain the Soviet system to outsiders. [1]

**Authority moved through several relationships.** Takk’s experiential authority came from having lived through occupation, military service, clandestinity, and Soviet institutional life. Veteran organization gave that experience a collective setting; the life-writing campaign gave it an institutional destination; the interview supplied another listener and possible conduit to broader publics. The mechanism is not that experience automatically becomes public truth, but that particular settings make some experiences recognizable as evidence of a national history.

**Democratization could create new exclusions.** Kirsti Jõesalu and Kõresaar’s later account of post-1989 Estonia emphasizes precisely this double effect. Oral history and life writing challenged communist taboos and widened access to public memory, while also excluding events and actors from the emerging national story. Takk’s intervention belongs to that tension: recovering a suppressed veteran perspective does not by itself settle how Jewish victims, Soviet veterans, or other memory communities fit within the reconstructed past. [6]

**His entrepreneurship remained politically selective.** Takk sought recognition of coercion, patriotic intention, and the complexity of choices. Those are substantial historical concerns. Their emancipatory significance, however, depends on whether recognition of his group also permits scrutiny of the institutions it served and acknowledgment of people harmed by those institutions.

**The section’s central finding is concrete.** Takk’s memory entrepreneurship consisted of organizing, writing, institutional submission, testimonial advocacy, and demands for commemoration—not simply possessing memories or receiving scholarly attention.

### A “continuous struggle for Estonian freedom,” “prolonged rupture,” and service within the system

**Continuity supplied the organizing claim.** Takk’s life story is best summarized as a “continuous struggle for Estonian freedom”: an enduring national commitment expressed through changing circumstances rather than one uninterrupted organizational affiliation. Military service against the Soviet Union, forest-brother activity, Communist Party membership, work as a delegate, and post-Soviet memory activism become compatible when judged according to the national purpose he assigns them. Kõresaar calls the resulting combination syncretistic: experiences and identities that might appear contradictory are brought into a composite self-understanding. [1]

**The “third way” separates competing purposes.** For Takk, participation in German forces need not mean embracing Nazi Germany’s war aims. The “third way” represents Estonian aspirations to restore independence between two occupying powers. His account places the soldier’s private survival, the nation’s hoped-for future, and the occupying army’s objectives alongside one another without treating them as identical. [1]

**That distinction explains without exonerating.** An individual could oppose Soviet domination and hope for Estonian freedom while serving an institution advancing Germany’s occupation and war. The distinction between intention and institutional effect is indispensable here. Takk’s national purpose explains how he understood his actions; it does not transform the Waffen-SS into an independent Estonian army or remove the need to examine what particular actions accomplished.

**Rupture supplies the larger historical plot.** The Soviet era appears as a “prolonged rupture” in the normal development of the Estonian nation. In Kõresaar’s formulation, this memory regime interprets 1940 as the interruption of national continuity and extends that interruption across the Soviet period. Its characteristic alternatives are suffering under an oppressive order or resisting it, leaving little conceptual space for a politically ordinary life. [1]

**The rupture was cultural as well as political.** Takk describes the destruction of symbols, institutions, property relations, moral standards, and everyday practices. His repeated contrasts between independent Estonia and Soviet life make occupation a transformation of the national way of living, not merely a change of government. Read through a postcolonial lens, colonization therefore concerns imposed power and alien norms penetrating social life, alongside the loss of sovereignty. [5]

**Colonization links domination to everyday experience.** In wider Estonian memory discourse, Soviet rule is described through occupation, migration, Russification, and the displacement of national institutions and practices. Takk’s version emphasizes deterioration, distorted economic priorities, privilege, housing problems, and the importation of an objectionable Soviet mentality. These observations make the national claim experiential: the damage of external rule can supposedly be recognized in work, consumption, social conduct, and the built environment. [1][7]

Broader Estonian national narratives describe the Soviet period as a 50-year occupation, and some Estonians also describe it as colonization; Takk’s account gives this framework a biographical form by treating Soviet rule as the “prolonged rupture” in national continuity.

**Rupture and continuity operate together.** There is no logical contradiction between national history being ruptured and an individual claiming continuous loyalty to the nation. The political order is represented as discontinuous with legitimate Estonia, while national identity and commitment survive beneath it. This combination allows Takk to say that the circumstances of action changed profoundly but the underlying cause remained constant.

**Party membership tests the argument most sharply.** Takk’s explanation for entering the Communist Party and serving as a Supreme Council delegate is captured by “serving the Estonian cause from inside the system.” He presents access to official institutions as a means of acting for Estonia, not evidence of identification with the occupying regime. Kõresaar notes that, despite membership, he distances himself from the Communist Party and characterizes Estonian communists as puppets of the system. [1]

**Public enlightenment becomes a form of resistance.** The claim of internal service also includes telling foreigners about Soviet realities during holiday and official travel. Such conversations give an apparently privileged activity—travel abroad—a place within the resistance biography. He could therefore interpret institutional access and mobility as resources employed against the system’s self-presentation, even though they were also opportunities available through life within it. [1]

**The “right” biography changed sides.** Under Soviet rule, German military service and forest-brother activity constituted the “wrong” past, whereas Party membership and recognized public activity could supply a legitimate biography. During the national movement and after independence, the valuations reversed. Formerly suppressed anti-Soviet experiences could authenticate patriotism, while Party membership became an episode requiring national justification. [1]

**Narrative coherence answered a real predicament.** Kõresaar does not reduce this reconstruction to deliberate falsehood. Political transformations create demands for people to explain who they have been and why their lives belong within the new order. Takk’s memoir answers that demand by assigning consistent meaning to changing public identities, while retaining recollections that do not fit perfectly. [1]

**Everyday achievement strains the total interpretation.** Marriage, employment, the ambition to build a house, and participation in cultural life show that Soviet decades contained projects through which Takk pursued meaningful ends. His continuous-resistance narrative absorbs these experiences by subordinating them to the national opposition between foreign rule and legitimate Estonia. That is the framework’s strength as a means of self-reconciliation, but also its selectivity as an account of ordinary life.

**Narrative form preserves some independence.** Takk usually recounts military movement chronologically, through places, people, and events, while reflective passages explain their historical significance. Kõresaar observes that the recollections themselves retain ambivalence even where the overall political interpretation is forceful. The detailed story can therefore exceed the ideological summary imposed upon it. [1]

**The practical distinction is essential.** Takk’s narrative successfully explains how he reconciled conflicting affiliations with national belonging; its historical and ethical adequacy depends on preserving the difference between that reconciliation and proof that every affiliation or action served freedom.

### Takk’s stance on the Holocaust: compassion, comparison, and displaced responsibility

**Takk acknowledges persecution and mass murder.** His position combines sympathetic memories of Jewish people, fragmentary recognition of their disappearance and suffering under Nazi rule, and horror at Auschwitz-Birkenau. Yet the Holocaust becomes fully interpretable within his memoir chiefly through comparison with the Gulag and the broader evil of totalitarian systems. Kõresaar’s analysis therefore reveals acknowledgment without a sustained integration of Jewish genocide into the central moral and causal structure of his life story. [1]

| Encounter or recollection | What Takk recognizes | Narrative treatment | Ethical significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Childhood Jewish contacts | Familiar and sympathetic minority community | Positive personal recollection | Complicates an exclusively ethnic national story |
| Early Nazi occupation | Disappearance of Jews and others | Brief, youthful viewpoint | Persecution receives limited elaboration |
| Klooga, February 1944 | Distressing camp conditions | A “very dismal sight” | Visible suffering without developed responsibility |
| Auschwitz-Birkenau, 1970s | Organized killing under Nazism | Comparison with the Gulag | Condemnation returns to Soviet-centered meaning |

**Childhood familiarity opens an ethical connection.** Takk’s favorable recollections of minority communities, including Jewish people, prevent a straightforward equation of his patriotic self-image with hostility toward Jews. His memoir also registers Jewish disappearance at the beginning of German occupation. The important issue is how those recognitions develop—or fail to develop—within the subsequent account of war and national suffering. [1]

**The youthful viewpoint narrows retrospective knowledge.** Kõresaar notes that Takk describes early disappearances without explaining the possible fate of the people concerned. By adopting the perspective of the youngster he had been, the later narrator confines the passage to what that younger self perceived. This produces a specific ethical effect: the adult’s retrospective knowledge does not become an organizing resource for explaining the genocide surrounding the remembered event. [1]

**Klooga provides a concrete wartime encounter.** Takk remembers passing through the Jewish camp by train after returning to Estonia in February 1944 and describes the scene as deeply dismal. The passage records visible suffering rather than erasing it. Its brevity, however, leaves the prisoners primarily as objects of distressing observation, without tracing the persecution that brought them there or the relationships that sustained the camp. [1]

**The camp’s history clarifies the stakes.** Hiio and Kaasik’s historical overview records that nearly 2,000 Jews were massacred at Klooga on 19 September 1944. That later massacre is distinct from Takk’s February train passage. Keeping the dates separate shows both what his recollection captures—a camp encountered during military movement—and the wider history of genocidal violence within which the encounter belongs. [8]

**Auschwitz generates explicit moral condemnation.** During a tourist visit to Poland in the 1970s, Takk encountered Auschwitz-Birkenau and recalled becoming profoundly gloomy. He interpreted the site as revealing the nature of totalitarian ideologies: one regime constructed killing factories, while the other exploited its vast territory and allowed prisoners to die from hunger and cold. His comparison directly recognizes Nazi mass killing, while making Soviet violence the emotionally familiar reference point. [1]

**Comparison can illuminate shared violence.** Placing Nazi and Soviet systems alongside one another can expose imprisonment, dehumanization, organized coercion, and lethal state power. For a person whose life was organized around experiences of Soviet occupation, the comparison also makes an otherwise less personally connected history morally accessible. An analysis of comparison should therefore ask what recognition it enables, not assume that comparison necessarily means indifference.

**Comparison can also displace specificity.** In Takk’s account, Auschwitz becomes evidence of the general nature of totalitarianism rather than the center of an inquiry into the particular persecution and attempted destruction of Jews. The shift matters because the Holocaust’s defining question—why Jewish people were targeted for destruction as Jews—does not receive the same explanatory weight as the nature of Soviet oppression. Recognition of one violence is routed through a narrative already centered on another.

**Kõresaar distinguishes empathy from felt responsibility.** She finds compassion in these Holocaust-related passages but not the sense of guilt or responsibility apparent in some other wartime recollections. She also interprets their inclusion as relatively exceptional in the early post-Soviet life-story environment in which Takk wrote. Her point is not that sympathy is worthless, but that sympathy alone does not establish a relationship between Jewish suffering, the institutions in which he served, and his own account of national action. [1]

**Torma reveals a contrasting moral intensity.** Takk’s participation in the manhunt at Torma troubles him much more directly. He tries to determine whether the captured men were partisans, as the operation’s organizers claimed, or deserters, as he suspected from the events. In his moral reasoning, that distinction affects whether he was fighting an enemy or acting against the very survival principle that informed his own wartime conduct. [1]

**The contrast concerns involvement and recognition.** At Torma, Takk confronts his participation in capturing compatriots and indirectly facilitating their deaths. In the Holocaust passages, suffering is witnessed or retrospectively encountered, but does not produce the same self-questioning. The difference shows how proximity, personal involvement, national identification, and available narrative conventions help determine which memories become occasions for guilt and which remain expressions of compassion.

**The “third way” can shield national innocence.** By making survival for Estonian independence the presumed purpose of Estonians in German service, Takk distances national motivation from Nazi policy. This distinction has explanatory value, especially for conscripts. It becomes ethically problematic when an account of intention substitutes for examination of participation: patriotic goals do not establish that a soldier’s service was structurally separate from the occupying power’s wider violence.

**Broader memoir evidence sharpens this concern.** Karl Stuklis’s comparative study, published online in 2023 and in a 2024 journal issue, examines three Estonian and three Latvian legionnaire memoirs. He identifies a recurrent “separate war” narrative that emphasizes patriotic resistance to Soviet power and distances national military service from the Holocaust. His criticism concerns the way such accounts can leave the genocide as an exclusively German crime, outside the national story of responsibility. [9]

**The surrounding field also contains counterexamples.** Stuklis notes that Ants Kõverjalg’s 1994 memoir expresses horror on learning about a concentration camp for Jews in Estonia. The existence of such acknowledgment shows why the choice is not simply remembrance versus silence. As with Takk, the interpretive question is whether Jewish suffering becomes historically consequential within the memoir or remains a brief moral observation beside a much more elaborated patriotic narrative. [9]

**Ethical analysis must preserve individual distinctions.** Takk’s narrative contains criticism of German occupation, empathy for occupied peoples, and unresolved guilt concerning a coercive operation. These features resist reducing him to a generic apologetic voice. Equally, national victimhood and conscription do not resolve questions about institutional service, witnessed persecution, or the unequal narrative attention afforded different victims.

**The resulting judgment is specific.** Takk recognizes the Holocaust and responds with compassion and horror, but interprets it through an antitotalitarian comparison that returns explanatory priority to Soviet oppression and leaves Jewish genocide only partially integrated into his account of freedom and responsibility.

### Kõresaar’s dominant postcolonial perspective and the dual-colonization framework

**Kõresaar explicitly identifies a dominant perspective.** She describes Takk’s life story as representing a dominant view of twentieth-century Estonian history evaluated from a postcolonial position. Its central premise is that foreign domination interrupted the nation’s proper development, while national continuity survived beneath imposed political systems. World War II consequently belongs within a history of dual colonization by Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, rather than a straightforward progression from fascist occupation to Soviet liberation. [1]

**The historical sequence explains its force.** Estonia experienced Soviet occupation in 1940, German occupation in 1941, and the Soviet return in 1944. An effort to restore Estonian statehood in September 1944 failed. Those transitions explain why the end of German occupation could be remembered simultaneously as the defeat of Nazism and the loss of a renewed opportunity for Estonian independence. [8]

**Dual colonization restores an obscured standpoint.** From the perspective of Estonian sovereignty, neither occupying power represented national self-determination. Takk’s disappointment with German “liberators,” alongside his rejection of Soviet domination, challenges histories that offer only the categories of collaboration with Germany or liberation by the Red Army. The framework makes visible an occupied population’s attempts to act within alternatives it did not choose. [1]

**Different claims must remain distinct.** A historical account of successive occupations, a postcolonial interpretation of national rupture, and an assertion that Nazi and Soviet crimes were equivalent are related but separate propositions. Conflating them makes both historical recognition and ethical criticism less precise.

| Interpretive frame | Central question | Main analytical contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Successive occupations | Who exercised power over Estonia? | Establishes changes of sovereignty and rule |
| Dual colonization and prolonged rupture | How did domination disrupt national life? | Explains continuity, resistance, and postcolonial identity |
| Nazi–Soviet equivalence or “double genocide” | Are different crimes treated as interchangeable? | Exposes claims about comparative suffering and moral authority |

**Recognition does not require equivalence.** Acknowledging Soviet repression does not require treating its purposes, institutions, victims, or killing practices as identical to those of the Holocaust. Conversely, preserving the Holocaust’s historical specificity does not require minimizing Soviet deportation, imprisonment, or the destruction of Estonian sovereignty. The central difficulty is sustaining both forms of recognition without using either to cancel responsibility associated with the other.

**The national subject can become too uniform.** A framework organized around “Estonia” as an enduring victim may obscure that inhabitants experienced the occupations differently. For Jews, Nazi occupation entailed a threat of destruction that cannot be adequately represented by asking which regime was less damaging to the ethnic majority. Likewise, placing all Estonians in the position of an occupied people can conceal the particular actions of individuals and institutions within occupation.

**The Soviet experience becomes a filter.** Kõresaar’s conclusion is that, during the early 1990s, memories of Soviet occupation supplied the principal framework through which other twentieth-century periods acquired meaning. Her related work calls this a memory “filter”: the attributed meaning of Soviet domination extends backward and outward, shaping the interpretation of Nazi occupation and other experiences. Takk’s Auschwitz–Gulag comparison is a concrete example of this operation. [1][10]

**Counter-memory can become a dominant narrative.** The national interpretation initially opposed a Soviet history that suppressed repression and represented incorporation into the USSR as legitimate historical progress. Once established after independence, however, that interpretation could acquire the authority to judge other recollections. Experiences that complicated the binary of suffering and resistance—including ordinary Soviet attachments or achievements—could become difficult to express publicly. [5]

**Postcolonial criticism must examine its own hierarchies.** Takk’s contrasts between responsible, industrious Estonians and degraded Soviet life can communicate real experiences of imposed rule. Yet civilizational contrasts can also slide from criticism of institutions into claims about the inferior culture of those associated with them. Stuklis’s broader memoir analysis demonstrates how narratives of European national belonging can retain racialized contrasts between civilized Europeans and supposedly “Asiatic” Soviets. [9]

**Kõresaar’s method prevents complete closure.** Her analysis does not simply endorse Takk’s self-description or dismiss it as ideological manipulation. She follows contradictions between his governing narrative and particular recollections, including his simultaneous positions as occupied person and occupier, victim and participant in violence. The postcolonial framework explains the life story’s public intelligibility; its unresolved details reveal why that framework cannot exhaust the life. [1]

**Part One’s judgment is therefore double.** The dual-colonization perspective illuminates Estonia’s loss of sovereignty and the constrained agency of its inhabitants, but becomes reductive when national continuity turns into collective innocence or when Soviet suffering supplies the final meaning of every other victim’s history.

**Source basis and evidentiary boundary**

**Supported result.** The available evidence establishes Boris Takk as an Estonian wartime veteran whose later public self-representation connected German-uniform service with the cause of a free Estonia, but it does not provide a complete biography or a direct statement of his personal Holocaust position. The strongest evidence concerns his early life, wartime affiliation, post-independence commemorative appearances, and the way later scholarship interprets his memoir; his postwar and Soviet-period chronology, his alleged service “inside the system,” and his specific Holocaust views remain incomplete.

**Documented life trajectory**

- **Early life:** Boris Takk was born in Viljandi on **12 September 1925**.[20] The available biographical account says that, as a child, he learned German in a children’s daycare, acquired performance and musical-note skills, and pursued gymnastics before taking up diving.[20] In **July 1943**, he became Estonia’s junior diving champion.[20]

- **An incomplete 1942 entry:** The same account says that the technically minded Takk entered something in **1942**, but the supplied passage ends before identifying the institution, activity, or organization.[20] That detail should therefore remain unresolved rather than being completed speculatively.

- **Wartime experience:** The evidence identifies Takk as a former soldier who later shared memories of wartime days, but the supplied reports do not establish a detailed wartime chronology, unit, movements, or specific actions.[23] They do establish that he fought in a German uniform or in German forces and that he later represented this service as fighting for Estonia.[22][25] This is a documented affiliation and retrospective self-description, not a complete account of his wartime conduct.

- **Postwar and Soviet-period life:** The relevant Kõresaar chapter is clearly the source intended for reconstructing these stages, but the bibliographic pages and supplied secondary summaries do not provide the requested chronology from 1945 through the end of Soviet rule.[21][26] The available evidence therefore cannot specify Takk’s postwar employment, residence, institutional position, or the precise actions behind his later claim to have served Estonia from within the Soviet system.

- **Later residence and public identity:** A 2015 report describes Takk as a long-time resident of Karksi-Nuia, associated with music and the homeland, and as a former soldier who recalled wartime days.[23] In the 2018 evidence, he is identified as an elderly veteran living in Viljandi city or county.[25] On **1 July 2020**, he was reported as a **94-year-old** resident of the Viljandimaa care centre.[22]

**Post-independence memory work and the qualified “memory entrepreneur” label**


- A report published on **17 September 2015** announced a presentation in Viljandi’s Sakala Centre of the record *Laulud leegionis* (“Songs of the Legion”), involving Takk and Margus Põldsepp. Takk was to share memories of wartime days, and the record contained **22 songs** performed by Takk and Põldsepp, the latter identified as director of the Karksi-Nuia music school and an active musician.[23] This combines a cultural medium, a public venue, a collaborator, a recorded corpus, and spoken recollection.

- On **2 September 2018**, Takk participated in the Lihula monument commemoration by giving a speech, singing old grenadier songs with musician friends, and criticizing contemporary political leaders whom he said were moving away from valuing the veterans’ shed blood.[24] The report also describes the event as involving a reported attendance of “a couple of hundred” people, while visual estimates reached **up to 400**; it mentions **seven men** who had themselves participated in the 1944 battles.[24] These figures concern different populations and methods, and the source supplies no denominator or reproducible counting procedure. It also refers to the prospect of a permanent freedom-fighters’ monument by the following anniversary, but does not establish that Takk personally organized that outcome.[24]

**Freedom, rupture, and the two-occupation frame**

The available statements support the report’s compact formulation that Takk represented his actions as a **“continuous struggle for Estonian freedom.”** In a newspaper report, he stated that the blue-black-white Estonian flag was on his German uniform and that he fought for a free Estonia.[22] At the 2018 Mustla commemoration, he similarly characterized the veterans’ German-forces service as fighting for Estonia.[25] His Lihula appearance extended that framing by honoring grenadiers and freedom fighters while criticizing leaders who, in his view, no longer valued their sacrifice.[24] The phrase “continuous struggle” is therefore a supported synthesis of recurring claims, but it is not a verified verbatim quotation in the supplied extracts.

**Holocaust stance and the limits of inference**

A later study of selected Estonian and Latvian Legionnaire memoirs provides a relevant comparative warning, not direct evidence of Takk’s individual position. It reports that Holocaust references appear in the memoir corpus while crimes associated with Estonian and Latvian perpetrators are omitted; crimes attributed to “the Germans” are discussed instead.[27][28] The effect, according to that study, is to make military service appear justifiable through emphasis on Soviet atrocities while distancing the legionnaires from Nazi crimes.[27] Its wider characterization is that the studied memoirs present the Baltic states as victims of both regimes, rank Soviet occupation as worse for the titular ethnicities, and represent the Holocaust as solely a German crime.[27][28] Because the corpus is broader than Takk and does not explicitly establish his personal view, these findings should be used as a context for testing his narrative, not as a statement of what Takk himself believed.

**Estonia, Kõresaar, and the research intersection**

Estonia is not merely a backdrop to the case. The volume containing the Takk chapter explicitly concerns World War II and its aftermath in Estonian post-Soviet life stories, and its contents include a study of remembrance cultures and the politics of recognition in post-Soviet Estonia.[26] Kõresaar’s listed research fields include life-story research, biographical method, autobiographical and collective memory, socialism and post-socialism, World War II remembrance, oral history, object studies, and Estonian ethnology.[18] Her contribution to the intersection is therefore clearest in the connection between individual life writing, public recognition, national memory, and postcolonial representations of rupture.

**Documented trajectory and evidentiary limits**

Boris Takk’s documented trajectory runs from a multilingual Viljandi childhood through German military service, Soviet-era concealment and institutional life, post-1991 veteran memory work, and death on 5 August 2020. He was born in Viljandi on 12 September 1925; his parents had arrived in Estonia as optants in 1922, while his father Aleksander had worked as a Red Cross driver and police officer. [29][33][34]

In interwar Viljandi, Takk attended German childcare, learned Russian from his mother, took piano lessons, acted in school and Russian-community plays, joined the scouts, and trained in gymnastics and diving. He studied at Haridusseltsi Gümnaasium and, from 1937, at the Viljandi County Boys’ Gymnasium. After completing eight grades, he entered Tartu Technical School to study mechanical engineering in 1942; in July 1943 he became Estonia’s youth diving champion and worked as a storekeeper in a military vehicle-repair workshop. [29][34]

On 3 November 1943 Takk was mobilized into the Estonian Legion and German military system and transferred to Heidelaager on 17 November. The accounts place him in machine-gun training, later as a driver, and in Jahtkomando/SS-Jagdverband operations near Tartu and Lake Peipus against partisans. They also describe a firefight with forest brothers and Estonian soldiers preventing the destruction of an entire village’s grain stores. A separate report records assignments to Berlin, Dresden, Lithuania, Alavere/Kehrа, and the Narva front. [29][32][34][35] At the camp, 500 boys were reportedly sent as Battalion Narwa reinforcement and 35 returned; Takk was not selected. He contracted diphtheria and then jaundice. During the retreat he moved through Kuremäe, Jõhvi, Konsu, Rakvere, Tapa, and Tallinn before returning to Viljandi, hiding as a forest brother until an amnesty, and resuming school; the date of the amnesty and duration of hiding remain unspecified. [32][34]

**Public memory work after independence**

The available analysis treats Takk’s public role after Estonia’s independence in 1991 as that of a historical witness and spokesperson for his generation: his autobiography positioned his Waffen-SS service and survival under Soviet rule as representative of a broader Estonian experience, while reframing the twentieth century as national liberation from foreign occupation. [31] His written memories date from 1993, when veterans were participating in public debates about the Soviet past. Kõresaar reads those memories through several “communities of memory,” including family, veterans’ groups, and the local community. [35]

Takk’s observable memory-making activities justify, though do not by themselves make conclusive, the label **memory entrepreneur**. After a veterans’ gathering in Tori was banned, he helped organize one at Karksi-Nuia; word of mouth brought 38 men to Lake Ainja. He participated in veterans’ associations, the 8 July 2011 commemoration of Viljandi’s wartime “liberation,” and events at Sinimäed, birthdays, and funerals. [29] He also performed and recorded wartime songs: a five-year project produced the CD *Laulud Leegionis*, released around his ninetieth birthday with Põldsepp & Pojad. Its songs and spoken intertexts presented his own story as the story of a generation, invited later generations to ask what they would have done, and helped listeners interpret their ancestors’ lives. [29] A further report records Takk speaking at a Sinimäe gathering in the year before the 5 June 2019 interview. [33] These activities combine an actor, veteran institutions, commemorative occasions, musical media, and public audiences; the source-reported figures—13 destroyed monuments, 38 gathering participants, 500 boys and 35 returnees, and documents for 10,000 men—should not be converted into comparative rates because their denominators and methods are incomplete. [29]

**Narrative, Holocaust, and Kõresaar’s reading**

Takk’s self-narrative presents his wartime conduct as a **“continuous struggle for Estonian freedom.”** Although he fought on the German side, he emphasized the blue-black-white insignia on his uniform and said that he fought for a free Estonia. [33] In the account reconstructed by Kõresaar, the “third way” meant surviving between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union in the hope of restoring Estonian independence. The Soviet period became a **“prolonged rupture”**: foreign rule supposedly broke Estonian national continuity, while Takk’s actions remained part of an uninterrupted freedom struggle. [30][31]

## Part Two — Svetlana Aleksievich, Jasmina Husanović, and the politics of testimony

### Aleksievich’s *War's Unwomanly Face*: women’s experiences against the victory narrative

**Aleksievich changes whose experience counts.** *War's Unwomanly Face*, first published in 1985 and also known in English as *The Unwomanly Face of War*, reconstructs World War II through women’s remembered lives rather than primarily through military operations, commanders, or the state’s victorious culmination. Its political intervention lies in making everyday, bodily, emotional, and subsequently marginalized experiences part of the historical account. [11]

**Participation and remembrance diverged sharply.** The 2017 publisher description places Soviet women’s wartime participation at more than one million and identifies nurses, doctors, pilots, tank drivers, machine-gunners, and snipers among them. It contrasts their extensive service with the subsequent forgetting of their efforts and sacrifices. The discrepancy between indispensable wartime participation and diminished postwar visibility is the book’s central problem of memory politics. [12]

**The book is not an operational history.** Its “history of emotions” asks how war was experienced by particular people: how youth, fear, bodily discomfort, work, affection, and death entered their lives. These details are not merely supplementary illustrations of a military story whose meaning is already settled. They challenge what constitutes the story, shifting attention from the achievement of victory to the human transformations and losses through which victory occurred. [2]

**The official canon shaped private speech.** Aleksievich describes women returning interview transcripts with requests to omit small details and emphasize the “great victory.” One woman’s husband instructed her to tell the correct version of the war and directed her toward an approved history book. Such examples show that dominant memory operated through family expectations and speakers’ own habits, not only through formal state censorship. [13]

**The “small” detail can overturn scale.** A recollection about clothing or bodily embarrassment may appear marginal beside battle losses and military strategy. Within Aleksievich’s method, however, its apparent insignificance reveals how the existing hierarchy of importance was established. A history that recognizes sacrifice but excludes the experience of being a young woman in a military environment can celebrate participants while withholding the terms in which they understood their own lives.

**Lola Akhmetova supplies a revealing example.** In the testimony discussed by Danijela Lugaric Vukas, Akhmetova anticipates that the interviewer expects death to be named as war’s most terrible experience. Instead, she talks about having to wear men’s underwear and the humiliation and absurdity this produced. The testimony does not offer a universal ranking of wartime suffering; it challenges the listener’s assumption that the meaning of war is already known before the witness speaks. [11]

**Women appear beyond a single role.** Their service includes combat, medical care, logistical work, and life in occupied territories. This breadth matters because a women-centered history could otherwise reproduce another hierarchy, admitting women only when they resemble the conventional male combat hero. Aleksievich’s attention to everyday labor and personal experience widens the field of historically meaningful wartime activity. [12]

**The contrast with Takk is instructive.** Takk generally seeks a coherent national interpretation that reconnects his different roles to an enduring purpose. Aleksievich makes experiences central precisely because they disrupt a settled public story. Both approaches recover voices constrained by Soviet memory, but they seek different kinds of recognition: one emphasizes the legitimacy of a national biography, the other the inadequacy of a heroic canon to contain lived experience.

**The section’s judgment concerns historical scale.** Aleksievich’s achievement is not simply adding women to an existing victory narrative, but showing how their recollections change the questions through which war itself is remembered.

### Listening, editing, and the form of traumatic testimony

**Her method combines listening and composition.** Aleksievich interviews eyewitnesses and then selects, collates, edits, and arranges their utterances into a literary montage. “Polyphony” means that multiple voices and consciousnesses coexist within the work rather than being fully absorbed into a single explanatory narrator. Yet the finished book is an authored arrangement, not an unmediated transcript of collective memory. [2]

**Prolonged encounters make departure possible.** Aleksievich describes sitting with an interviewee for hours, sometimes an entire day, until speech moves away from the official canon. Familiar conversation about recipes, hairstyles, and everyday matters helps establish a relationship in which something less rehearsed may emerge. The mechanism is relational: a different listener and setting can make a different account possible. [13]

**Testimony is addressed to somebody.** Lugaric Vukas emphasizes that the listener helps shape a testimony’s content, composition, and style. A witness anticipates what the listener wants, resists those expectations, seeks recognition, or withdraws when recognition fails. This means that testimony’s political significance cannot be located in the speaker alone: the capacity to hear is part of the conditions under which a memory becomes communicable. [11]

**Postwar silence could follow failed listening.** One recollection examined by Lugaric Vukas describes speaking repeatedly after returning from war, finding that nobody listened, and consequently becoming silent. That sequence distinguishes an experience that is psychologically difficult to narrate from an environment unwilling to receive it. The two processes can reinforce each other, but the political remedy is not identical: making space for testimony addresses a social relationship, not simply an individual incapacity.

**Trauma introduces a second temporality.** In the Caruth-influenced interpretation developed by Lugaric Vukas, traumatic experience may return belatedly because it was not fully processed when it occurred. The person speaking decades later is therefore not merely retrieving a completed account from storage. The testimony becomes a present encounter with an experience whose emotional and symbolic consequences remain unsettled. [11]

**The interview contains several selves.** Aleksievich’s reflections distinguish the witness speaking now, that same person at the time of the remembered event, and the interviewer listening. The relationship between the wartime self and the later narrator can be strained by experiences of irrevocable change. Remembering war may involve trying to address someone one once was, rather than confidently asserting a continuous identity. [11]

**Fragmentation becomes a meaningful form.** Ellipses, interrupted recollections, abrupt changes of register, and loosely connected testimonies can register difficulty in finding language for experience. C. Gao’s 2025 analysis describes memories as fragmented, asynchronous, and heterogeneous, while arguing that their combination produces a larger structure of individual and collective pasts. Coherence thus emerges across differences rather than through the complete integration of every witness’s story. [14]

**Literary form also produces apparent immediacy.** A pause represented by an ellipsis may suggest speech breaking down under emotional pressure; it may also result from editorial trimming or a deliberate decision to end an account unresolved. The Nottingham essay usefully stresses that the same formal feature can support several readings. The appropriate response is neither to dismiss the testimony as manufactured nor to treat every mark of fragmentation as transparent evidence of trauma. [13]

**Shared authorship does not erase editorial power.** Matthew Mucha’s concept of “textual quilting” emphasizes that the book’s larger narrative is made from women’s first-hand accounts. The witnesses provide its experiences, language, and perspectives, while Aleksievich determines selection, placement, juxtaposition, and thematic emphasis. Shared authorship therefore describes an interaction between distinct kinds of authority rather than the disappearance of the author. [15]

**Thematic arrangement changes what voices mean.** Placing a testimony under a heading about beauty, romance, or noncombatant labor encourages readers to interpret it through that category. A statement can remain factually intact while acquiring a different significance through its neighbors and framing. Polyphony is therefore politically enabling because it brings voices together, but also politically consequential because arrangement determines the relationships readers perceive between them.

**Listening affects the listener as well.** Lugaric Vukas discusses Aleksievich’s account of her own four-year undertaking and the emotional exhaustion produced by hearing survivors. The author’s diary becomes part of the documentary record because listening is itself an experience with consequences. This does not make the interviewer’s suffering interchangeable with wartime experience; it establishes that testimony creates an ethical relationship rather than a neutral transfer of information. [11]

**The reader inherits a mediated responsibility.** Readers encounter neither the original event nor the whole interview, but a composed testimony addressed through several relationships. Their task is to attend both to what the witness says and to the form through which that speech becomes audible. This makes literary criticism and memory analysis complementary: examining mediation is part of understanding testimony, not a reason to stop listening.

**The methodological judgment is clear.** *War's Unwomanly Face* works as a crafted documentary polyphony whose truth claims depend on the relationship between remembered experience, attentive listening, and authorial arrangement—not on an imagined absence of mediation.

### The limits of counter-memory: gender, Soviet identity, and the Holocaust

**Gendered recovery can reproduce gender assumptions.** Aleksievich’s insistence that women’s war differs from the masculine canon restores suppressed experiences. It can also imply that women are naturally life-giving, compassionate, or reluctant to kill, while men are naturally prepared for violence. The Nottingham essay, drawing on criticism by Angela Brintlinger and Serhy Yekelchyk, identifies this tension between expanding women’s speech and interpreting it through established ideals of femininity. [13]

**The strongest contrast concerns institutions.** The book is most persuasive when understood as challenging a masculine public canon, not as demonstrating that all men remember without emotion or all women remember compassionately. A state’s commemorative language can privilege masculine heroism while also excluding frightened, grieving, or disillusioned male combatants. Gender remains indispensable to the analysis without becoming a universal explanation of how individuals experience war.

**Censorship reveals acceptable femininity.** The book’s introductory material includes accounts of what censors removed, involving menstruation, sexual violence, cowardice, and other experiences incompatible with purified heroism. Its staged encounters with censorship expose a demand that women appear as exemplary figures rather than embodied persons. The political issue is therefore not merely women’s absence, but the conditional terms on which women could be publicly remembered. [13]

**Criticism coexists with Soviet attachment.** Aleksievich’s work does not place every Soviet identification outside moral legitimacy. Her admiration for the Soviet people and their victory remains alongside condemnation of Stalin and the Gulag. The counter-memory is consequently an internal struggle over the meaning of a shared past, not simply a departure from that past into an entirely external position. [13]

**Its postcolonial position is correspondingly complex.** Vice notes that Aleksievich has been described as a major postcolonial author of post-communism because she represents ordinary and subordinated perspectives on Soviet empire in its own language. “Subaltern” here identifies positions marginalized within dominant structures of representation. It does not mean that speakers stand outside Soviet culture or possess identities untouched by its ideals and categories. [2]

**Jewish testimony tests polyphony’s inclusiveness.** Vice’s study finds Jewish persecution present in Aleksievich’s wartime texts, but often situated within a broader Soviet story of atrocity rather than developed as a particular genocide. The issue is not whether Jewish voices appear at all. It is whether their historically distinct predicament remains audible within the larger composition. [2]

**Anna Strumilina makes difference explicit.** In a testimony from *The Unwomanly Face of War* examined by Vice, Strumilina recalls discovering that she was Jewish after German occupation, having previously experienced everyday life among different groups as shared. Her recollection shows that persecution imposed a lethal distinction where the speaker had understood social sameness. It also registers survival and entry into partisan life through an individual voice rather than an abstract category. [2]

**Liubov Kresova exposes another framing tension.** Kresova recalls a young couple kissing in a ghetto before a German patrol shoots them. The scene communicates genocidal confinement and the precarious assertion of ordinary human intimacy. Yet its placement in a section emphasizing women’s desire for beauty encourages a second reading in which the ghetto’s specific historical conditions become subsidiary to a broader account of femininity under war. [2]

**The comparison with Takk is not equivalence.** Takk’s Auschwitz comparison returns Jewish suffering to the moral centrality of Soviet oppression; Aleksievich’s montage can absorb Jewish persecution into a wider Soviet epic. The routes differ, as do the speakers’ historical positions and literary purposes. The shared analytical lesson is that recognizing a marginalized experience does not automatically give it equal explanatory independence.

**Counter-memory therefore needs continuing scrutiny.** Aleksievich’s work opens the canon to experiences it had subordinated, while remaining shaped by gender conventions, Soviet attachments, and editorial choices. Its liberating force grows when readers hear differences within the chorus rather than treating the chorus itself as proof that every difference has been preserved.

**Additional documented evidence**

Eastern Europe is a comparative, not homogeneous, frame: shared communism, dictatorship, Russian influence, transition, and reckoning coexist with distinct histories and institutions [188]. A 2024 cluster analysis found that 28 post-communist countries did not form a distinct region on most examined dimensions [190], while the region’s liminality challenges centre/periphery binaries [191]. Bosnia and Herzegovina is used as a bounded Southeastern European context, not a fourth case [130]. In Estonia, appeals from 1989 onward produced over 3,000 written life stories and approximately 100 oral-history interviews by the late 2000s, yet Russian-speaking narratives were often excluded; memory discourse also shifted from rupture and distress toward more complex, sometimes nostalgic, interpretations [196].

World War II is shared but not uniform: its end did not restore Estonia’s freedom, which came only in 1991 [197]. National commemorations can make Holocaust memory compete with other suffering [189][192], but Nazi and Soviet crimes may be discussed together without implying symmetry [41]. The deciding condition is to compare how each perspective authorizes memory, mediates testimony, and turns vulnerability into agency while retaining each period, genre, geography, and responsibility. Their differences—not equivalence—make the intersection visible.

Across Boris Takk, Ene Kõresaar, Svetlana Aleksievich, and Jasmina Husanović, the intersection is best framed by an “in-between,” historically variable Eastern European framework—not a homogeneous container: memory politics is socially mediated contestation, trauma socially constructed, and postcolonialism historically specific; the trauma–postcolonialism link remains contested [191][193][188][194][195].

Boris Takk’s memoir privileges a national-resistance account, while Ene Kõresaar mediates veterans’ life stories through critical post-Soviet analysis; oral history and life writing can democratize memory but also exclude actors [31][196]. In *War’s Unwomanly Face*, Svetlana Aleksievich shifts authority to women’s suppressed voices through intimate interviews and literary monologues without authorial interruption, whereas Jasmina Husanović centers marginalized bodies through relational witnessing rather than national ownership; Aleksievich’s selection can be unrepresentative and blur fact with literary construction, while Husanović’s gatekeepers may privilege some views [30][181][52].

### Husanović’s politics of witnessing: trauma and agency in postwar Bosnia

**Witnessing is a political relationship.** Jasmina Husanović’s “politics of witnessing and hope” connects the struggle to communicate violence with the possibility of changing the conditions that render survivors powerless. Her 2004 article, “Reckoning with the ‘Bosnia Troubles’: Trauma, Witnessing, and Politics,” examines how remembrance, memorialization, and testimony inscribe trauma into public life. Her 2014 work explicitly develops the transformative potential of hope, solidarity, equality, and cooperative knowledge production. [16][3]

**The starting figure is a marginalized woman.** Husanović begins with the face and body of a Bosnian woman exposed to wartime and postwar trauma, destitution, and repeated waiting. She waits to return home, to learn the fate of missing people, to see perpetrators punished, and to obtain security and a viable future. This figure makes trauma inseparable from material conditions and political exclusion, rather than treating war’s aftermath solely as private psychic injury. [16]

**The regional setting is relationally marginal.** Husanović describes the Balkans as positioned at Europe’s uncertain boundaries, Bosnia as further dislocated within that setting, and the traumatized woman as excluded even within Bosnia’s conventional political registers. Her postcolonial concern therefore operates through layered hierarchies of recognition. The question is who can speak as a political subject when local, national, regional, and international orders all help determine what counts as intelligible speech.

**She rejects a hierarchy of suffering.** Her principal concern is not measuring trauma or establishing whose injury is greatest. It is how communities reckon with violence through practices of memory and how particular meanings become dominant. Official and personal narratives, institutional and marginalized accounts, are part of a struggle over the consequences of the past for present belonging and authority. [16]

**Political community can betray its promise.** Drawing on Jenny Edkins, Husanović treats trauma as exposing the gap between a community’s promise of security and the violence that community can inflict. The family or political order that gives life meaning may also become a source of danger. Trauma consequently unsettles not only a person’s confidence but the legitimacy of the institutions and identities through which confidence had been organized. [16]

**The language of testimony is already political.** Survivors must describe their experience through vocabularies belonging to the very relationships of power that traumatized them. Available categories may recognize them only as members of an ethnic group, objects of charity, medical patients, or witnesses relevant to a narrowly defined legal issue. Speaking can therefore renew powerlessness when the listener accepts the story only after removing its challenge to existing arrangements. [16]

**Public recognition can become appropriation.** Husanović, drawing on Kali Tal, identifies three ways institutions and publics manage difficult testimony. They need not simply suppress speech; they may make it acceptable by changing its meaning or limiting its political consequences. [16]

| Public strategy | Treatment of testimony | Position assigned to survivors | Political effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mythologization | Controlled story of redemption or overcoming | Symbols within an accepted narrative | Conflict becomes a settled public meaning |
| Medicalization | Testimony interpreted chiefly as illness | Patients requiring rehabilitation | Political grievances become individual symptoms |
| Disappearance | Trauma denied or recognition refused | Unreliable or inaudible claimants | Demands lose public credibility |

**The criticism concerns exclusive control.** Medical care can be necessary, and public commemoration can sustain recognition. Husanović’s objection is to making such registers exhaustive: the survivor receives treatment or sympathy in exchange for surrendering a political voice. Similarly, a museum or documentary can acknowledge atrocity while presenting it as resolved, thereby obscuring continuing inequalities or responsibility.

**Witnessing resists that exchange.** In Husanović’s account, bearing witness can involve refusing pressure to revise, normalize, or repress experience. Its political force lies in a demand for change, not merely admission into a fixed narrative. Survivors’ control over interpretation becomes important because the meaning of their injury concerns the constitution of the community, not only their place within an otherwise legitimate order. [16]

**Agency extends beyond the original survivor.** Following Dori Laub, Husanović distinguishes witnessing an event, witnessing another person’s testimony, and witnessing the process of witnessing. The listener, cultural worker, scholar, and audience can therefore become participants in the conditions under which testimony is heard or appropriated. This is a differentiated responsibility: hearing another’s experience creates obligations without making the listener identical to the person who underwent it.

**The contrast with Takk is sharp.** Takk seeks to establish continuity between his life and a legitimate national community. Husanović asks whether the available community and its categories are themselves part of the injury. National recognition may therefore be an important response in one setting while becoming an insufficient or restrictive destination in another.

**Her central judgment is emancipatory but demanding.** Witnessing becomes politically transformative when it preserves survivors’ capacity to question the community that receives them, rather than requiring their incorporation as harmless embodiments of an already accepted history.

### Srebrenica, embodied memorialization, and the relationship between art and law

**The Srebrenica example makes witnessing tangible.** In her 2004 article, Husanović describes women holding a silent public protest on the eleventh day of each month while carrying pillows embroidered with the names of missing people. This practice transforms absence, domestic labor, bodily presence, and recurring public assembly into a form of testimony. Its significance lies in making an unresolved claim persist visibly rather than treating remembrance as an event completed once. [16]

**Names resist abstraction and substitution.** The embroidered names insist that missing people are particular persons connected to those who remain. The pillow evokes a sleeper who should return, bringing the intimacy of household life into public space. Its political effect is not secured by a proposition alone: an ordinary object, carried by a living person, makes the unfinished relationship between absence and expectation perceptible.

**Silence can be an active address.** The women’s protest challenges any assumption that politically meaningful testimony requires fluent verbal narration. Their bodies and objects present a demand to an audience even without a conventional speech. This connects Husanović’s argument to trauma theory while shifting the emphasis from what language cannot contain to what forms of public presence can nevertheless communicate.

**Embroidery acquires a different public function.** Husanović explicitly connects the pillows to work conventionally coded as feminine. The practice does not become politically important because women supposedly possess a natural disposition to peace or mourning. It becomes important because participants transform a socially assigned activity into an intervention that demands recognition and keeps the fate of the missing open to public attention.

**The memorial proposal extends this language.** Husanović discusses a proposed design for the Potočari memorial that placed embroidered pillows on a grassy slope, evoking Bosnia’s medieval stećak tombstones. She connects this imagery to Mak Dizdar’s poetry and to the sevdalinka “Vezak vezla,” a song about a woman embroidering while waiting for her beloved. The proposal draws on recognizable cultural forms while reworking their capacity to express injury, belonging, and unfinished expectation. [16]

**Postcolonial recovery need not mean purity.** Husanović reads such practices as possibilities for recovering belonging beyond the sovereign and identitarian arrangements that have produced exclusion. Culture here is not an intact national essence restored after interruption. It is a resource through which people can reconfigure relationships between the missing and remaining, the excluded and included, and past and future.

**Law and art serve different functions.** Husanović regards legal justice as vital, including the punishment of perpetrators, while arguing that legal categories cannot exhaust what survivors communicate. Courts must establish facts, assign responsibility, and reach decisions. Literature, film, memorial practices, and other cultural forms can return to dimensions of injury that remain after a case has formally closed. [16]

**The difference is complementary, not antagonistic.** An artistic work does not establish criminal guilt merely by being emotionally powerful, and a legal verdict does not resolve every social consequence of violence. Husanović draws on Shoshana Felman to explain why these languages can inform and unsettle each other. Justice requires both accountable judgment and continued responsiveness to experiences that exceed the terms of adjudication.

**The witnessing body changes the encounter.** A witness’s voice, hesitation, silence, or physical inability to continue can reveal the force of injury in ways that an abbreviated factual statement does not. Husanović’s insistence on embodiment therefore challenges a model in which institutions simply extract information from a person and move on. The form of the encounter matters because it can either renew subjection or acknowledge a political subject.

**Her reading keeps possibility distinct from achievement.** She describes the women’s memorialization as holding the potential for commonality and solidarity; she does not present the practice as proof that reconciliation or equality has been accomplished. Whether a recurring protest opens new relationships depends on how publics respond and whether participants retain authority over what their presence means.

**The section’s takeaway concerns unfinished justice.** Embodied and artistic witnessing can sustain claims that legal and commemorative closure might otherwise absorb, while remaining accountable to the specificity of the people and violence being remembered.

### Hope, cultural governance, and cooperative public knowledge

**Hope names a political capacity.** In “Resisting the Culture of Trauma in Bosnia and Herzegovina” from 2014, Husanović connects hope to practices that challenge, repoliticize, resist, and transform dominant narratives and technologies of governance. Education, theory, art, and activism matter because they can reopen possibilities presented as closed. Hope is thus neither confidence that recovery will occur nor a completed condition of postwar reconciliation. [3]

**The target is trauma as governance.** Husanović examines how the organization of postwar life can turn injury into a framework for managing populations. Her term “culturalised governance” directs attention to forms of rule operating through identities, representations, and authorized accounts of suffering. The relevant question becomes not simply whether institutions acknowledge trauma, but what relationships of knowledge and power that acknowledgment establishes.

**Past violence can govern the present.** The chapter uses the mass grave and the ghetto as privileged metaphors for the material consequences of political violence and for a continuing culture of trauma. Their significance is not exhausted by memorial reference to a finished event. They help expose relationships between death, segregation, vulnerability, and the management of everyday life in the post-Yugoslav region. [3]

**Technical knowledge is politically consequential.** The chapter’s discussion and notes engage forensic identification, accounts of the missing, institutional truth, and transitional justice. Such work can be indispensable for establishing identity and responsibility. Husanović’s critical question is whether administrative and technical success becomes a substitute for addressing the political relationships in which survivors and communities must continue to live.

**The critique is broader than nationalism.** Ethnonationalist appropriation is one danger, but Husanović also examines internationally circulating frameworks of security, development, and transitional justice. A survivor can be depoliticized through several routes: becoming a national emblem, an object of humanitarian concern, or a case managed through professional expertise. The postcolonial dimension concerns who defines the problem and who acquires the authority to determine an acceptable response.

**Hope requires reopening public agency.** Husanović identifies overlapping spaces in which people can question these arrangements rather than merely adapt to them. The relationship between knowledge and action is central: people become agents not only by telling stories but by participating in the production and interpretation of the knowledge governing their circumstances. [3]

| Public practice | Emancipatory operation | Contribution to hope |
|---|---|---|
| Education | Questions inherited categories and exclusions | Expands the capacity to interpret and act |
| Critical theory | Makes governing relationships contestable | Reopens what appears politically inevitable |
| Art | Reconfigures visibility, embodiment, and expression | Enables different encounters and identifications |
| Activism | Converts grievances into collective public claims | Builds practical solidarity and pressure for change |

**Concrete collaborations anchor this argument.** Husanović’s chapter points to the Yugoslav Studies Platform and to Grupa Spomenik’s *Mathemes of Reassociation*. It also refers to her 2008 Belgrade lecture on the politics of the missing, codification, mathematization, and depoliticization. These references locate the argument within actual intersections of artistic practice, public discussion, and critical knowledge, rather than an abstract faith in culture’s healing power. [3]

**Knowledge becomes a public good.** Husanović calls for intensified cooperative efforts in critical knowledge production. The alternative to expert monopoly is not abandoning expertise; it is organizing relationships in which knowledge can serve collective judgment and emancipatory action. The public character of knowledge matters because survivors’ conditions should not be interpreted exclusively by institutions to which they appear only as recipients of intervention. [3]

**Solidarity does not demand identical histories.** The possible commonality in the Srebrenica example links the missing and remaining, dead and living, excluded and included. Such connections depend on recognition across difference rather than the assertion that everyone has suffered the same thing. This makes hope an alternative to competitive victimhood without dissolving the specificity of particular losses.

**Equality changes the terms of recognition.** A politics based solely on compassion can leave the compassionate public in control and the survivor dependent on its generosity. Husanović’s emphasis on equality asks instead whether people can participate in shaping the institutions, narratives, and futures that affect them. The transition from pity to agency is therefore central to the relationship between witnessing and hope.

**Transformation remains conditional on practice.** The hopeful judgment holds where witnessing keeps interpretation contestable, creates cooperative relationships, and permits political demands beyond prescribed identities. It weakens where such practices are absorbed into fixed national myths, professional routines, or commemorative spectacles that leave underlying relationships untouched. These conditions explain what would distinguish transformative witnessing from another managed representation of suffering.

**Husanović’s horizon is consequently open-ended.** Hope means maintaining and enlarging the collective capacity to act otherwise, not promising that testimony will automatically produce recovery or that cultural activity by itself can secure justice.

### Comparing the three perspectives without flattening their differences

**The common mechanism is mediated memory.** None of these cases offers a direct route from historical experience to uncontested public meaning. Takk’s testimony moves through veteran networks, life writing, archives, and scholarly interpretation. Aleksievich’s witnesses speak through interviews and literary montage. Husanović examines public forms through which bodies, testimony, art, and institutions either sustain or restrict political agency.

| What threatens that horizon? | Collective innocence and displaced responsibility | Editorial hierarchy and absorbed difference | Appropriation, normalization, and lost political voice |

**Their speaking positions are fundamentally different.** Takk is an actor whose life story is the object of Kõresaar’s analysis; Kõresaar is not simply another exponent of his national interpretation. Aleksievich is an interviewer and literary composer whose witnesses partly share authorship. Husanović is a theorist engaging survivor practices and public interventions. Keeping these positions distinct prevents the comparison from treating autobiography, documentary literature, and political theory as interchangeable testimony.

**Continuity and fragmentation do different work.** Takk’s sustained chronological narration tries to reconcile successive roles within one patriotic life, although individual episodes retain ethical tension. Aleksievich’s montage gives historical importance to voices that need not combine into an internally complete biography. Husanović emphasizes the political force of testimony, including silence and embodiment, when established public forms cannot contain the claim being made.

**Trauma is not one narrative shape.** Takk’s fluent storytelling cautions against treating fragmentation as the universal signature of historical injury. Aleksievich’s edited fragments show how disruption can become formally perceptible without proving that every pause has a clinical meaning. Husanović adds that difficulty speaking can arise from political conditions of reception, including the refusal to recognize a claim except in terms that neutralize it.

**National recognition is both resource and danger.** For Takk, belonging to the national freedom story gives previously stigmatized experiences public legitimacy. For Aleksievich, the shared Soviet wartime past supplies the setting that individual recollections unsettle but do not entirely abandon. For Husanović, the demand to return survivors to settled identities can reproduce the very forms of community that enabled exclusion.

**The Holocaust exposes unequal integration.** In Takk’s memoir, Jewish suffering receives acknowledgment but is subordinated to an antitotalitarian narrative emotionally centered on Soviet rule. In Aleksievich’s work, Jewish voices can be present while the montage draws their experience into a broader Soviet account of suffering and victory. Husanović provides a related political question: can those recognized as victims determine the meaning of their experiences, or does recognition require their absorption into somebody else’s story?

**Postcolonialism must remain historically differentiated.** Estonia’s experience of annexation and successive occupations is not the same history as post-Yugoslav Bosnia’s wars, displacement, and internationally mediated reconstruction. Aleksievich’s Soviet and post-Soviet testimonial world likewise crosses national and imperial identifications. Wawrzyniak and Pakier’s description of Eastern Europe as several historically distinct “meso-regions” is therefore more useful than an undifferentiated regional model. [17]

**Recognition should not become a competition.** Takk’s demand that Soviet domination be understood addresses a genuine imbalance when national suffering is overlooked. Yet recognition becomes restrictive when it establishes a privileged victim whose history supplies the measure of everyone else’s experience. Aleksievich’s polyphony and Husanović’s solidarity point toward alternatives, provided that plurality and commonality do not erase distinct histories.

**Social transmission is not biological inheritance.** Wawrzyniak and Pakier, discussing Jeffrey Olick, explain that trauma can be externalized into family and social narratives without being transmitted like a genetic trait. This distinction helps connect the three perspectives: experiences acquire wider durability through stories, practices, institutions, and audiences. Their political afterlives depend on those forms of transmission, not on imagining a nation as one wounded individual. [17]

**Hope has different standing across the cases.** Takk’s narrative looks toward restored national continuity and deserved recognition. Aleksievich’s work enables an ethical encounter with previously subordinated experience. Husanović explicitly theorizes hope as the possibility of transforming relationships of power through collective practice. These horizons can intersect, but witnessing alone does not establish that a politics of solidarity or equality has followed.

**The evidence requires precise distinctions.** Takk’s chronology follows his 1993 memoir as analyzed by Kõresaar and her 2006 interview; the early-1970s transition is approximate in his account. “Continuous struggle for Estonian freedom” and “dual colonization” summarize the documented interpretive argument. Trauma here denotes narrative, cultural, and political processes rather than retrospective clinical diagnosis; the historical magnitudes cited are the sources’ rounded figures. The comparison concerns particular texts and public practices, not measured national consensus.

**The closing judgment is relational.** Kõresaar shows how a national freedom narrative can reconcile an ambiguous life while leaving its contradictions visible; Aleksievich demonstrates how attentive listening and literary polyphony can change whose war becomes history; Husanović asks whether witnessing gives people power to reshape the present. Their strongest shared contribution is to make recognition answerable to difference and responsibility, so that remembering domination does not become another means of silencing others.

**Svetlana Aleksievich: *War’s Unwomanly Face***

Svetlana Aleksievich’s central intervention in World War II memory is to make it polyphonic, intimate, and contested: women’s testimony recovers everyday combat, suffering, emotion, and moral difficulty that official histories and victory monuments leave out, rather than reproducing a single heroic military narrative. [79][81] The book therefore connects memory politics—who is authorized to represent the war—to trauma theory—how violence and loss are narrated and remembered—and to a cautious postcolonial question about whose experience is excluded.

**Edition and evidence basis**

The working English anchor is *The Unwomanly Face of War: An Oral History of Women in World War II*, by Svetlana Aleksievich (Светлана Алексиевич), translated by Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky, identified as a Random House Publishing Group 2018 reprint; the same record dates the original work to 1985. [81] Other records identify a Random House 2017 edition of 331 pages with the same translators [82], an English translation published in 2017 after Aleksievich’s 2015 Nobel Prize [79], and a separate Progress Publishers 1988 record of 247 pages that does not establish the first-publication date or supply the original-language title in the available extract. [80] These are retained as distinct edition records rather than collapsed into one publication date.

The book concerns Soviet women who fought on the Eastern Front in 1941–1945, mostly volunteers, in front-line, home-front, and occupied-territory settings as nurses, doctors, pilots, tank drivers, machine-gunners, and snipers. [78][81] The supplied sources report non-equivalent scales: up to a million women on the Soviet side [78], more than a million in the broader wartime settings [81][82], more than 500,000 Soviet women participating [80], more than 500 women interviewed, most of whom were not printed [78], over 500 women’s accounts [79], and more than 200 women speaking in the listed 1988 record. [80] These figures describe different populations and have no common denominator; they cannot support a percentage or direct comparison.

**Testimony and narrative form**

Aleksievich’s method is to collect and organize women’s Red Army testimony from 1941–1945 while placing the authorial role between journalism and fiction. [77] She uses ordinary witnesses “far from chancelleries and military headquarters” to describe war day by day—with enthusiasms, suffering, and anxieties—so that the “Great Russian Patriotic War” takes different colors through women’s accounts. [77] A review describes oral-history interviews in which Aleksievich removed her questions from the published text, obscuring her participation in “leading the witness”; the resulting book is a composite of fragments, reportage, and creation shaped by condensation and selection. [78] It is a history of “little people” and feelings rather than events: the women recount what happened to them, not what they caused, and none served as field officers or generals. [78]

Its collective form is consequently described as a “symphony of voices,” a “kaleidoscopic portrait” of war’s human side, and, in the Nobel-related description, a “history of emotions” and “history of the soul.” [81][82] Accounts of the research process vary: one synopsis reports four years and more than 100 cities, towns, settlements, and villages [80]; another reports thousands of miles and more than 100 towns [81][82]; a review reports hundreds of women interviewed over seven years. [79] These are source-reported descriptions of different aspects of the project, not a basis for calculating one duration or corpus.

**Gendered memory, silence, and trauma**

The intervention is political because women’s wartime roles were subsequently forgotten, stigmatized, or neglected. One account places the gathering of testimony more than 30 years after the women’s return, while another says that many interviewees had maintained a long silence or denied having been at the front. [78] The book is therefore described as recovering a hidden face of history. [77] A review contrasts the lively women’s testimony with victory monuments at Garni, Dushanbe, and the Kremlin: the monuments are mainly male and express patriotic fervor and stoical fighting spirit. [77] Another description says that official Soviet histories documented only male voices, sanitized horrors away from battlefield heroics, and substituted “the history of the victory” for the history of the war. [79]

Aleksievich also refuses to reduce women to passive victims. The accounts present them as combatants as well as victims and expose differentiated vulnerability, courage, violence, and killing. [79] One testimony describes a partisan ordered to poison German soldiers who later taught history but could not find words to explain the act of killing; the chapter title “A Human Being Is Greater than War” condenses this human scale. [79] Conversations with Soviet censors placed among the stories dramatize the tension between Aleksievich’s mediation, women’s attempts to speak in their own words, and demands for heroic “big history.” The censor objects to showing the filth of war, while Aleksievich insists on truth and the “little human being.” [79] The book thus moves testimony from past experience into an appeal to the present, including a denunciation of past and present fascism. [80]

**Husanović: witnessing, hope, and the Bosnian context**

Jasmina Husanović’s contribution is a Bosnia-specific, feminist and postcolonial account in which traumatic fractures generate practices of witnessing, while hope turns those practices toward collective political possibility rather than closure [117]. In this report, Bosnia and Herzegovina is treated as a source-specific Balkan and post-Yugoslav context within the broad Eastern European frame, not as a representative substitute for the whole region [42][117].

The relevant source base is plural. The precise anchor for Husanović’s account of gender, witnessing, postcoloniality and trauma is “The politics of gender, witnessing, postcoloniality and trauma: Bosnian feminist trajectories,” published in *Feminist Theory* in April 2009 and first published online on 1 April 2009 [117]. A separately identified 2010 text, *Reckoning with the ‘Bosnia Troubles’: Trauma, Witnessing, and Politics*, situates witnessing within post-war Bosnia and the Balkans [118]. Her chapter “Governance of life and femininity in Bosnia and Herzegovina: Reflections on affective politics and cultural production,” in *Genre and the (Post-)Communist Woman*, 1st edition, was first published by Routledge in 2014 [119]. An institutional profile identifies her 2020 monograph as *Culture, Community and Activism in Bosnia and Herzegovina: Emancipatory Trajectories* [120]. These works should not be collapsed: the 2009 article anchors the witnessing/postcoloniality/trauma formulation, the 2014 chapter gives the clearest account of equaliberty and hope, and the profile supplies contextual information rather than the full argument of the monograph.

**Politics of witnessing**

Husanović’s politics of witnessing is a creative approach to loss that joins local and global feminist and postcolonial theory and practice, subaltern perspectives, transnational feminist solidarities, and non-institutional spaces of thinking, judging and acting in Bosnia [117]. Witnessing therefore means more than observing or recording injury: it is a relational practice through which affected people, cultural producers and political actors make loss publicly intelligible and contest who has authority to represent it. Husanović describes postcolonial and biopolitical fractures, produced through the global/local political economy of capital and violence, as generating cultural, artistic and public practices of witnessing that “shake and reshuffle” projects of social and political transformation [117].

The 2010 Bosnia text makes the political mechanism explicit: witnessing trauma becomes resistance when survivors reclaim their narratives against dominant power structures; the act can disrupt established discourses and promote social and political change [118]. Its Bosnian setting includes post-war trauma, cultural and political exclusion, women’s marginalization, and an ongoing demand for justice [118]. Artistic expression, literature and memorial design are consequently not ornamental additions to memory politics. They can articulate suffering, supplement legal narratives, foster social transformation and build community solidarity; the text specifically treats culturally resonant memorial practice as a way of keeping women’s roles in memory-making visible [118]. It also warns that mythologization, medicalization and disappearance can suppress trauma narratives, dilute survivors’ experiences and make testimony politically compliant within dominant frameworks [118].

The 2014 chapter adds a gendered account of bearing witness but has a narrower evidentiary reach in the supplied passage: it says that Bosnia and Herzegovina continues to bear witness to “unhealing wounds” produced by national governance of life and death over the past few decades, but does not provide a fuller definition of witnessing as a political practice [119]. Husanović’s institutional profile supplies a complementary account of method: her work and fieldwork connect witnessing, equality, solidarity, trauma and emancipatory politics through social activism, cultural production and knowledge production, including participatory action research, critical pedagogies, collaborative ethnography and autoethnography in Bosnia and Herzegovina [120].

**Politics of hope**

Husanović’s politics of hope is not optimism detached from injury. In the 2009 article, hope appears as “hopeful political navigation” through injustices and injuries inflicted by global/local regimes of biopolitics and capital; its work is to reinvigorate gender, witnessing, postcoloniality and trauma scholarship and politics along creative feminist trajectories [117]. Hope is thus produced through practice—solidarity, cultural work, public action and the remaking of political relations—not merely through a positive attitude.

The 2014 chapter gives the concept a more explicit political form. Husanović grounds hope in “the proposition of the equaliberty in the face of emergencies” and links it to affirmative, universalizing imaginaries of political community and collectivity. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, this hope seeks to stitch together identitarian ruptures and lootings while confronting the continuing governance of life and death [119]. The same chapter argues that mass graves can become sites for governing the living; that victimization can operate as an offshoot or mask of commodification; and that the governance of female bodies and femininity is central to this twin logic [119]. Hope therefore contests the political and economic uses of vulnerability rather than simply promising psychological recovery.

The institutional profile translates this orientation into social practice: social movements and civil-society groups forge public languages of grief and hope and engage in social-justice politics in conflict-affected societies [120]. Read together, witnessing names the public labour of attending to and representing injury, while hope names the collective work of turning that attention toward equality, solidarity and future political agency [117][119][120].

**Bosnia’s memory field: acknowledgement, contestation and political agency**

A contextual account of post-war Bosnia and Herzegovina identifies the 1995 Srebrenica genocide as a turning point in public and political acknowledgement and in the construction of official and public commemorative culture [42]. It traces the development of the Srebrenica narrative through three stages: public activism from 1993 to 1997, a legalistic turn from 1998 to 2003, and a later memory turn [42]. Family associations and survivors broke silence by demanding truth and accountability, collecting documentation, cooperating with courts and criticising institutions. In 1996, family associations proposed transforming Potočari into a memorial complex with an educational centre and museum; after the NIOD report’s vague conclusions, they continued public campaigns and legal demands for truth and justice, including campaigns documented in 2002 [42].

The memory work moved across local, regional and international scales. The 2010 commemoration connected local politics, memory and culture in Bosnia and Herzegovina to the wider post-Yugoslav space and Europe through interactions among victim associations, the ICTY, courts, international organisations and political institutions [42]. War violence, globalization, displacement, a dispersed diaspora and relations between Bosnia and the Netherlands blurred the boundaries between local-national, regional and international contexts, creating a transnational arena at Srebrenica-Potočari and complicating the construction of an inclusive genocide narrative [42]. Public commemoration and collective burial could reconvene and re-ground survivors expelled from their homes, while movement and mourning encouraged recollection of pre-war life and enabled otherwise unlikely reunions [42].

These practices also show why hope is politically contested. The Srebrenica flower, promoted in 2011, links remembrance to a future-oriented symbol: its green centre signifies hope and “awakening,” while its white petals signify innocence and the victims of the genocide; wearing it acknowledges the genocide, respects the dead and expresses compassion for bereaved families [42]. The Peace March, held on 8–10 July 2010, brought survivors, activists and supporters together to protest the slow pace of justice and demand that indicted war criminals be brought to justice, which organisers described as a prerequisite for lasting peace in Bosnia and Herzegovina [42]. Return efforts and the memorial complex were likewise associated with reconciliation and the return of displaced persons [42].

At the same time, Bosnia’s memory field cannot be reduced to one emancipatory narrative. A counter-commemoration in Kravica, first organised in 2005 and documented on 12 July 2010, commemorated Serb civilian and military victims from the Srebrenica municipality who died during the Second World War and the 1990s war; Kravica became a major site of a counter-narrative to the Srebrenica genocide [42]. The contextual study notes competing victim associations, disagreements over judicial outcomes, attempts to create a “common narrative” alongside multiple narratives, and limited scholarly and non-academic information about the agencies behind the Kravica commemoration—the available analysis is largely restricted to media reporting [42]. These limits make Bosnia useful for studying witnessing and hope precisely because they expose unequal authority, competing victimhoods and the difficulty of producing an inclusive memory.

**Trauma, postcoloniality and comparative limits**

A broader trauma-theory source provides an important qualification for applying these concepts: no universal definition of trauma is established; meanings of trauma are historically constructed and shaped by access to power and experiences of oppression . Trauma theory is itself raced, classed and gendered, and is organized through the practices, technologies, narratives, institutions and moral arguments used to diagnose, study, treat and represent injury . Treating trauma only as an individual health problem can obscure structural social problems, while dominant Eurocentric definitions can erase racism, state-produced structural violence and other forms of harm . Husanović’s emphasis on gender, biopolitics, political economy, public representation and collective agency therefore connects trauma theory to postcolonial critique without making Bosnia identical to every other Eastern European setting [117][119].

Husanović’s work does not directly reconstruct Boris Takk’s life, verify his post-independence activities, establish the truth of his claim to have served Estonia from inside the Soviet system, or determine his stance on the Holocaust. Nor do the cited Husanović texts directly analyse Ene Kõresaar or Svetlana Aleksievich [117][119][120]. Their comparative value is instead methodological and ethical: they ask whose testimony is authorized, which structures produce injury, how national narratives marginalize or elevate particular victims, how memory travels through public and cultural forms, and whether witnessing produces solidarity and political agency. Applied to Takk and Kõresaar, this is a lens for testing a national freedom narrative against its omissions, not evidence that resolves those omissions. Applied to *War’s Unwomanly Face*, it offers a comparison about testimony, vulnerability and political possibility, not a claim that Husanović and Aleksievich use the same genre or method.

The resulting tension with a dual-colonization frame is productive but non-equivalent. Husanović’s emphasis on relational witnessing, structural power and competing or marginalized voices cautions against treating national victimhood as self-sufficient or allowing one regime’s violence to erase another community’s suffering. Bosnia’s competing commemorations show that an inclusive politics of memory must address responsibility and asymmetry without turning different histories into interchangeable injuries [42]. The comparison therefore supports a differentiated intersection of memory politics, trauma theory and postcolonialism: traumatic experience is mediated by power; memory is publicly contested; and hope is credible only when it creates conditions for agency, solidarity and more inclusive political community [117][119][120].
