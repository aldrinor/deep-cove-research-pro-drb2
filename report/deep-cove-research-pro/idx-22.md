# Electric Vehicle Life-Cycle Costs and Incentive Policies Through 2019

**EVs were competitive in selected applications:** by 31 December 2019, electric vehicles could match or beat internal combustion engine vehicles in life-cycle cost, but they were not universally cheaper. Raustad’s U.S. comparison placed the Nissan Leaf at $4,683 per year against $6,040 for the Hyundai Elantra over ten years, including resale value. [1] LeasePlan’s European fleet analysis reported lower EV costs in 56% of its scenarios and an average cost advantage of 5%. [2] The decisive trade-off was higher acquisition and depreciation exposure versus lower energy, maintenance, and tax costs.

**How the report proceeds:** the first two sections compile model-level costs and policies in the twelve requested countries. The analysis then explains why studies differ, identifies the circumstances in which EVs were cost-competitive, and evaluates how policy affects both ownership costs and adoption.

**Relative findings also support dollar comparisons:** let \(C\) denote the matched ICE vehicle’s ownership cost in USD under the same market, mileage, and ownership-period assumptions. **These calculations preserve the comparison:** each multiplier equals one plus the source’s percentage cost difference divided by 100. For example, the Leaf’s largest reported consolidated advantage gives \(C \times (1-0.113)=0.887C\). This measures the EV’s cost relative to its actual comparator; it does not substitute an unrelated vehicle’s dollar price.

**The compilation’s practical value:** absolute USD cases establish the scale of ownership costs, while conditional dollar relationships extend the comparison to further models without imposing a false common ownership period.

**Canada combines regulation and proposed assistance:** the IEA’s 2019 report identifies Quebec’s existing ZEV mandate as a provincial policy comparable to California’s. Separately, the federal announcement of 17 April 2019 proposed purchase incentives, with intended eligibility for purchases and leases from 1 May. It differentiated support between BEVs and longer-range PHEVs on one side and shorter-range PHEVs on the other. [7][11]

**Switzerland also had federal tax advantages:** the 2017 Graubünden policy document records EV exemption from the federal automobile tax, normally 4% of vehicle value, and the absence of mineral-oil tax on electric propulsion. These national tax provisions operated alongside different cantonal approaches, rather than a uniform nationwide purchase subsidy. [12]

**Eligibility determines the actual benefit:** Spain’s MOVES decree required scrappage of an M1 passenger vehicle more than ten years old or an N1 light commercial vehicle more than seven years old for the specified new-vehicle purchases. Italy’s purchase contribution varied with emissions and scrappage and applied to qualifying new M1 vehicles with list prices below approximately $56,180, excluding VAT. These conditions make the applicable incentive an individual calculation rather than a country-wide constant. [13][15]

**Regulatory privileges complement financial support:** Germany’s 2015 Elektromobilitätsgesetz allowed municipalities to provide preferential parking, bus-lane access, and access to restricted traffic zones for qualifying vehicles. California’s ZEV program instead places obligations on manufacturers: its scheduled credit requirement rises from 4.5% in 2018 to 22% in 2025, with a $5,000 penalty per deficit credit. Those credit percentages are regulatory accounting requirements, not direct vehicle-sales shares. [17][18]

**The policy snapshot’s main finding:** by 2019, EV promotion extended well beyond purchase subsidies, affecting acquisition, recurring taxation, charging access, manufacturer behavior, and the practical usefulness of electric vehicles.

## 3. Why EV life-cycle costs vary across models and studies

**Variation reflects different economic questions:** even within Raustad’s common ten-year, resale-excluded assumptions, BEV annual costs ranged from $4,534 for the Chevrolet Spark EV to $9,586 for the Tesla Model S—a calculated 2.11-fold spread. Across studies, additional differences arise because ownership periods, vehicle specifications, utilization, taxation, financing, and cost boundaries change simultaneously. [1]

### Vehicle characteristics: batteries, efficiency, segment, and depreciation

**Battery size creates a capital-cost trade-off:** larger batteries increase driving range but also increase the amount of expensive storage purchased with the vehicle. Wolfram and Lutsey’s 2016 review cites an assumption that batteries represented about 75% of BEV powertrain cost. That is a share of powertrain cost—not of the complete vehicle or its lifetime expenditure—and explains why battery capacity strongly affects acquisition costs. [5]

**Chemistry and scale change unit costs:** Lutsey and Nicholas reported 2018 battery costs of $100–$150/kWh for nickel cobalt aluminum oxide, or NCA, packs, compared with $150–$200/kWh for nickel manganese cobalt oxide, or NMC, packs typical of other manufacturers. Their review also cited a volume-weighted industry pack average of $176/kWh. The technology categories were associated with different production volumes, so their price difference should not be attributed to chemistry alone. [19]

**Capacity differences can be financially substantial:** as an explicitly illustrative estimate, an additional 20 kWh priced at the sourced $100–$200/kWh range would add $2,000–$4,000 in pack cost: 20 kWh × $100–$200/kWh. This is a component-cost illustration, not a quoted retail-price premium. Vehicle retail prices also incorporate integration, other equipment, manufacturer margins, and market positioning.

**Production scale affects more than materials:** a 2019 *Batteries* study modeled a 20-kWh NCA pack and found a Pearson correlation of −0.9721 between logarithmic production volume and pack cost, compared with 0.1562 between active-material price and pack cost. Within that model, manufacturing scale was much more strongly associated with cost than the tested material-price changes. The correlation is not a universal causal elasticity; it demonstrates why studies assuming different factory sizes can produce very different battery costs. [20]

**Study date matters materially:** BloombergNEF’s battery-price survey, summarized in December 2019, placed average pack prices at $156/kWh in 2019 against a quoted $1,100/kWh in 2010. Those endpoints imply a calculated reduction of approximately 86%: \((1,100-156)/1,100\). Thus, an early-decade LCC calculation can represent a substantially different technology-cost environment from a late-2019 calculation, even when both studies examine a vehicle called an “electric car.” [21]

**Lower battery prices need not lower LCC proportionately:** manufacturers can use cost reductions to increase range, power, or equipment instead of reducing the selling price. A 2019 analysis of German electric and plug-in hybrid cars found strong technological learning in prices normalized by vehicle power, yet user costs did not decline in the same way because the vehicle mix shifted toward larger, more powerful models. This separates improvement in technology cost from improvement in the cost of the vehicle consumers actually buy. [22]

**Range has value beyond energy savings:** a larger battery can make more trips feasible without intermediate charging, reduce dependence on public chargers, and improve suitability for a household’s only car. Conversely, paying for rarely used range raises fixed cost without generating proportional operating savings. The IEA’s 2019 report therefore emphasized matching battery capacity to actual travel needs and avoiding battery oversizing, especially in commercial vehicles. [7]

**PHEVs require a separate comparison:** a plug-in hybrid buys some electric-driving capability while retaining combustion capability. Its smaller battery can reduce initial capital requirements relative to a long-range BEV, but its energy expenditure depends on how often it is charged and how much travel exceeds electric range. Vincentric explicitly noted that imposing 15,000 miles annually on every model could force a PHEV to use more gasoline than a short-distance commuter would use in practice. [3]

**Vehicle segment can outweigh propulsion type:** comparing a premium electric sedan with an inexpensive gasoline hatchback mixes drivetrain economics with differences in size, performance, and equipment. Lebeau and colleagues’ 2013 three-segment analysis found EVs cost-attractive only in its premium segment. By 2019, LeasePlan found a different pattern across its selected model pairs: the Leaf and E-Tron were favorable in all consolidated combinations, whereas the Model 3 remained more expensive than its selected BMW comparator. The relevant question is therefore whether an EV is competitive with an appropriately matched alternative, not whether every electric model is cheaper than every conventional model. [23][2]

**Depreciation creates another major difference:** Vincentric’s selected BEVs experienced 38% worse average depreciation than its selected non-luxury ICE vehicles. The article connected resale pressure partly to rapid technological progress: an early BEV offering roughly 80 miles of range could face competition from newer vehicles offering more than 200 miles. Technological improvement can therefore reduce the cost of a new generation while increasing depreciation for an older generation. [3]

**Vehicle-level judgment:** EV LCC differences reflect the quantity and quality of transport service purchased—especially range, size, performance, battery technology, and resale exposure—not propulsion technology alone.

### Study design: what is counted and over what period

**Cost boundaries alter the answer:** a transparent owner-cost calculation can be organized around acquisition, infrastructure, operating expenses, replacement costs, and resale. In a cash-purchase formulation, the basic relationship is:

\[
LCC=P-I+K+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{E_t+M_t+A_t+X_t+B_t}{(1+r)^t}-\frac{R_T}{(1+r)^T}.
\]

**Each term has a distinct role:** \(P\) is the purchase price, \(I\) the purchase incentive, \(K\) charging equipment and installation, \(E_t\) energy expenditure, \(M_t\) maintenance and repairs, \(A_t\) insurance, \(X_t\) recurring taxes and fees, \(B_t\) battery replacement, and \(R_T\) resale value. The discount rate \(r\) converts later cash flows into present value. A financed-purchase model substitutes down payments and loan cash flows for the cash purchase price; it should not count both the full purchase price and repayment of the same principal.

**Published studies implement different boundaries:** the following comparison shows why similarly labeled LCC or total cost of ownership, or TCO, results can answer different questions.

| Study | Ownership period | Utilization | Important cost treatment | Charging treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raustad, 2017 [1] | 5, 10, 15 years | 12,330 miles/year baseline | Incentives, financing, resale variants | PV/workplace variants examined |
| Vincentric, 2019 [3] | 36 months | 15,000 miles/year | Eight ownership-cost categories | Unhindered charging assumed |
| LeasePlan, 2019 [2] | 36, 48, 60 months | 20,000–40,000-km categories | Operational-lease service package | Home/work/public energy mix |
| Brussels van study, 2019 [24] | 8 years | 16,000 km/year | Discounted business TCO, excluding VAT | Charging infrastructure excluded |

**Ownership horizon changes cost recovery:** an EV’s purchase premium is paid near the beginning, while energy and maintenance savings accumulate with use. A short ownership period can therefore favor a lower-priced ICE vehicle; a longer period can favor an EV if the battery remains serviceable and operating savings persist. This mechanism explains why an exploratory Irish comparison found EVs more expensive initially, almost all cheaper after five years, and every electric car in its selected sample cheaper over ten years. [25]

**Annualized figures require careful interpretation:** Raustad’s Leaf cost falls from $5,360 per year over five years to $4,683 over ten years, but that does not by itself establish the optimal replacement interval. Comparing one fifteen-year ownership with three successive five-year ownerships requires accounting for the purchase and resale of the replacement vehicles. Raustad expressly distinguished comparing vehicles over a common period from choosing between unequal replacement strategies. [1]

**Terminal resale can reverse apparent comparisons:** the Leaf’s reported ten-year annual figure is $5,352 without terminal resale and $4,683 with resale—a calculated difference of $669 per year. That difference does not represent lower electricity consumption or improved maintenance. It represents recognition of an asset that the owner still possesses at the end of the accounting period. A comparison that credits resale to one powertrain but not the other would therefore be structurally biased.

**Battery replacement introduces discontinuities:** a study assuming no replacement over ten years can produce a much lower total than one charging a replacement near the end of ownership. Raustad placed replacement in year eleven, whereas the Canadian fuel-and-maintenance comparison by Logtenberg and colleagues assumed that the battery lasted the vehicle’s life. These assumptions allocate very different risks to the owner, even before differences in replacement prices are considered. [1][26]

**Operating savings are not complete LCC:** the Canadian study found approximately 71% savings in fuel and maintenance for its selected BEV comparisons. It explicitly excluded depreciation and insurance and treated battery replacement as outside maintenance. Its result is strong evidence of lower selected operating expenses, but the percentage should not be interpreted as a 71% reduction in the entire cost of owning a vehicle. [26]

**Charging costs require consistent allocation:** a home charger dedicated to one vehicle can reasonably enter that owner’s cost calculation. A shared workplace charger should be allocated across its users or recovered through charging prices. Including both the infrastructure’s full capital cost and an electricity tariff that already recovers that capital would double-count part of the expense. Conversely, leaving infrastructure outside a business TCO calculation answers a narrower question about vehicles using an already provided charging network.

**Study-design judgment:** much of the apparent disagreement between EV cost studies is explained by differences in accounting boundaries, ownership periods, residual values, and replacement assumptions before any disagreement about vehicle technology begins.

### Government policies: different owners face different effective prices

**The incentive is part of the scenario:** Raustad’s Leaf remained cheaper than its Elantra comparator after removal of the $7,500 federal incentive, but its advantage became substantially smaller. Other model selections, including Vincentric’s three-year fleet comparison, were more dependent on federal support. This means that “with incentives” and “without incentives” are separate economic cases, not interchangeable descriptions of the same vehicle. [1][3]

**Brand and purchase date also matter:** the Congressional Research Service reported in June 2019 that Tesla and General Motors had reached the federal credit’s 200,000-vehicle manufacturer threshold and that their credits were phasing down. Thus, a credit assumption appropriate to an earlier U.S. model could overstate the assistance received by a purchaser of another brand or at another date in 2019. [8]

**Taxes influence several cost components:** registration-tax exemptions reduce acquisition cost; ownership-tax exemptions reduce annual expenses; company-car rules affect the employee or business user’s taxable costs. An EV can consequently be attractive as a company car but less attractive to a private household buying the same model. The Netherlands, Germany, Portugal, and Belgium illustrate why identifying the taxpayer is as important as identifying the vehicle. [14]

**Policy can alter the comparator too:** a bonus–malus system simultaneously rewards low-emission vehicles and penalizes higher-emission alternatives. An EV’s relative advantage can therefore improve even if its own manufacturer price does not fall. France’s emissions-based bonus–malus and Italy’s Ecobonus/Ecotassa structure operate through this two-sided mechanism. [14][15]

**Policy effects depend on use:** a parking concession benefits an owner who regularly pays for parking more than an owner who parks free at home and work. Likewise, access to a restricted urban area can be highly valuable to a delivery fleet but irrelevant to a rural household. These measures can change the practical attractiveness of an EV without creating the same monetary benefit for every owner.

**Policy-related judgment:** differences in eligibility, taxpayer status, purchase timing, and local privileges can produce different LCCs for an otherwise identical vehicle.

### Economic environment: energy prices, finance, and actual utilization

**Relative energy prices drive operating savings:** Raustad used Orlando regular gasoline at $3.206 per U.S. gallon and residential electricity at $0.1142/kWh. Under its ten-year assumptions, the Leaf’s electricity expenditure was $3,919 against $10,931 in gasoline for the Elantra—a calculated $7,012 difference. Those savings arise from the combination of energy efficiency and local energy prices, rather than from either factor alone. [1]

**A worked example shows the mechanism:** using the study’s 33-mpg Elantra input gives an illustrative gasoline cost of $3.206 ÷ 33 = $0.0972 per mile. Applying its Leaf consumption range of 0.27–0.33 kWh per mile gives electricity costs of $0.0308–$0.0377 per mile: consumption × $0.1142/kWh. The resulting estimated energy-only saving is approximately $0.0595–$0.0663 per mile.

**Simple payback is narrower than LCC:** the same study’s listed purchase prices imply a $4,230 Leaf premium after a full incentive: $31,415 − $19,685 − $7,500. Dividing that premium by the energy-only saving gives an illustrative recovery distance of approximately 64,000–71,000 miles. Without the incentive, the $11,730 premium implies approximately 177,000–197,000 miles. These calculations deliberately isolate purchase price and energy; they do not reproduce the study’s full insurance, financing, maintenance, tax, and resale calculation.

**Charging time changes the relevant tariff:** a 2019 critical-price and critical-distance study found that alternative charging patterns could reduce the break-even distance by approximately 40% relative to peak-price charging. Applying its carbon-emissions tax increased the critical electricity price—the highest electricity price consistent with the modeled EV advantage—by 8.5%. These are vehicle- and scenario-specific sensitivities, but they show how the same EV can cross from disadvantage to advantage without a hardware change. [27]

**Annual mileage determines accumulated savings:** low-mileage owners spread acquisition costs over fewer kilometers and earn fewer annual energy savings. Davis’s analysis of the 2017 U.S. National Household Travel Survey found that all-electric vehicles averaged 6,300 miles annually and PHEVs 7,800, compared with 10,200 for gasoline and diesel vehicles. These measured household averages were below both Raustad’s 12,330-mile baseline and Vincentric’s 15,000-mile fleet assumption. [28]

**Observed mileage does not prove a constraint:** Davis’s figures describe how sampled households used their vehicles; they do not establish that EVs inherently cannot serve high-mileage drivers. Ownership selection, second-car use, household travel patterns, and model availability can all affect the average. Nevertheless, transferring a fleet model’s high-utilization savings directly to the average household would overstate annual benefits when the household actually drives less.

**Discount rates change future savings’ weight:** Raustad used a 4.53% discount-rate input, while the Brussels van study reported a −1.29% real rate, calculated by subtracting its 1.8% inflation assumption from a 0.51% nominal bond-rate input. A higher positive discount rate reduces the present value of future operating savings, tending to disadvantage a technology whose benefits arrive after a larger initial purchase. Different discounting assumptions can therefore change the result even when the undiscounted expenses are identical. [1][24]

**Consumption measurements introduce further variation:** Wolfram and Lutsey’s review reported real-world fuel consumption commonly 20%–40% above official type-approval measurements. A study using optimistic laboratory consumption will produce different energy costs from one using on-road data. For PHEVs, charging frequency adds a further source of variation because it changes the division of travel between electricity and liquid fuel. [5]

**Economic-environment judgment:** the most transferable result is the mechanism—higher use and cheaper electricity usually improve EV economics—not a particular cost figure detached from its local prices, financing conditions, and travel pattern.

## 4. Were EVs competitive with ICEVs by the end of 2019?

**The answer is a qualified yes:** EVs had achieved LCC competitiveness in identifiable passenger-car and commercial-vehicle applications by the cutoff. The evidence supports neither “EVs were always cheaper” nor “EVs needed subsidies in every case.” The most convincing judgment comes from matched comparisons and explicit sensitivity tests.

### The U.S. Leaf–Elantra comparison: an advantage with and without the credit

**The Leaf retained a measurable advantage:** Raustad’s resale-inclusive results show lower Leaf costs across both ownership periods, including when the federal incentive is removed. Percentage savings below are calculated as the annual dollar saving divided by the Elantra’s annual cost. [1]

| Scenario | Leaf, USD/year | Elantra, USD/year | Leaf saving, USD/year | Saving |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 years, incentive included | $5,360 | $7,076 | $1,716 | 24.3% |
| 5 years, incentive removed | $6,733 | $7,076 | $343 | 4.8% |
| 10 years, incentive included | $4,683 | $6,040 | $1,357 | 22.5% |
| 10 years, incentive removed | $5,369 | $6,040 | $671 | 11.1% |

**The credit strengthened rather than created parity:** in this particular comparison, the Leaf’s five-year advantage narrowed from $1,716 to $343 annually when support was removed. That distinction matters: the vehicle was competitive without the credit under the study’s assumptions, but its advantage became more sensitive to changes in residual value, insurance, charging expense, or other owner-specific costs.

**Operating assumptions contributed substantially:** the study emphasized lower electricity expenditure, but its itemized ten-year present-value costs also show a large insurance difference. The following figures come from the study’s cost table, with savings calculated as ICE expenditure minus EV expenditure. [1]

| Ten-year cost component | Leaf, USD | Elantra, USD | Leaf saving, USD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Energy | $3,919 | $10,931 | $7,012 |
| Insurance | $17,532 | $24,899 | $7,367 |
| Maintenance | $5,054 | $6,723 | $1,669 |
| Tires | $787 | $787 | $0 |
| Taxes and license | $3,184 | $2,511 | −$673 |

**Energy alone does not explain everything:** the insurance saving in this example is slightly larger than the energy saving. Thus, the full LCC advantage should not be described as a pure consequence of drivetrain efficiency. The result also depends on the study’s model-specific insurance inputs, maintenance assumptions, acquisition costs, financing, and resale treatment.

**The incentive’s timing affects its value:** deleting the credit raises the Leaf’s reported annual cost by $1,373 over five years and $686 over ten years. Those values differ from simply dividing $7,500 by five or ten because the model discounts cash flows and places the federal incentive in its second-year accounting. A later tax benefit and an immediate dealer discount can therefore have different financing and present-value effects even when their face amounts match. [1]

**The U.S. case’s judgment:** an affordable BEV could outperform a compact gasoline vehicle without purchase assistance, but the magnitude of that advantage was sensitive to ownership-specific costs.

### Norway, Finland, and Ireland: advantage, parity, and delayed recovery

**Norway provides a strong favorable case:** Lindberg and Fridstrøm’s five-year comparison gives estimated converted totals of $28,574 for the LEAF and $45,643 for the comparable ICE vehicle at 15,000 km annually. The calculated saving is approximately $17,069, or 37.4%, using the common conversion reference. Because both totals use the same exchange rate, the percentage advantage is unaffected by currency conversion. [4]

**Efficiency and taxation reinforce each other:** the Norwegian comparison showed approximately 81% lower energy expenditure, 13% lower maintenance, and 86% lower charges in its vehicle-tax row for the LEAF. This is not simply evidence that electricity contains cheaper energy. It reflects the combined effect of vehicle efficiency, energy prices, and fiscal treatment in a market deliberately structured to favor low-emission vehicles. [4]

**Finland supplies a parity counterpoint:** Klemola’s February 2019 comparison found approximately equal lifetime costs for the Volkswagen e-Golf, gasoline Golf, and diesel Golf. It used a 298,000-km lifetime scope and applied January 2019 prices and taxes throughout that lifetime. The comparison supports competitiveness, but not an automatic electric-car cost advantage. [6]

**Irish results illustrate ownership-duration effects:** the 2019 exploratory report hosted by Ireland’s Central Statistics Office found electric cars more expensive initially and through the first year. Almost all examined EVs became cheaper after five years, and all electric cars in its sample were cheaper over ten years. Its calculations included depreciation, taxation, servicing, maintenance, fuel, vehicle registration tax, and home-charger installation, while assuming new cars were bought outright rather than financed. [25]

**These findings are economically consistent:** Norway’s strong advantage, Finland’s approximate parity, and Ireland’s delayed recovery can coexist because the initial price gap and the stream of operating and tax savings differ. None requires assuming that one country’s result applies to every other market.

**The regional passenger-car judgment:** by 2019, EVs could be cheaper or approximately equal in lifetime cost, but the timing and size of the benefit depended heavily on national conditions and ownership duration.

### European fleet evidence: a small average advantage concealing large differences

**LeasePlan reported a majority of favorable cases:** its 2019 study reported EV costs below ICE costs in 508 of 912 scenario entries, equivalent to 55.7%, rounded to 56%. The remaining 404 entries, or 44.3%, favored ICE vehicles. Its consolidated average EV TCO was 5% lower. This is evidence about the publisher’s selected fleet portfolio—not the proportion of all European buyers who would save money. [2]

**The cost structure explains the balance:** higher depreciation and interest were offset by substantially lower taxes, energy expenditure, and maintenance. These differences are percentages of each corresponding ICE cost component; they are not percentages of a common total and should not be added together.

| Cost component | EV difference versus ICE | Economic interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Depreciation and interest | +17% | Higher capital-cost exposure |
| Maintenance | −23% | Lower servicing requirements |
| Tires | +2% | Slightly higher wear costs |
| Insurance | +6% | Higher insured vehicle value |
| Management fee | 0% | No consolidated difference |
| Taxes | −88% | Strong fiscal advantage |
| Fuel/electricity | −54% | Lower energy expenditure |
| Total TCO | −5% | Net EV advantage |

**The aggregate result is not technological inevitability:** taxes were the most favorable percentage difference, while depreciation and interest remained materially higher. Consequently, the 5% total advantage combined engineering benefits with fiscal advantages and leasing-market assumptions. A result driven partly by tax treatment can change when policy changes, even if the vehicle itself does not.

**Actual model pairs reveal the trade-offs:** the selected consolidated scenarios below reproduce the report’s term and mileage-category labels. Negative percentages mean the EV is cheaper than its matched ICE comparator. [2]

| Term/mileage category | Zoe vs 208 | Leaf vs Golf | Model 3 vs 3 Series | E-Tron vs GLE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 36 months / 20,000 km | +16.2% | −2.4% | +14.2% | −13.9% |
| 36 months / 40,000 km | +1.5% | −8.1% | +9.4% | −16.7% |
| 48 months / 40,000 km | −2.7% | −11.3% | +6.7% | −16.8% |

| 60 months / 40,000 km | −2.3% | −9.6% | +8.6% | −5.5% |

**The Zoe demonstrates a crossover:** its operating savings were insufficient to offset higher costs in the shorter, lower-mileage combinations, but it became cheaper in selected longer or higher-mileage cases. The Leaf and E-Tron were cheaper in all nine consolidated combinations published for their model pairs, whereas the selected Model 3 remained more expensive in all nine. These are distinct model outcomes, not evidence for a single EV-wide ranking.

**Matching quality changes the interpretation:** LeasePlan’s Model 3 was a 274-kW long-range dual-motor vehicle, while the BMW comparator was a 140-kW 320d xDrive. The appendix explicitly notes that a more powerful BMW could have been selected but was not consistently available across countries. By contrast, the Leaf and Golf pair both had 110-kW power ratings. The Tesla comparison therefore includes a substantial performance difference alongside the propulsion difference. [2]

**Higher mileage is not strictly monotonic:** some published cells become less favorable at the highest mileage or longest term. This does not overturn the general operating-savings mechanism. It shows that depreciation schedules, residual assumptions, service costs, and the composition of available vehicle and country cases can prevent the consolidated result from moving smoothly with distance.

**The fleet evidence’s judgment:** EVs were credible cost-saving fleet options in 2019, but a modest average advantage concealed model-specific crossovers and comparisons influenced by performance and specification choices.

**Country effects reach beyond electricity tariffs:** LeasePlan attributed the spread to fiscal treatment, vehicle and labor costs, and demand effects on both new-vehicle and used-vehicle prices. An EV benefiting from strong registration-tax relief and favorable residual demand can outperform an identical powertrain sold into a market with weaker support and lower-priced conventional alternatives.

**The Spanish result is especially instructive:** Spain had purchase and charging support in 2019, yet only 11% of the represented LeasePlan scenarios favored EVs. The presence of an incentive program therefore does not establish cost parity. Incentive size, eligibility, vehicle pricing, the chosen comparator, and the rest of the cost structure still determine whether the support closes the gap.

**The geographic judgment:** local break-even calculations are more useful than importing a Norwegian or Dutch ownership-cost result into a different market.

### Commercial vehicles: small vans approached parity before all large vans did

**Commercial results varied by vehicle class:** the 2019 Brussels-Capital Region study used eight-year ownership and 16,000 km annually. It found near parity for several small electric vans, a substantial advantage for its electric Nissan comparison, and higher costs for the selected large electric vans. [24]

| Electric vehicle | Conventional comparator | Reported EV TCO result |
|---|---|---|
| Peugeot Partner electric | Comparable diesel Partner | Within 1% |
| Renault Kangoo ZE-r | Comparable diesel Kangoo | Within 1% |
| Renault Kangoo ZE-b | Comparable diesel Kangoo | Within 1% |
| Nissan NV200 electric | Diesel NV300 | Almost 15% lower |
| Renault Master electric | Comparable diesel van | Around 15% higher |
| Volkswagen Crafter electric | Comparable diesel van | Around 15% higher |
| Maxus EV80 | Comparable diesel van | Around 15% higher |

**Battery and service requirements help explain differences:** the EV80 had a 56.4-kWh battery, compared with 33 kWh for the electric Master and 35.8 kWh for the electric Crafter. The larger pack provided greater range but also increased capital exposure. For freight applications, vehicle usefulness includes payload, cargo volume, route coverage, and charging downtime, so a low cost per vehicle is not sufficient if it requires additional vehicles to deliver the same service. [24]

**Utilization thresholds were vehicle-specific:** the Kangoo ZE-b became lower-cost than the diesel Kangoo above 15,000 km per year, while the electric NV200 became more competitive at approximately 3,000 km annually. The Nissan result also involved different model generations because the diesel NV200 had been replaced by the NV300. Its favorable percentage should therefore be interpreted as the study’s practical alternative comparison rather than a perfectly controlled drivetrain experiment. [24]

**Residual battery value could change parity:** the Brussels sensitivity analysis found that assigning the EV80 battery a 50% residual value after eight years reduced its TCO by approximately 14%, bringing it close to diesel parity. Other large electric vans did not reach break-even even under the theoretical 100% battery-residual assumption, although their cost gaps narrowed substantially. This demonstrates that second-life value can matter without being sufficient to rescue every vehicle configuration. [24]

**High utilization can create replacement costs:** a 2016 German delivery-vehicle study challenged the usual claim that more mileage always improves EV competitiveness. It found that medium-duty EVs benefited from higher mileage only when operating savings exceeded the additional battery-replacement costs associated with intensive use. Long battery warranties were particularly important for double-shift operation. [29]

**The commercial-vehicle judgment:** route-matched small electric vans could be competitive by 2019, while larger or intensively operated vehicles required closer attention to battery investment, replacement exposure, and the quantity of transport service delivered.

### Counterexamples establish the boundary of the conclusion

**Brunei remained an ICE-favorable case:** its July 2019 techno-economic study found EVs more expensive than both conventional and non-plug-in hybrid vehicles, with acquisition cost contributing substantially to EV LCC. The authors identified a conditional policy-and-price scenario in which a $4,100 initial subsidy combined with gasoline at $0.70 per liter would allow EVs to compete comfortably. That was a modeled proposal, not an observed incentive package. [30]

**The U.S. fleet ranking was incentive-sensitive:** Vincentric found the Prius Prime first overall at $0.37 per mile under its three-year assumptions. After federal rebates were removed, the Prius Prime remained first, the Hyundai Ioniq Plug-In fell to fifth, and conventional ICE vehicles dominated the top ten. This supports the competitiveness of particular PHEVs while showing that BEV competitiveness could remain fragile over a short fleet holding period. [3]

**The overall competitiveness judgment:** by the end of 2019, EVs were an economically competitive alternative in selected markets and uses—not a universally lower-cost replacement for ICEVs. The balance favored EVs when acquisition premiums were moderate, charging was affordable, utilization was sufficient, residual values were favorable, and tax or operating savings accumulated. It favored ICEVs when those conditions reversed.

## 5. Policy objectives, LCC reduction, and widespread adoption

**The central policy role was transitional:** governments sought to overcome a period in which EVs could offer lower operating costs and public environmental benefits while still imposing higher purchase costs, infrastructure needs, and unfamiliar risks on buyers. Effective policies addressed those barriers together rather than assuming that a purchase rebate alone would create a mature market. [7]

### Main objectives: environmental benefits, energy security, and market development

**The objectives extend beyond cheaper cars:** the policies can be grouped by the public outcome they seek and the mechanism connecting that outcome to vehicle adoption.

| Policy objective | Relevant measures or examples | Main economic channel |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce climate emissions | Emissions-based taxes and standards | Changes relative vehicle costs |
| Improve urban air quality | Access rules and clean public fleets | Rewards low local emissions |
| Reduce petroleum dependence | Electrification and charging deployment | Substitutes electricity for oil |
| Improve early affordability | Purchase aid and registration-tax relief | Reduces initial ownership burden |
| Develop domestic industry | Turkish manufacturing support; battery policy | Encourages investment and scale |
| Overcome network barriers | Charger grants and EV-ready buildings | Improves convenience and access |

**Climate and air quality are related but distinct:** a BEV eliminates combustion exhaust at the point of use, which is valuable in dense urban areas. Its life-cycle climate effect also depends on electricity generation, battery production, vehicle manufacture, and disposal. The IEA’s 2019 assessment found lower life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions for average electric cars than for an average gasoline car under the global-average electricity assumptions, but substantially different results across power systems. [7]

**Low private LCC is not environmental proof:** the Brunei study illustrates this distinction particularly clearly. Alongside its unfavorable EV financial result, it found higher EV greenhouse-gas emissions under the local energy-chain assumptions and recommended cleaner electricity and improved power-plant efficiency. A subsidy can improve a purchaser’s financial calculation without changing the electricity system that determines much of the environmental outcome. [30]

**Energy security motivates fuel substitution:** electrification reduces reliance on petroleum products and exposes transport to a different set of energy sources and supply chains. This can diversify energy dependence, although battery materials and manufacturing introduce their own sourcing concerns. The IEA’s 2019 report therefore treated battery-material traceability, sustainable sourcing, reuse, and recycling as part of the policy agenda, rather than assuming electrification eliminates resource dependence. [7]

**Industrial policy seeks future cost reductions:** Turkey’s 2019 manufacturing provision targeted qualifying domestic production and R&D rather than directly compensating every vehicle buyer. Its potential contribution to LCC operates through investment, production capability, and eventual cost competitiveness. Whether consumers receive lower prices depends on successful production, scale, competition, and the transmission of manufacturing savings into vehicle prices. [10]

**Policy objectives require separate tests:** vehicle affordability, emissions reduction, domestic employment, and adoption are not interchangeable outcomes. A measure can increase registrations yet deliver expensive emissions reductions, or support industrial development before generating an immediate reduction in owner costs.

**The objectives judgment:** EV policy is best understood as a combination of environmental, energy, affordability, and market-development policy, with success measured against the objective actually being pursued.

### Purchase incentives reduce the upfront gap, but delivery and eligibility matter

**A subsidy directly changes owner LCC:** in the basic owner-cost equation, a purchase subsidy reduces the acquisition term. In Raustad’s listed Leaf–Elantra price comparison, the $7,500 incentive covered a calculated 63.9% of the $11,730 initial price difference: $7,500 ÷ $11,730. It left a $4,230 purchase premium to be recovered through other advantages. This illustrates why an incentive can materially affect adoption without making an EV’s sticker price equal to an ICEV’s. [1]

**Immediate discounts also address liquidity:** a buyer may understand that lower running costs will repay a premium over several years yet still struggle to finance the larger purchase. Italy’s 2019 scheme delivered the contribution as a seller discount; the manufacturer or importer subsequently reimbursed the seller and recovered the amount through a tax credit. That delivery mechanism can affect the cash needed at purchase differently from a later tax benefit. [15]

**Scrappage support changes the net calculation:** Spain’s older-vehicle requirement tied EV promotion to retirement of older vehicles, while Italy varied support with qualifying scrappage. The gross grant is not necessarily the owner’s complete financial gain because surrendering a usable vehicle can have an opportunity cost. The appropriate comparison includes the value of the vehicle given up, the replacement that would otherwise have been purchased, and the incentive actually earned.

**Price and emissions caps target assistance:** Italy’s purchase eligibility combined a price ceiling with an emissions threshold below 70 g CO₂/km, while France’s maximum bonus applied to vehicles at or below 20 g/km under its 2019 terms. Such conditions can limit support for expensive vehicles or higher-emission configurations. They also mean that two vehicles marketed as “electric” may receive different assistance because one is a BEV and another a PHEV with different certified emissions. [15][14]

**Charging deductions are not purchase grants:** Italy’s 2019 guidance allowed a 50% deduction on eligible charging expenditure up to a converted estimated $3,371, spread over ten equal annual installments. Thus, the maximum undiscounted deduction was approximately $1,685—50% of the eligible expenditure ceiling—not $3,371. Its present value is lower than its undiscounted sum when future tax benefits are discounted at a positive rate. [15]

**The purchase-support judgment:** upfront assistance can bridge a decisive cost and financing gap, but the effective reduction in LCC depends on eligibility, payment timing, scrappage requirements, and who actually receives the benefit.

### Recurring tax relief can be as important as a purchase bonus

**Ownership-tax exemptions create repeated savings:** recurring relief reduces expenditure each year the vehicle is retained. Its lifetime value therefore depends on the normal tax rate, ownership duration, and discounting. Denmark’s registration-tax treatment and the Netherlands’ zero-emission acquisition and ownership exemptions also show why a country can provide substantial support through foregone taxation without offering a simple cash purchase bonus. [14]

**Company-car rates are not cash subsidies:** Germany’s reduction from 1% to 0.5% of gross catalogue value per month reduced the taxable benefit associated with a qualifying company car. The cash saving depends on the employee’s tax rate and applicable circumstances. Likewise, the Netherlands’ 4% minimum company-car rate is a taxable-benefit parameter, not a 4% discount on the purchase price. [14]

**Deductibility requires another translation:** Belgium’s 120% corporate deductibility did not mean that the government paid 120% of the vehicle expense. If eligible expenditure is \(D\) and the applicable corporate tax rate is \(\tau\), deductibility above an ordinary 100% allowance produces an additional tax saving of \(0.20D\tau\). The relevant LCC benefit is that tax saving, rather than 20% of expenditure paid automatically to every buyer.

**VAT treatment differs between buyer types:** Portugal’s qualifying VAT deduction can be valuable to an eligible business purchaser, while a private household’s tax position may be different. Similarly, the Brussels van model excluded VAT when calculating business costs. Mixing a VAT-exclusive business comparison with a VAT-inclusive household comparison would attribute part of a tax-accounting difference to propulsion technology.

**Fiscal support can change vehicle choice:** a fixed purchase bonus has a different distributional effect from exemption from a tax that rises with vehicle price or emissions. The latter can generate a much larger absolute benefit for an expensive vehicle. Policy design therefore influences not just how many EVs are purchased but which segments, buyers, and business structures receive the largest gains.

**The recurring-tax judgment:** a complete EV cost comparison needs the purchaser’s actual fiscal position; headline grants alone can substantially understate or misrepresent policy support.

### Charging and access policies reduce barriers that monetary LCC can miss

**Charging location affects both price and convenience:** LeasePlan used a charging mix of 60% at home, 30% at work, and 10% public charging. Its effective energy price can therefore be represented as \(0.60q_h+0.30q_w+0.10q_p\), where the three \(q\) terms are home, workplace, and public charging prices. A buyer dependent on public charging faces a different cost and convenience proposition from the average represented by that mix. [2]

**Infrastructure policies address a coordination problem:** prospective buyers hesitate when charging is inconvenient, while charging providers face weak revenues when few EVs use their equipment. Public charging investment, workplace grants, building requirements, and interoperability standards can help overcome this mutually reinforcing barrier. The IEA’s 2019 synthesis explicitly linked charging availability and EV-ready buildings with consumer confidence and greater adoption. [7]

**Infrastructure cost depends on utilization:** the Congressional Research Service reported that an installed direct-current fast charger could cost up to $300,000, including permitting, design, and installation. That is an infrastructure cost per unit, not a cost to assign automatically to every EV. Its economic burden per charging session falls when the equipment is used more intensively, while low utilization can keep charging expensive despite a vehicle purchase subsidy. [8]

**Housing conditions change the appropriate intervention:** SHURA’s 2019 Turkey report described approximately eight million residential buildings and a limited number of single-family houses. In such a setting, assuming universal access to a private garage charger would be inappropriate. Shared residential, workplace, and public charging can matter more to adoption than a policy package designed around detached-house ownership. [31]

**Regional programs can target practical gaps:** Ticino’s 2019 decree coupled purchase assistance with home and employer charging grants and specified minimum technical requirements for eligible chargers. Its purchase program also required an importer premium matching the cantonal contribution. The design therefore combined public support, a supplier contribution, and infrastructure assistance rather than relying on a single unrestricted payment. [9][32]

**Access privileges increase usable value:** free or preferential parking, lower tolls, bus-lane access, and exemptions from access restrictions can reduce expenditure or improve convenience. Their monetary value varies with actual use, but their adoption effect can extend beyond standard LCC tables. A business that needs reliable access to an urban delivery area may value the ability to operate there more than a small difference in calculated cost per kilometer.

**The infrastructure-and-access judgment:** policies that make an EV easy to charge and useful for its intended trips can be essential even when a conventional ownership-cost calculation already shows parity.

### Standards, mandates, procurement, and industrial support affect supply

**Demand incentives require vehicles to buy:** a generous subsidy achieves little if suitable models are scarce or dealers do not offer them in the relevant market. The ICCT’s 2019 review reported more than thirty ZEV models in California, only a fraction of that availability elsewhere in the United States, and a California ZEV market share more than four times the national average. These observations are consistent with the importance of supply-side policy alongside consumer support. [18]

**Mandates change manufacturer incentives:** California’s credit requirements give manufacturers a reason to supply qualifying vehicles and improve the attributes that earn credits. Quebec’s mandate similarly acts on vehicle supply rather than simply lowering an individual buyer’s tax bill. Such rules can increase model choice and competition, potentially reducing cost indirectly while also expanding the set of buyers whose transport needs an EV can meet. [18][7]

**Public procurement can create early scale:** governments and public operators can provide predictable demand for vehicles and associated charging equipment. The IEA identified procurement as a way to stimulate demand and encourage manufacturers to increase EV availability. Its account of 2018–2019 EU developments included the Clean Vehicles Directive’s public-procurement role and building-related charging requirements, showing how regulation can coordinate vehicles and infrastructure. [7]

**Industrial support has a different timetable:** Turkey’s Law No. 7186 allowed investment contributions for qualifying projects to offset specified vehicle excise-tax liabilities through the statutory period ending in 2035. The August 2019 legal commentary reported that implementing procedures were still pending at that time. The enacted provision should therefore be understood as authority for industrial support, rather than an already demonstrated reduction in every Turkish consumer’s EV purchase cost. [10]

**Targets guide but do not guarantee outcomes:** Canada’s 2019 national vision sought ZEV shares of 10% of new light-duty sales by 2025, 30% by 2030, and 100% by 2040. These announced goals communicate direction; they are distinct from a binding manufacturer obligation, an operating purchase program, or a forecast of actual sales. Their influence depends on the measures, infrastructure, and vehicle supply supporting them. [11]

**The supply-side judgment:** mandates, standards, procurement, and manufacturing support can improve availability and production economics, but their contribution to owner LCC depends on successful implementation and the passage of cost reductions through to purchasers.

### Evidence of adoption effects: meaningful, but context-dependent

**U.S. evidence supports a financial response:** the Congressional Research Service summarized Jenn, Springel, and Gopal’s 2018 finding that each $1,000 in tax credits or rebates was associated with a 2.6% increase in EV sales. This supports the proposition that purchase assistance can stimulate uptake. It should not be mechanically multiplied across all subsidy levels or interpreted as an identical response in every country and vehicle segment. [8]

**The Dutch experience shows policy sensitivity:** annual PHEV registrations exceeded 40,000 in 2015. Gómez Vilchez and Thiel’s 2019 study described a subsequent market collapse following introduction of a special BPM tax in January 2017 after years of exemptions. The sequence is consistent with strong sensitivity to fiscal treatment, particularly for vehicles whose attractiveness depended on tax advantages. [33]

**Policy changes can shift purchase timing:** buyers can accelerate purchases before a benefit expires, creating a peak followed by a decline. Such timing effects mean that a sharp fall immediately after withdrawal need not equal an equally large permanent loss of underlying demand. A credible assessment distinguishes vehicles bought earlier because of a deadline from vehicles purchased only because the incentive existed.

**Denmark illustrates changing policy exposure:** the same 2019 study described sales declines after electric cars began paying part of the registration tax in 2016. The later 2018 Danish fact sheet and ACEA’s May 2019 inventory documented renewed relief for the 2019 tax position. This helps explain why the year attached to a cost comparison is substantive: an “EV in Denmark” did not face one fixed tax regime throughout the decade. [33][14]

**Subsidies are neither sufficient nor exclusive:** Gómez Vilchez and Thiel also identified the United Kingdom as a counterexample in which withdrawal of purchase support was not followed by declining electric-car sales or market shares. Their explanation emphasized non-monetary influences. Expanding model choice, better range, charging deployment, awareness, and changing preferences can sustain growth even when one incentive becomes less generous. [33]

**Swiss evidence reinforces that distinction:** Graubünden’s 2017 policy document cited the Mendrisio trial as a warning that purchase assistance could create demand that fell when support ended. Solothurn’s 2019 government response offered a different warning: roughly twenty years separated introduction of its 1991 EV tax exemption from the first local purchases, suggesting that availability, range, and charging were important complementary conditions. Neither case supports treating a tax exemption alone as sufficient for mass adoption. [12][34]

**The adoption-effect judgment:** financial incentives can accelerate uptake and their withdrawal can disrupt demand, but the effect depends on the vehicle market, charging network, consumer population, and other policies operating at the same time.

### Lower owner cost is not the same as cost-effective public policy

**Subsidies transfer costs between parties:** a purchase grant lowers the buyer’s private LCC, but the public budget pays for that reduction. A tax exemption likewise reduces government revenue relative to the ordinary tax treatment. These transfers can be justified by environmental benefits, learning, or network development, but they are not automatically reductions in the resources needed to manufacture and operate the vehicle.

**Additional adoption is the relevant denominator:** some subsidized purchasers would have bought an EV without assistance. CRS noted the concern that higher-income taxpayers could claim credits for purchases that would otherwise have occurred. In that case, expenditure per additional EV exceeds expenditure divided by all subsidized EVs. The same reasoning applies to emissions: fiscal cost should be related to additional real-world emissions avoided, not simply the number of supported registrations. [8]

**The Dutch audit supplies a concrete warning:** the Algemene Rekenkamer reported that support for efficient and semi-electric vehicles had cost about an estimated converted $5.62 billion since 2007. It cited official calculations equivalent to approximately $1,124 per avoided tonne of CO₂ and judged actual costs higher after accounting for the gap between test and real-world fuel consumption. Practical consumption was reported as up to 35% above NEDC test results. These are public-policy cost-effectiveness measures, not vehicle LCC totals. [35]

**Distribution can also be uneven:** the same audit cited five-year fiscal benefits for vehicles first registered in 2013 equivalent to more than approximately $44,900 for a Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV and about $95,500 for a Tesla S Performance. It noted that benefits mainly accrued to business drivers rather than private motorists. The examples show why a policy that produces very attractive private ownership costs can still raise questions about targeting and public expenditure. [35]

**Learning can justify temporary intervention:** early deployment can help expand production, improve manufacturing experience, and support investment in charging. Those benefits can eventually reduce real resource costs, rather than merely transferring them. However, an enduring subsidy that fails to generate scale, competition, or technological improvement risks supporting a permanently expensive product instead of helping a technology move toward independent competitiveness.

**Transport revenue requires adaptation:** the IEA warned in 2019 that electrification could erode vehicle- and fuel-tax revenues without changes to the fiscal framework. It discussed carbon-related fuel taxation and location-specific distance charges as possible components of a longer-term system. An LCC analysis should therefore distinguish the incentives legally applicable to the purchaser’s case from an assumption that every early-market tax privilege will persist indefinitely. [7]

**The policy-design judgment:** the strongest role for policy is to bridge temporary affordability and coordination barriers while improving real costs, environmental performance, and access—not merely to maximize subsidies or registrations.

**Evidence basis and units:** the report retains U.S. studies’ source-period dollars; euro conversions are estimates at the documented 1 October 2015 reference of €0.89 per US$1, not inflation-adjusted 2019 purchasing power. Conditional \(C\)-based entries preserve relative ownership costs. Annual, per-mile, and total costs retain their stated periods and boundaries; policy years distinguish effective dates from labeled 2019 terms. LeasePlan’s published scenario counts and country percentages are retained separately because its scope labels are internally uneven. Modeled ownership outcomes and policy targets are distinguished from observed results.

**Final assessment:** as of the end of 2019, EV LCC competitiveness was established for selected vehicles and uses, but not across the entire market. Policies helped turn engineering advantages into affordable and practical ownership choices; their strongest justification depended on achieving additional adoption, genuine cost reductions, and environmental benefits at a reasonable public cost.

**Policy channels and evidence boundaries**

**China**

China’s study, published 11 October 2019, identifies climate change, energy consumption, pollution, technology development, infrastructure, and EV-market development as policy objectives. It describes credit-management policies, fuel-consumption regulations, carbon quotas, subsidies, and preferential taxes; China’s observed 2018 EV sales were 520,000 units, up 78% from 2017 [256]. The study attributes an important role to public policy and warns that reducing incentives could reduce BEV sales, but it does not provide a counterfactual estimate of policy causality.

For a selected Chinese light-passenger BEV and ICEV, the consumer LCC framework included purchase, operation, and recycling costs over a 15-year life using a 2017 base year, 200,000 km, an 8% discount rate, and 20% depreciation. Manufacturing, operation, and recycling were included, while transport, sales, and maintenance were excluded; the assumptions also included no battery replacement, owner-installed residential charging, and no purchase loan [256]. The reported values were 1.87 yuan/km for the BEV and 1.73 yuan/km for the ICEV, a BEV/ICEV ratio of 1.08; the BEV’s initial cost was 1.69 times the ICEV’s despite lower operating cost and higher resale value. High MSRP, purchase tax, insurance, battery-development cost, and weakened subsidies or tax preferences were identified as explanations [256]. Because no exchange-rate date, FX rate, or inflation factor is supplied, the yuan amounts remain explicitly unsupported for USD normalization; they are not claimed as USD LCC values.

The study modeled a current BEV subsidy of 88,000 yuan and subsidy reductions of 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, producing LCC sensitivity coefficients of 1.80, 1.91, 2.02, 2.13, and 2.24, respectively. Purchase-tax preferences were also varied from 0% to 100%, with coefficients of 1.94, 1.91, 1.87, 1.84, and 1.80 [256]. Subsidies generally had the larger modeled LCC effect; withdrawal could expose the purchase-price disadvantage and cause market shock, while prolonged dependence could weaken technology upgrading. China’s reported electricity mix—70.92% coal, 18.61% hydropower, 4.76% wind, 3.87% nuclear, and 1.84% solar—and a modeled 50% coal/50% other-renewables scenario belong to environmental rather than monetary LCC analysis; the scenario reduced comprehensive BEV environmental impact by about 31% [256]. The study is limited to one light-passenger BEV/ICEV pair and relies partly on foreign inventory data and simplifying assumptions [256].

**United States**

The US federal EV tax credit was enacted in 2008, offered up to $7,500 per eligible EV buyer, and phased out for each manufacturer after 200,000 sales. Its documented channels were lower purchase cost and higher EV sales; phase-out made EVs more expensive to purchase and reduced their sales share [201]. Federal GHG standards treated EVs as zero-GHG vehicles, giving automakers compliance and credit-sale incentives. State ZEV programs required EV sales: 11 states represented about 30% of US new-car sales, while California’s 12–15% requirement concerned the 2025 model year, not an end-2019 outcome [201].

State and local instruments included purchase rebates, tax credits, infrastructure grants, HOV access, and fleet-acquisition incentives. The source describes mandates as supply measures, rebates as demand measures, and higher sales and charging volumes as potential cost-reduction channels. Public fast charging was considered important but expensive at low utilization; incompatible networks and insufficient infrastructure remained adoption barriers [201]. A later report records more than 1.2 million US charging ports, possible apartment electrical upgrades of up to $1,500, and public fast-charging station costs of $25,000–$100,000 or more; time-of-use pricing and low-cost residential charging could make some EVs competitive with gasoline vehicles [201].

Utilization is also an LCC channel. In the 2017 National Household Travel Survey, average annual mileage was 6,300 miles for all-electric vehicles, 7,800 for PHEVs, 10,200 for gasoline/diesel vehicles, and 11,800 for conventional hybrids; EVs averaged 30% fewer miles overall, or 7,000 versus 10,200 miles per year [42]. Possible explanations were the first-generation Nissan Leaf’s range of less than 80 miles, short-trip use of EVs in multi-vehicle households, gasoline-vehicle substitution for longer trips, and urban or environmentally oriented buyer selection [42]. California electricity rates were higher than average, but the lower-mileage pattern was similar inside and outside California. A California example showed environmental benefits of $2,800 at 15,000 miles per year versus $1,400 at 7,500 miles; this is an environmental illustration, not monetary LCC [42]. The survey’s response rate was 15.6%, and nonresponse bias could not be ruled out [42].

A report published on 8 December 2020, used only to locate historical context, records approximately 350,000 US EV sales in both 2018 and 2019 against roughly 15 million annual new-vehicle sales, or about a 2% share [201]. Its conclusion that EVs remained more expensive than gasoline or diesel vehicles and slower to refuel is not a matched 2019 LCC result. Separately, the 2019 critical-price study models initial cost, carbon tax, fuel, and maintenance: EV profitability requires electricity below a critical price; the modeled carbon tax raises that threshold by 8.5%, while charging timing reduces critical distance by approximately 40%. The study identifies daily distance as decisive but supplies no country policy, enactment, eligibility, or observed adoption effect [225].

**Policy channels supported by the evidence**

The evidence supports conditional policy effects rather than a universal reduction in EV LCC. Purchase incentives and preferential taxation can lower the retail price, capital depreciation, and cumulative financing interest, thereby shortening the time needed to reach TCO parity with an ICEV [318]. A policy brief similarly identifies purchase incentives, preferential taxation, non-fiscal measures, and charging infrastructure as channels that can reduce EV prices and improve adoption, although it supplies no country-specific LCC evaluation or matched case [317]. It reports that limited model choice, taxes, and absent subsidies were cited as reasons for high EV prices, while greater competition, declining battery costs, and fiscal incentives could reduce prices and potentially bring TCO closer to ICEV parity [317].

Energy-price policy operates mainly through operating-cost expectations and consumer choice. A later panel covering Denmark, Finland, Norway, and Sweden during 2019–2022 estimates that a 1% gasoline-price increase raised BEV sales by 0.85% on average; the effect was larger for lower-cost models and less-established brands, while electricity-price effects were significant mainly for lower-cost EVs [54]. The same source describes gasoline taxation as particularly salient to purchase decisions, whereas electricity tariffs alone may have weaker effects; information campaigns and transparent billing could make electricity costs more visible [54]. Its 1% gasoline-price simulation reduced lifecycle GHG emissions by 0.16%, but this is an emissions result—not a monetary LCC result—and the evidence is later than the 2019 cutoff [54]. More generally, consumers weigh upfront and expected operating costs; charging availability affects convenience and reliability, while environmental attitudes and peer effects also influence adoption [54].
