# Contact-Wire and Pantograph-Slide Materials for High-Speed Rail: A Technical Review Through Early 2022

**The central trade-off is strength versus conductivity.** Pure-copper contact wire provides the electrical baseline; silver chiefly improves thermal stability, while tin and magnesium provide greater mechanical strength for higher-tension catenaries. Published application guides associate these families with approximately 160, 250, 300–350, and 350–400 km/h, respectively. Copper–chromium–zirconium offers a different, precipitation-strengthened route to high strength and good conductivity, supported by material tests and contact-wire development experience. These speed associations describe products and complete overhead-line designs, not intrinsic material limits. [1]

**Slide development addresses the opposite surface.** The pantograph slide is the replaceable current-collecting material that rubs against the contact wire. Pure carbon protects the wire but has mechanical and electrical limitations; metal-rich sintered materials improve strength and current conduction; metal impregnation and fiber-reinforced composites seek a better balance between those properties. Their development overlaps rather than forming one worldwide replacement sequence. [2]

In the overview below, wire strengths are reference values at **120 mm² nominal cross-section**, except the separately identified Cu–Cr–Zr examples. MPa means megapascal, equivalent to N/mm²; %IACS expresses conductivity relative to the International Annealed Copper Standard, for which 100% IACS is approximately 58 MS/m at 20 °C. Speeds are in kilometres per hour, km/h. [3]

| Component and material | Decisive properties or evidence | Application context | Main judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contact wire: pure copper | ≥330 MPa; ≥97% IACS | Typically ≤160 km/h; named system ≤230 | Electrical baseline; limited tension margin |
| Contact wire: Cu–Ag | ≥360 MPa; ≥97% IACS | Typically ≤250 km/h | Strong thermal-stability option |
| Contact wire: Cu–Sn | ≥420 MPa; ≥80% IACS | Approximately 300–350 km/h | Balanced high-speed strength and cost |
| Contact wire: Cu–Mg | ≥430/490 MPa for 0.2/0.5 grades | Approximately 350–400 km/h | High-tension family; conductivity matters |
| Contact wire: Cu–Cr–Zr | 550 MPa/78% IACS; patent example 650/84 | High-speed development and field testing | Attractive strength–conductivity combination |

| Slide: powder metallurgy | Strong, conductive sintered metal matrix | Established railway service; optimized high-speed designs | Robust, but counterface wear needs control |
| Slide: metal-impregnated carbon | Conductive metal network in carbon | Locomotives and high-current collection | Improves carbon’s electrical margin |

Wire figures come principally from the Liljedahl product tables [1]; Cu–Cr–Zr examples come from Nagasawa and colleagues’ testing [4] and CN111799035B [5]. Slide judgments are developed below using the 2016 materials review [2] and 2020 Japanese technical review [6].

**The review answers two linked questions.** Part A examines the five wire families in the requested order, explains how their properties translate into catenary performance, and ends with the contact-wire comparison table. Part B develops the slide-material history, composition, manufacturing routes, and operating trade-offs, then ends with the slide comparison table. Throughout, the practical question is compatibility: a durable slide is useful only if it also preserves the wire, transfers current reliably, and works within the pantograph–catenary system.

## Part A: Catenary Contact Wire Materials

### Grades, operating demands, and comparison basis

**The contact wire performs several jobs.** It carries traction current, sustains longitudinal installation tension, and supplies the sliding surface for the pantograph. TB/T 2809-2017 defines it specifically as the overhead-system wire that contacts the pantograph strip and transmits current. The messenger wire and droppers support it, but they are not the wearing conductor reviewed here. [7]

**High speed makes strength consequential.** A moving pantograph excites waves in the contact wire. Greater installation tension increases wave-propagation speed, helping the overhead line maintain satisfactory interaction with a faster train; wire tensile strength therefore affects the tension that the design can accommodate. Lamifil explicitly identifies this connection between tensile strength, installation tension, and operating speed. [8]

**Electrical performance constrains that strengthening.** Alloy additions and cold deformation can increase strength while reducing conductivity. Lower conductivity increases longitudinal resistance and electrical heating at a given current and geometry, so the strongest wire is not automatically the best conductor for a particular line. Sumitomo’s 2020 development paper treats this balance as a manufacturing problem involving alloy composition, oxygen control, and drawing conditions—not simply the selection of an alloy name. [9]

**Standard designations establish the comparison.** EN 50149:2012 covers grooved copper and copper-alloy contact wires at nominal cross-sections of 80, 100, 107, 120, and 150 mm². Relevant designations include Cu-ETP, CuAg0.1, CuSn0.2 and CuSn0.4, and CuMg0.2 and CuMg0.5. China’s TB/T 2809-2017 added Cu–Cr–Zr varieties and revised provisions for several established copper-alloy families; it was issued in October 2017 and implemented in May 2018. [10][7]

**Test conditions explain some numerical differences.** EN 50149 references room-temperature tensile testing and electrical resistivity evaluated at 20 °C. TB/T 2809-2017 specifies, among other details, a 250 mm tensile gauge length and 20 mm/min test speed; conductivity measurements follow 24 hours of conditioning at 20 °C ±0.5 °C and use an 8 A measuring current. Its post-softening test heats specimens at 300 °C ±5 °C for two hours before cooling and retesting. These are distinct measurements of mechanical strength, conductivity, and retained strength after heating. [10][7]

### Pure copper: the high-conductivity baseline

**High conductivity defines pure copper’s role.** In Liljedahl’s EN 50149-referenced Cu-ETP contact-wire series, minimum tensile strength decreases from 355 MPa at 80 mm² to 310 MPa at 150 mm²; the 120 mm² reference value is 330 MPa. The listed conductivity minimum is 97% IACS. “Pure copper” in this context means a commercial high-conductivity copper grade, commonly electrolytic tough-pitch copper, rather than an annealed laboratory specimen. [1]

**The advantages are fundamentally electrical.** Copper combines very high electrical and thermal conductivity with good formability and corrosion resistance. The German Copper Institute gives conventional Cu-ETP as the 100% IACS reference and reports a maximum pure-copper conductivity of 60 MS/m, or 103.4% IACS, at 20 °C. Those reference values explain copper’s attractiveness, while the lower procurement minimum describes the finished contact-wire product. [3]

**Manufacturing supplies much of its strength.** Contact-wire production includes casting, rolling, and drawing into the grooved profile. Cold drawing strengthens the conductor through work hardening: plastic deformation changes its internal defect structure and raises resistance to further deformation. With no substantial strengthening addition, however, the achievable balance between strength, ductility, and conductivity remains less favorable for high-tension service than in appropriately processed alloys. [9]

**Heat exposure weakens that advantage.** Heating can promote recovery and recrystallization, reducing the strength introduced by cold working. ELCOWIRE consequently describes unalloyed copper as accepting lower continuous operating temperatures and lower tension forces, and exhibiting greater wear, than its alloyed alternatives. This is especially important because a contact wire must retain mechanical integrity while simultaneously carrying current and losing material through sliding wear. [11]

**Typical service is conventional-speed electrification.** Liljedahl assigns Cu-ETP contact wire to commuter systems at speeds up to 160 km/h, consistent with the Copper Information Center’s material comparison. A useful counterexample is Siemens’ Sicat SA overhead-line design: it specifies Cu-ETP contact wire of 100–120 mm² and a running speed up to 230 km/h. That example shows how suspension, tensioning, spans, and complete-system design can extend the use of a familiar conductor. [1][12]

**Documented applications span several markets.** Sumitomo lists its unalloyed GT copper wire for conventional Japanese railway lines. Nexans’ Canada-referenced catalogue includes a 161 mm² ETP contact wire specified at at least 325 MPa and 99% IACS, illustrating how geometry and purchasing requirements change the numerical specification within the same broad material family. [9][13]

**Pure copper suits electrically demanding baselines.** Its main advantages are low conductor resistance, effective heat conduction, and straightforward forming. Its principal disadvantages are lower tension capacity and less thermal and wear margin. It remains a rational choice where those mechanical demands are moderate; greater speed, sustained thermal loading, or higher installation tension shifts the balance toward alloyed wire.

### Copper–silver: preserving conductivity while improving thermal stability

**Silver chiefly improves thermal endurance.** Liljedahl lists CuAg0.1 at 360 MPa minimum tensile strength and at least 97% IACS for the 120 mm² reference section, with application guidance up to 250 km/h. At this section, its listed strength is about 9.1% above Cu-ETP—calculated as 360/330 − 1—while the two products retain the same specified conductivity minimum. [1]

**Small additions produce a useful effect.** Commercial low-alloy copper–silver materials commonly contain approximately 0.03–0.12% silver by mass and have a single-phase microstructure. The German Copper Institute reports a softening temperature around 300 °C for CuAg0.10 and emphasizes that silver only marginally changes copper’s high electrical and thermal conductivity. The important benefit is therefore not an enormous increase in room-temperature strength, but better preservation of cold-worked properties during heating. [3]

**Creep resistance strengthens the thermal case.** Creep is time-dependent deformation under sustained load; creep rupture is eventual failure under that loading. The monograph’s 200 °C comparison shows substantially longer rupture times for CuAg0.03 than for Cu-ETP at the listed stresses. The silver alloy in this particular comparison contains 0.029% Ag and is identified as soft annealed, so these results illustrate thermal-mechanical behavior rather than the acceptance strength of hard-drawn CuAg0.1 contact wire. [3]

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 124 | 40 | >1,000 | >25 |
| 138 | 10 | >1,000 | >100 |
| 154 | 2.5 | 80 | 32 |
| 165 | 0.5 | 15 | 30 |

**Thermal stability benefits high-current lines.** Lamifil’s 2019 brochure positions CuAg0.1 for frequent-service conventional railways up to 250 km/h and describes enhanced thermal stability as permitting higher DC overcurrent without increasing wire wear in the stated application. Siemens likewise uses parallel CuAg0.1 contact wires in its DC overhead-line designs, explicitly connecting that choice to thermal stability and current-carrying capacity. [14][12]

**The principal disadvantage is silver cost.** Sumitomo’s 2020 comparison characterizes EN Cu–Ag at 97% IACS and 360 MPa as relatively expensive, compared with Cu–Sn at 80% IACS and 420 MPa. Thus, silver is attractive when its conductivity and thermal behavior solve an operating problem, but less attractive when maximum strength per unit material cost is the overriding objective. [9]

**Germany provides a concrete operating example.** The historical background of EP0779372B1 describes Deutsche Bahn’s Re250 overhead line using 120 mm² CuAg0.1 wire, approximately 350 MPa tensile strength, approximately 95% IACS conductivity, and regular operation up to 250 km/h. Those historical values differ from the later product reference above because they describe a particular wire and design. The same account records a brief run above 400 km/h after increasing pretension, separating a special test condition from regular service. [15]

**Cu–Ag occupies a thermal-stability niche.** Its advantages are near-copper conductivity, improved softening resistance, and better high-temperature load retention. Its disadvantages are precious-metal cost and a smaller strength gain than the more strongly alloyed wire families. The resulting judgment is to favor Cu–Ag where electrical and thermal requirements dominate, rather than treating it as the default route to the highest catenary tension.

### Copper–tin: a practical strength, conductivity, and cost balance

**Tin provides a balanced high-speed option.** For Liljedahl’s high-conductivity CuSn0.2 product, the 120 mm² minimum is 420 MPa with at least 80% IACS. Its application designation is up to 300 km/h, while other pre-cutoff material and supplier comparisons associate CuSn products with approximately 350 km/h or operation above 300 km/h. These anchors support a typical 300–350 km/h application band. [1][16]

**Its strengthening is composition-and-process dependent.** Tin in solid solution strengthens copper and increases the work-hardening response during drawing. The German Copper Institute also reports improved softening behavior, with an approximate 330 °C softening temperature for the low-alloy Cu–Sn materials discussed. These effects explain why a small tin addition can improve mechanical and wear performance without turning the conductor into a conventional, much more heavily alloyed bronze. [3]

**The manufacturing route is industrially established.** Sumitomo describes continuous casting and hot rolling to make wire rod, followed by cold drawing into the grooved contact-wire profile. Increasing the drawing reduction raises tensile strength through work hardening, but also affects conductivity. Adjusting tin content alone therefore does not determine the final product: wire-rod dimensions and drawing history are also important. [9]

**Oxygen control offers a concrete example.** In developing contact wire for India’s Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation, Sumitomo sought at least 82% IACS and 420 MPa. Its 2020 paper explains that tough-pitch copper generally contains 200–500 ppm oxygen, and that the interaction among tin, oxygen, and impurities influences the strength–conductivity balance. By controlling composition and drawing conditions together, the company reports meeting both customer requirements on its production line. [9]

**The advantages include strength and cost.** Sumitomo characterizes Cu–Sn contact wires as high-strength, heat-resistant, and wear-resistant products for high-speed and conventional lines. In its comparative table, Cu–Sn has lower cost and higher strength than the Cu–Ag reference, at the expense of conductivity. At the common 120 mm² reference used here, the listed strength increase over Cu–Ag is approximately 16.7%, calculated as 420/360 − 1. [9][1]

**Conductivity is the main sacrifice.** A wire specified at 80% IACS has approximately 21.3% greater resistance than one at 97% IACS when length, section, and temperature are identical: 97/80 − 1 = 79 (est.). This is a calculated comparison of conductivity specifications, not a prediction of whole-route energy consumption. The practical consequence is that voltage drop, thermal loading, feeder arrangements, and conductor section must remain part of selection.

**Different Cu–Sn grades retain different specifications.** An Eland Cables datasheet for 107 mm² CuSn0.4, certified for Network Rail’s product system, gives at least 430 MPa and 80% IACS. Lamifil’s earlier 107 mm² comparison gives 430 MPa for CuSn0.2, CuSn0.4, and an SNCF specification, but conductivity values of 72%, 80%, and 80% IACS respectively. The designation “Cu–Sn” therefore should not erase the distinction between grade, high-conductivity variant, and railway purchasing specification. [17][8]

**Japan supplies the strongest regional example.** Sumitomo reports Cu–Sn as the predominant contact-wire material for Japanese high-speed railways. It developed the improved-strength GT-SN-W product in 1992 and supplied it to Taiwan High Speed Rail in 2004. Its Indian DFCCIL order totaled approximately 3,400 km of contact wire for an approximately 1,500 km Delhi–Mumbai section; that freight-project reference demonstrates production-scale application, rather than a passenger high-speed operating-speed qualification. [9]

**Cu–Sn is a strong balanced choice.** Its advantages are useful tensile strength, heat and wear resistance, and relatively economical production. Its disadvantage is the conductivity penalty compared with Cu-ETP and Cu–Ag. It is particularly compelling where a high-speed catenary needs greater tension capacity but the most demanding Cu–Mg strength level is not necessary.

### Copper–magnesium: high tension with an electrical penalty

**Magnesium emphasizes mechanical performance.** At 120 mm², Liljedahl lists minimum tensile strengths of 430 MPa for CuMg0.2 and 490 MPa for CuMg0.5, with conductivity minima of 80% and 76% IACS in that brochure. Increasing Mg content therefore strengthens the listed product while reducing its electrical performance. Application guides place the family broadly in the 350–400 km/h class. [1]

**Cold drawing develops the alloy’s strength.** The Copper Information Center describes approximately 0.1–0.7% magnesium additions as producing a cold-workable, solid-solution-strengthened alloy. Unlike Cu–Cr–Zr, these low-alloy Cu–Mg grades are not strengthened by a precipitation-aging heat treatment. Their final properties depend strongly on drawing, which also explains why the smallest listed cross-sections have the highest tensile-strength minima. [16]

**Thermal conduction also decreases with magnesium.** The Copper Information Center reports thermal conductivities of approximately 310 W/(m·K) for CuMg0.2 and 270 W/(m·K) for CuMg0.5. The German Copper Institute describes improved resistance to static and dynamic loading at elevated temperature, noteworthy abrasion resistance, and a softening temperature around 350 °C for the Cu–Mg materials discussed. The gain in strength and softening resistance therefore accompanies a reduced ability to conduct both current and heat. [16][3]

**Product variants change the electrical trade-off.** Lamifil’s 107 mm² comparison gives a CuMg0.5 baseline at 500 MPa and at least 62% IACS, a high-strength version at 570 MPa and the same conductivity minimum, and a high-conductivity version at 500 MPa and at least 72% IACS. These are distinct product specifications; the 62% baseline also differs materially from the 76% figure in Liljedahl’s listing. [8]

| CuMg0.5 variant, 107 mm² | Minimum tensile strength (MPa) | Minimum conductivity (%IACS) | Intended benefit |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Lamifil reference baseline | 500 | 62 | Established strength level |
| Lamifil HS | 570 | 62 | Higher installation tension |
| Lamifil HC | 500 | 72 | Lower electrical resistance |

**The numerical spread has practical significance.** The broader Copper Information Center tables give conductivity ranges of 62–82% IACS for CuMg0.2 and 41–64% IACS for CuMg0.5, while a separate Lamifil fine-wire product at a 1.8 mm reference diameter lists 63% minimum and 66% average conductivity. Together, these examples show why neither a generic alloy-family value nor a fine-wire property should replace the finished contact-wire purchasing specification. [16][18]

**Higher strength enables demanding catenaries.** ELCOWIRE reports supplying 500 km of 150 mm² Cu–Mg contact wire for the Madrid–Barcelona line, designed for 350 km/h, with automatic tensioning devices up to 31.5 kN; deliveries occurred during 2001–2003. The Copper Information Center also records Cu–Mg railway development in Europe from 1992 and an application on the Hanover–Berlin high-speed line in 1995. Germany and Spain are therefore concrete regional examples. [11][16]

**Manufacturing and fatigue temper the advantage.** Magnesium’s tendency to oxidize and the alloy’s casting characteristics create processing challenges, while stronger alloying reduces conductivity. More importantly, Xu, Song, and Liu’s January 2022 study combined Cu–Mg tensile and fatigue tests with pantograph–catenary and solid-wire models, finding a fatigue-life reduction exceeding 50% when the modeled speed class rose from 350 to 400 km/h. The vulnerable locations were associated with the wire section and steady-arm region. [3][19]

**Strength and fatigue are different properties.** Ultimate tensile strength measures resistance to a single increasing tensile load; fatigue concerns accumulated damage under repeated loading. A higher tensile-strength value can permit greater installation tension, but it does not by itself establish the fatigue life of a clamped, worn, dynamically loaded contact wire. The 2022 result is particularly useful because it demonstrates this distinction within an explicitly high-speed Cu–Mg system.

**Cu–Mg suits high-tension applications selectively.** Its principal advantages are strength, abrasion resistance, and suitability for high installation tension. Its disadvantages are reduced electrical and thermal conductivity, processing demands, and the need to preserve fatigue margin as speed increases. The correct choice is a specified CuMg grade and product variant matched to the catenary—not an assumption that every Cu–Mg wire delivers the same 400 km/h capability.

### Copper–chromium–zirconium: precipitation-strengthened contact wire

**Cu–Cr–Zr offers a different strengthening route.** Nagasawa, Aoki, and Katayama’s 1998 work reports 550 MPa tensile strength and 78% IACS for a 4 mm diameter test wire, together with good wear characteristics. A 110 mm² contact wire was then manufactured and field-tested on a commercial line, again showing good wear. These are useful material and field-test anchors for the family. [4]

**A full-size manufacturing example is stronger.** The first embodiment of Chinese patent CN111799035B, granted in November 2021, reports a 121 mm² contact wire with 650 MPa tensile strength, 5.8% elongation, and 84% conductivity at 20 °C in its IACS-based disclosure. The patent’s broader process objective is above 600 MPa and above 80% IACS. Its reported example remains distinct from the earlier small-diameter test result. [5]

**Precipitation combines strength and conductivity.** The patent specifies approximately 0.6–1.0% Cr and 0.06–0.2% Zr, with copper as the balance, for its CTCZ material. Solution treatment dissolves alloying elements in the copper matrix; subsequent aging produces second-phase precipitates that impede deformation. Removing some alloying solute from the matrix during precipitation also permits a useful conductivity recovery, giving this route a different property balance from relying mainly on dissolved Mg and cold work. [5]

**Thermal properties are another attraction.** The German Copper Institute describes CuCr1Zr as combining high room-temperature strength, high softening temperature, and improved creep-rupture strength. It reports approximately 78–86% IACS in the fully hardened state. Its temperature-dependent tensile data for a 9.5 mm rod illustrate that good thermal stability still means temperature-dependent properties, rather than unchanged strength. [3]

| Test temperature (°C) | Smooth CuCr1Zr rod tensile strength (MPa) | Elongation after fracture (%) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 20 | 550 | 22 |
| 200 | 465 | 21 |
| 300 | 415 | 19 |

**Controlled processing is the principal burden.** The 2021 patent describes casting, continuous extrusion, solution treatment, rolling, aging, and final peeling and drawing. Its background identifies composition uniformity, impurities, gas content, cast structure, and long-length production as manufacturing challenges. A 2021 RTRI report independently identifies difficulty producing chromium–zirconium precipitation-hardened copper contact wire, termed PHC, in small quantities, motivating development of a lower-cost cobalt–phosphorus alternative. [5][20]

**Residual stress adds another process variable.** A 2011 contact-wire study used solution treatment at 950 °C for one hour, 40% pre-deformation, aging at 480 °C for two hours, and a second 75% deformation. It reported a surface-to-subsurface residual-stress difference of about 80 MPa after treatment, compared with 780 MPa in the contrasted condition. This demonstrates why nominal composition and ultimate tensile strength alone do not fully characterize a precipitation-hardened contact wire. [21]

**Thermal advantages still require operating discipline.** The monograph notes diminished precipitation-hardening effectiveness above about 500 °C and a propensity for grain-boundary porosity and brittleness under particular elevated-temperature creep conditions. These are material and exposure-dependent findings, not evidence that ordinary railway operation at a single stated temperature causes failure. Brazing and welding likewise require attention to the alloy’s heat-treated condition. [3]

**A conditional speed proxy permits comparison.** A numerical comparison can be constructed from the 350 km/h Siemens Sicat HA design using 120 mm² CuMg0.5, whose reference tensile-strength minimum is 490 MPa. For an ideal tensioned wire, wave speed follows \(c=\sqrt{T/\mu}\), where \(T\) is tension and \(\mu\) is mass per unit length. Assuming equal density, equal use of tensile strength, and the same train-speed-to-wave-speed ratio gives the estimated mechanical proxy \(v_{\mathrm{proxy}}=350\sqrt{R_m/490}\): the 550 and 650 MPa Cu–Cr–Zr anchors yield approximately **371–403 km/h**, rounded to **370–400 km/h**. [12][1]

**That proxy measures mechanical potential only.** It compares published strengths under stated assumptions; the 550 MPa input comes from a small-diameter specimen, while the 650 MPa input is a patent embodiment. Actual applicable speed is decided by the finished grade’s allowable tension, fatigue, wear, thermal behavior, pantograph interaction, and line-specific running qualification. China supplies standardization and manufacturing-development examples, while Japan supplies PHC development and commercial-line testing experience. [7][20]

**Cu–Cr–Zr is attractive but process-intensive.** Its advantages are the combination of strength, conductivity, thermal stability, and encouraging wear evidence. Its disadvantages center on manufacturing control, heat-treatment sensitivity, joining considerations, and supply economics. Through early 2022, it merits treatment as an important high-speed contact-wire technology with evidence of development and testing, rather than simply another interchangeable EN-series conductor.

### How wire properties translate into service decisions

**Complete systems explain the speed differences.** Siemens’ catalogue supplies several useful counterexamples to a material-only speed hierarchy. CuAg0.1 appears in both 160 and 250 km/h DC configurations, while CuMg0.5 appears in both a 230 km/h system and a 350 km/h high-speed system. Their different tensions, sections, and suspension designs are part of the explanation. [12]

| Overhead-line system | Contact wire | Contact-wire tension (kN) | Running speed (km/h) | Design context |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Sicat SA | Cu-ETP, 100–120 mm² | 12 | Up to 230 | Standard AC catenary |
| Sicat SD | 2 × CuAg0.1, 120 mm² | 2 × 12 | Up to 160 | High-current DC |
| Sicat HD | 2 × CuAg0.1, 120 mm² | 2 × 15 | Up to 250 | Higher-speed DC |
| Sicat SX | CuMg0.5, 80 mm² | 15 | Up to 230 | Long-span AC design |
| Sicat HA | CuMg0.5, 120 mm² | 27 | Up to 350 | High-speed AC |

**Strength should not be confused with stiffness.** Liljedahl’s tables give approximately the same elastic modulus—120 kN/mm², or 120 GPa—for the compared Cu-ETP, CuAg, CuSn, and CuMg products. Alloying changes the allowable stress and resistance to permanent deformation far more than it changes this listed elastic stiffness. Higher wave speed primarily follows the installation tension enabled by strength, rather than a large increase in Young’s modulus. [1]

**Cross-section changes both electrical and mechanical behavior.** For fixed length, resistance decreases as cross-sectional area increases. At fixed tension, tensile stress increases as the remaining area decreases, so sliding wear simultaneously worsens the electrical path and reduces mechanical margin. Conversely, simply increasing section adds mass per unit length; in the ideal wave-speed relation, higher mass offsets some benefit unless tension also increases. This is why strength, section, wear allowance, and tension must be considered together.

**The common-section comparison gives clear priorities.** At 120 mm², the selected product series places CuMg0.5 above CuMg0.2, CuSn0.2, CuAg0.1, and Cu-ETP in listed tensile strength. However, Cu–Ag and Cu-ETP retain the highest specified conductivity, while Cu–Sn and CuMg0.2 occupy similar strength–conductivity territory in that series. The Cu–Cr–Zr examples offer a promising combination, but belong to their own specimen and process bases. [1][4]

**Thermal selection can reverse a simple ranking.** A lower-resistance conductor reduces bulk electrical loss, but resistance to softening and creep governs how much strength survives the resulting temperature history. Cu–Ag is therefore valuable even where its room-temperature strength improvement is modest. Cu–Mg can be preferable when tension governs, while Cu–Sn can offer a more economical balance and Cu–Cr–Zr can justify added processing where its property combination is valuable.

**Additional documented evidence**

Using the declared 31 March 2022 cutoff, the evidence supports a clear material trade-off: pure copper maximizes conductivity but has limited strength and thermal-softening resistance, whereas Cu–Ag, Cu–Sn, Cu–Mg and Cu–Cr–Zr progressively provide greater mechanical or thermal capability at the cost of alloying, processing complexity, or some conductivity. The speed figures below are documented service, design, qualification or test contexts for complete catenary systems—not intrinsic material-only speed limits. The five materials are discussed in the user-prescribed order.

**1. Pure copper contact wire (Cu; CT/T)**

**Pure copper contact wire is the unalloyed-copper member of the contact-wire family, designated CT or T in the railway nomenclature.** TB/T 2809-2017 covers copper and copper-alloy contact wires for electrified railways, although its broad title alone does not establish a separate pure-copper subclass.[157] The detailed nomenclature identifies copper as material **T**, with CT120 and CT150 as representative product models.[164][165] The copper used for railway contact wire must be no less pure than standard cathode copper **Cu-CATH-2** under GB/T 467-2010.[164][165] The supplied rendering of the chemical-composition table records approximately **99.90% copper by mass**, but its OCR alignment makes the trace-element limits unreliable; Cu-CATH-2 is therefore the safer grade basis to retain.[164]

At the pantograph–catenary interface, pure copper contact wire is the overhead conductor that carries traction current while its lower surface slides against the pantograph contact strip. The railway standard describes the contact wire as transferring electrical energy through contact with the pantograph slide.[165] Its main material advantage is electrical and thermal conductivity, together with corrosion resistance. A pre-cutoff technical review reports conductivity of at least **97%**, tensile strength of approximately **350–360 MPa**, and a nominal working temperature of **90 °C**.[159] The 2017 standard extract gives the CT category a conventional tensile-strength minimum of **360 MPa** and a conductivity minimum of **97%**; those values are associated with the standard’s conventional test table, nominally using a **150 mm²** cross-section, while the source states that the tensile-strength value for a 120 mm² wire is 2% higher.[164] The supplied passage does not explicitly identify the conductivity percentage as an IACS value, so it should not be silently treated as directly interchangeable with independently reported %IACS data.

Pure copper’s principal limitation is its weak strengthening response and poor resistance to thermal softening. The source reports that heating causes recrystallization and softening; after a **300 °C, 2 h** softening treatment, tensile strength falls by about **40%**, leaving approximately **60%** of the untreated value.[159] The same source associates the resulting loss of strength and surface hardness with increased wear and operational risk. The standard extract gives a common linear-expansion coefficient of approximately **1.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹** and Young’s modulus of **120 GPa** for the various listed contact-wire models, but those values are not stated as pure-copper-specific.[164] The standard also requires contact-wire wear testing against specified pure-carbon, metal-impregnated-carbon or composite-carbon slides; the supplied OCR does not preserve a reliable unit for its numerical wear limit, so no precise wear-rate value is assigned here.[164]

The source-supported speed conclusion is conservative: pure copper contact wire is reported as suitable for lines at **120 km/h or below**, and the review says it was “basically no longer” adopted because of low strength and poor high-temperature softening resistance.[159] The supplied standard extract does not state a general pure-copper service-speed range or application-country list, so those fields remain **not reported** rather than being filled from generic copper-wire data.[164] At the couple level, an older source reports that pure-carbon slides give particularly good wear reduction when running against copper contact wire; this is evidence about the slide–wire pair, not a standalone strength or wear advantage of pure copper.[162] A 2008 PHC catenary study likewise documented simulation, running tests and commercial-line installation of a copper-alloy contact-wire system, but did not establish a pure-copper-specific performance conclusion.[85]


**2. Copper–silver alloy contact wire (Cu–Ag; CTA)**

**Copper–silver contact wire improves the thermal-softening and current-carrying capability of copper while retaining high conductivity, but its room-temperature strengthening is limited and silver increases cost and resource consumption.** The railway nomenclature identifies this material as Cu–Ag or CTA.[159][164] The basic structure is a copper matrix containing silver in solid solution. One reported example uses **0.05% Ag**; the source states that this raises copper’s recrystallization temperature from approximately **140 °C to 340 °C**.[159] A separate 2012 source reports domestic copper-silver contact-wire formulations containing approximately **0.08–0.12% silver**, equivalent to about **1.5 kg of silver per tonne** of alloy wire.[158] These percentages describe different reported formulations and should not be treated as one universal Cu–Ag grade.

Silver and copper have similar electronic structures, so the reported small silver addition has little effect on conductivity while improving high-temperature stability. The 5 January 2022 source reports conductivity of at least **97% IACS**, a working temperature of approximately **150 °C**, a **13–26%** increase in current-carrying capacity, and only a **4–6%** strength reduction after softening.[159] It gives room-temperature tensile strength of approximately **370 MPa**, indicating that the solid-solution strengthening benefit is useful but modest. The resulting performance profile is therefore high conductivity, improved thermal-softening resistance, improved wear and high-temperature capability, and greater current capacity than pure copper.[159]

The principal advantages of Cu–Ag are its combination of conductivity and thermal capability. A documented German **Re-250** catenary used copper-silver contact wire at approximately **250 km/h**, and the same review describes domestically produced Cu–Ag wire as suitable for high-speed railways below **300 km/h**.[159] The 2012 survey reports extensive use in China on conventional lines at approximately **120–160 km/h**, on domestic electrified railways below **200 km/h**, and in urban rail transit, where low voltage, high current, high train density and frequent pantograph passages increase the value of conductivity and current capacity.[158] Thus, the documented application countries in the supplied evidence are principally **China and Germany**, with the precise speed context depending on grade and catenary design.

The main disadvantages are economic and metallurgical. Silver is a precious metal, and the reported **0.08–0.12%** silver content and **1.5 kg/t** consumption create a resource burden as wire demand increases.[158] The same source reports that low-tin Cu–Sn wire was developed to approach Cu–Ag performance while reducing silver consumption and manufacturing cost; for one comparison, the low-tin alternative had processing cost of approximately **80%** of the Cu–Ag wire.[158] Cu–Ag also does not provide the same room-temperature strength increase obtainable from stronger Cu–Mg or precipitation-hardened Cu–Cr–Zr systems. Consequently, its deciding condition is whether the system prioritizes high conductivity and thermal capacity at moderate-to-high speed enough to justify silver cost, rather than maximum tensile strength.


**3. Copper–tin alloy contact wire (Cu–Sn; CTS, CTSL, CTSN, CTSH, representative CuSn0.4)**

**Copper–tin contact wire provides a practical strength–conductivity balance with simple, economical production, and different Cu–Sn grades cover conventional through approximately 350 km/h railway applications.** The material is designated Cu–Sn or CTS; the supplied sources also identify low-tin CTSL, high-strength CTSN, high-strength or high-tin CTSH, and the EN 50149 **CuSn0.4** designation.[158][159] The source does not establish one universal tin content or temper for the whole Cu–Sn family.

Tin provides solid-solution strengthening. In the reported range of **0.050–0.300% Sn**, increasing tin raises tensile strength while reducing conductivity: the source gives **432 MPa and 91.3% IACS** at 0.050% Sn, versus **454 MPa and 78.8% IACS** at 0.300% Sn.[159] Tin can be added directly to molten copper with little reported loss, supporting uniform composition, stable production, high yield and comparatively low cost.[159] A Cu–Sn–rare-earth variant reported in the same pre-cutoff review reached **446 MPa** and **84.5% IACS**; this is best classified as a modified Cu–Sn subtype rather than as a separate top-level contact-wire family.[159]

The main performance advantages are high strength relative to pure copper and Cu–Ag, elevated softening resistance, adequate conductivity, good wear behaviour, and practical corrosion or environmental resistance. The source describes Cu–Sn wire as having a broad performance range, good adaptability, simple manufacture, stable quality and low manufacturing cost.[158][159] The decisive disadvantage is the conductivity penalty associated with increasing tin: high-strength grades may meet a speed or tensile requirement while providing lower conductivity than Cu–Ag or low-tin grades. The supplied pre-cutoff evidence therefore supports a grade-specific trade-off rather than a universal ranking.

Application evidence spans several countries and system contexts. The 2012 survey identifies Cu–Sn contact-wire use in **Japan, China, France and Taiwan-related infrastructure**, while its broader country comparison also discusses Germany and other European practice; the material assignment varies by line and grade.[158] Japan had researched and used Cu–Sn wire since the 1980s, and the source reports that Japanese Shinkansen Cu–Sn wire accounted for approximately **80% of the relevant contact-wire mileage** and achieved a **300 km/h** target in 1997.[159] In China, CuSn0.4 or related high-strength grades were used on passenger lines designed for **200–250 km/h**.[158] A high-strength CuSn0.4 example with a 150 mm² cross-section is reported at **430 MPa and 80% IACS**.[158] For a 350 km/h design context, the source reports a Cu–Sn grade reaching approximately **510 MPa and 63% IACS**, with a single-pantograph trial at **386 km/h** and a double-pantograph trial at **350 km/h** on the Guangzhou–Shenzhen section; fewer hard bends, less arcing and stable running were reported in that trial.[158] A French test section using Cu–Sn contact wire is reported at **500 km/h**, with a 120 mm² wire and reported tensile strength of **534 MPa**.[158]

These figures are not interchangeable: **430 MPa/80% IACS**, **510 MPa/63% IACS**, and **534 MPa** refer to different grades, cross-sections, processing conditions and service or test contexts. The applicable-speed conclusion is therefore conditional: conventional or lower-strength Cu–Sn grades are documented around **200–250 km/h**, high-strength grades are documented in **350 km/h design and trial contexts**, and the 500 km/h French figure is a test result rather than routine commercial operation.


**4. Copper–magnesium alloy contact wire (Cu–Mg; CTM, CTMM, CTMH)**

**Copper–magnesium contact wire offers a strong high-speed combination of tensile strength, thermal stability and wear resistance, but magnesium makes melting, composition control and billet production substantially more difficult.** The material is designated Cu–Mg or CTM; the revised nomenclature includes CTM, CTMM and CTMH grades.[165] For the representative CTMH150 class, the 2017 railway-standard interpretation reports minimum tensile strength of **530 MPa** and minimum conductivity of **65% IACS at 20 °C**.[165] The source identifies Cu–Mg as a solid-solution-strengthened alloy: adding magnesium raises tensile strength, hardness, heat resistance and wear resistance, but lowers conductivity.[159]

The strength–conductivity trade-off is demonstrated by reported processed compositions. One preparation increased Mg from **0.025% to 0.58%**, while tensile strength rose from **420 to 580 MPa** and conductivity fell from **98.0% to 63.5% IACS**.[159] Other processed examples gave **566 MPa/85.3% IACS** for Cu–0.2Mg and **589 MPa/79.4% IACS** for Cu–0.4Mg; a Conform–ECAP and cold-working route produced reported combinations of **575 MPa/81.5% IACS** and **627 MPa/72.4% IACS**.[159] These are laboratory or process-specific results, not one universal CTMH specification, but they show why Cu–Mg is attractive where strength must be increased without abandoning useful conductivity.

The manufacturing disadvantage follows directly from magnesium’s chemical activity. Magnesium oxidizes and burns readily, so it cannot simply be added to molten copper; the source describes use of a copper–magnesium master alloy, upward or horizontal continuous casting, subsequent drawing and, in some routes, continuous extrusion for grain refinement.[158] Ordinary continuous casting and rolling is unsuitable for the reported Cu–Mg billet because of poor melt fluidity and poor hot-workability.[158] In the cited comparison, Cu–Sn billet production reached approximately **10 t/h**, whereas the upward-cast Cu–Mg route produced approximately **0.8 t/h**; Cu–Mg billets also had reported grain sizes of at least **1.0 mm**, compared with no more than **0.025 mm** for the hot-worked Cu–Sn route.[158] Additional grain-refinement processing improves the structure but increases process complexity and cost. The same source reports Cu–Sn processing cost at approximately **60–70%** of Cu–Mg processing cost, excluding raw-material cost.[158]

The principal application advantage is high-speed capability. The source reports that Cu–Mg contact wire was developed under German railway leadership; Cu–Mg-120 was tested on the Frankfurt RE330 catenary at approximately **330 km/h**, and Cu–Mg wire was subsequently adopted in **Spain, France and Belgium**.[159] In China, Cu–Mg wire passed a **300 km/h** trial on the Qinhuangdao–Shenyang passenger line in 2002, after which Cu–Mg was reported as predominant on Chinese lines at **300 km/h and above**.[159] The 2012 survey also reports Cu–Mg use above **300 km/h** in Germany and on several Chinese **350 km/h** passenger lines, including Beijing–Tianjin, Wuhan–Guangzhou and Zhengzhou–Xi’an; France is reported to use both Cu–Mg and Cu–Sn above 300 km/h.[158]

Its disadvantages are therefore not simply lower conductivity. They include oxidation and magnesium burn-off, composition non-uniformity, difficult melting, lower production capacity, coarse-grain risk, additional extrusion or refinement steps, higher manufacturing cost and possible process defects. The material also does not automatically guarantee a higher speed: the source explicitly notes that different 350 km/h lines selected different contact-wire alloys.[158] In the pantograph–catenary interface, Cu–Mg is relevant because its higher strength and thermal stability help maintain contact-wire geometry and mechanical integrity under high-speed current collection, while its conductivity and production route remain design constraints.

**5. Copper–chromium–zirconium alloy contact wire (Cu–Cr–Zr; TCZ/CTCZ, representative PHC-110)**

**Evidence and comparability qualification**

The required five-material roster is supported by the pre-cutoff railway-standard and technical-review evidence; the broad TB/T 2809 title should not be mistaken for proof that every possible copper alloy belongs to one identical grade family.[157][164] Values above are deliberately retained with their grade, cross-section, temperature and service or test context. They should not be averaged into a single material value: for example, Cu–Sn values of **430 MPa/80% IACS**, **510 MPa/63% IACS** and **534 MPa** describe different products and conditions, while pure-copper values of **350–360 MPa** and the CT standard minimum of **360 MPa** come from different evidence presentations.[158][159][164]

Later records were not used to extend the early-2022 technology state. The 2023 BS IEC 63190 record describes catenary-wire characteristics and test procedures but is post-cutoff.[80] A March 2026 general wire page reports **100% IACS** for ETP C110 and **35,000–60,000 psi** for OF copper depending on temper, but it is not railway-contact-wire evidence available by the cutoff.[111] A 29 November 2022 pure-copper additive-manufacturing paper reports general-conductor conductivity above **100% IACS** and relative density above **99.9%**, but it concerns additively manufactured specimens rather than catenary wire and is post-cutoff.[161] A 2026 ETP AC 80 product sheet reports **99% IACS** and **355 MPa**, but its post-cutoff generation date makes it lead-only.[126] The later 2023 contact-wire review record is likewise treated only as a navigation lead, not as evidence for the requested as-of state.

**Pure copper (Cu-ETP).** Railway nomenclature identifies electrolytic Cu-ETP, hardened during drawing; the available passages do not give its full chemistry or temper [107][99]. Contact wire and slide form the conducting traction interface, so conductivity and wear resistance are both essential [107][58]. Benefits are high electrical/thermal conductivity, good tensile strength and atmospheric-corrosion resistance; Polish practice reports traction currents above 1,200 A without overheating the wire [107]. Limitations are poor mechanical and wear resistance: same-copper pairing increases abrasion (clean-contact μs = 1.5; approximately 0.5 with oxide or contamination), while roughness, debris and contact separation increase resistance, arcing and wear; one failure study recorded local interface temperatures up to 550 °C [107][58]. A 2013 laboratory model separates wear into friction, Joule-effect and arc-dissipation contributions [99]. Its 36.6-km, 1.5-kV example operated at 80 km/h, 1,120 A and 70 N—an operating condition, not an intrinsic material limit [58].

Pure copper is reported at 103.1 %IACS in a 2015 material comparison [166]; the NIST reference is 100 %IACS for annealed copper at 20 °C, with approximately 97.3 %IACS for hard-drawn copper and observed values of 99.62 % for hard-drawn and 101.88 % for annealed wire [95]. These are generic/reference values, not one catenary grade; no contact-wire tensile value is established here. Poland is documented as an application country, but no worldwide inventory is supported. IEEE 1896 identifies wires by metallurgical content and conductivity, while EN 50318 concerns dynamic-interaction validation rather than material properties [106][60]. A 2023 conveyor-belt tensile method and 2024 vacuum-arc tests on additively manufactured electrodes are post-cutoff, non-rail evidence and are not used for the early-2022 review [94][97].

**Copper–silver (CuAg).** PKP was introducing CuAg0.10 because it offered better wear resistance and a higher recrystallization temperature than copper [107]. No pre-31-March-2022 strength or conductivity value is established in these cards. An ADIF draft dated November 2022 lists CuAg0.1 in 160–220 km/h design catenaries with 100 °C permanent and 150 °C short-duration conductor limits; these are post-cutoff, draft-only design associations [136].

**Copper–tin (CuSn).** A 2015 CTS120 study used up-casting, continuous extrusion, rolling and drawing. At 0.050% Sn, it measured 91.3 %IACS and 432 MPa; at 0.300% Sn, 78.8 %IACS and 454 MPa [166]. Increasing Sn increases lattice scattering and strength, while extrusion breaks cast grains and produces a fine recrystallized structure; the values remain composition- and process-specific [166]. A later draft names CuSn0.2, but it is post-cutoff [136].

**Copper–magnesium (CuMg).** The analogous CTMH150 route produced 150-mm² wire; 0.025% Mg gave 98.0 %IACS and 420 MPa, whereas 0.580% Mg gave 63.5 %IACS and 580 MPa [166]. At equal 0.3% addition, Cu-Mg measured 76.5 %IACS versus 79.8 %IACS for Cu-Sn [166]. A generic C18661/CMG1 wire sheet—not railway contact wire—reports 35–46 ksi annealed, 55–70 ksi H02, 75–90 ksi H04, 90 ksi H08 and 74 %IACS annealed at 68 °F; its temper values are manufacturer recommendations without ASTM ranges [115]. A November-2022 draft associates CuMg0.5 with 200–350 km/h designs, but this is post-cutoff and design-only [136].

**System-level qualification**

The catenary contact wire and pantograph slide form the sliding electrical-contact path through which a high-speed train receives traction power; wear affects both components, and current, uplift force and train speed are important operating variables [37]. Material choice must therefore be separated from system design: wire tension, mass per unit length, span, elasticity and support stiffness influence speed and contact-force variation, while poor compatibility causes arcing, contact loss and accelerated wear [75]. Too little contact force can interrupt current collection and cause arcing; too much accelerates wear of both wire and strip [39]. A 2020 simulation found that more than 70% of cases with random contact-wire irregularities were worse than the ideal irregularity-free case, but this was a system simulation rather than a material comparison [91]. Similarly, 300 and 350 km/h measurements of contact-wire uplift describe railway operation, not a material qualification [92].

**Pure copper contact wire**

The clearest grade basis is Cu-ETP (CW004A) under EN 50149:2012; the Chinese GB/T 12971.1-2008 basis requires copper wire to use cathode copper no lower than Cu-CTH-2 [163][169]. For Cu-ETP, the supplier specification gives a minimum conductivity of **≥97% IACS at 20°C** and minimum tensile strengths of **355, 355, 350, 330 and 310 MPa** for 80, 100, 107, 120 and 150 mm² cross-sections, respectively; it identifies conventional commuter service up to **160 km/h** [169]. These are product-specification values, not an intrinsic speed ceiling.

GB/T 12971.1-2008 evaluates tensile strength, elongation, high-temperature residual strength, resistivity, torsion, repeated bending, vibration, axial fatigue, grain size and oxygen content; cited methods include GB/T 4909.3-1985 and GB/T 3048.2-2007 [163]. For CT/CTY types it gives a maximum resistivity of **0.01768 Ω·mm²/m at 20°C**, while the supplied extract does not convert that value directly to %IACS. It also gives a CT allowable maximum working temperature of **95°C**; its current-carrying calculation assumes 40°C ambient temperature, 0.5 m/s wind and the source-transcribed solar-irradiance value of 1000 W/m³ [163]. The 2015 up-casting–continuous-extrusion–drawing result—dynamic recrystallization producing fine grains and improving strength, conductivity and fatigue—was reported for copper-alloy wire and should not be transferred to pure copper [122].

**Copper–silver alloy contact wire**

The representative pre-cutoff grade is **CuAg 0.1**, specified under EN 50149:2012. Its minimum conductivity is **≥97% IACS at 20°C**; minimum tensile strengths are **375, 375, 360, 360 and 360 MPa** at 80, 100, 107, 120 and 150 mm², respectively. The product literature identifies long-distance passenger systems and speeds up to **250 km/h** [169]. These figures are manufacturer-stated application values and remain conditional on the complete catenary and pantograph system.

**Copper–tin alloy contact wire**

The representative grade is **CuSn 0.2** under EN 50149:2012. The specification gives **≥80% IACS at 20°C** and minimum tensile strengths of **460, 450, 430, 420 and 420 MPa** for 80, 100, 107, 120 and 150 mm² cross-sections [169]. It is listed for high-speed routes up to **300 km/h** [169]. A later catalogue attributes the high-speed suitability of CuSn/CuMg alloys to the high strength of tin or magnesium, which permits greater suspension stress and higher catenary wave-propagation speed; that later statement is retained only as a qualified lead [168]. Country-specific in-service evidence is not established by the supplied pre-cutoff spans.

**Copper–magnesium alloy contact wire**

Two grades must be kept distinct. **CuMg 0.2** is specified at **≥80% IACS** with minimum tensile strengths of **460, 450, 440, 430 and 420 MPa**; **CuMg 0.5** is specified at **≥76% IACS** with **520, 510, 500, 490 and 470 MPa**, for the same five cross-sections, all at 20°C [169]. The product literature lists CuMg 0.2 and CuMg 0.5 for high-speed routes up to **400 km/h** [169]. This is a product/application claim, not a material-only maximum, and no country-level deployment denominator is supplied.

The electrical-sliding evidence shows why conditions matter: at 250 N and 300 A, CuMg wear initially increased from 50 to **150 km/h**, reached a maximum, and then decreased at **250 km/h**; the cited study also reports increasing wear with normal force over 75–250 N at 150 km/h [156]. These observed laboratory relationships cannot be converted into a service-speed ranking.

**Copper–chromium–zirconium alloy contact wire**

The pre-cutoff study tested age-hardened **CuCrZr C18150**, nominally **1.4% Cr, 0.12% Zr, balance Cu**. It reported **74% IACS** conductivity and **168 HV** hardness [156]. Age-hardening and Cr/Zr precipitates increased hardness and tribological performance; in the reported six-hour electrical-sliding comparison, CuCrZr had **6.7 times** the wear resistance of pure Cu and was presented as a compromise between wear resistance, conductivity and safety [156]. Its wear resistance nevertheless remained below CuBeNi and CuBe in that test, and current reduced wear resistance through Joule heating and arc erosion [156].

Finally, 100% IACS corresponds to **58.108 MS/m at 20°C** under the stated IACS convention; grade, temperature, processing and measurement method must remain attached to every conductivity comparison [41]. Later 2025–2026 pages and catalogues report system speeds, catenary design conditions or generalized copper properties, but they are post-cutoff and are not used to establish the requested early-2022 material state [40][77][78][108][168].

Post-2022 comparison [170]: Cu-ETP has high electrical/thermal conductivity and corrosion resistance, but little cold-work strengthening and heat softening (≤160 km/h). Cu–Ag solid solution improves strength, wear and heat resistance with little conductivity loss but is costly (<250 km/h). Cu–Sn is stronger, heat-resistant, suitably conductive and cheaper than Cu–Ag (~300 km/h). Cu–Mg improves strength and wear/heat resistance and supports >350 km/h, but lowers conductivity and costs more than Cu–Sn. A 2013 patent reports CTMH-120 Cu–Mg at 609–649 MPa; its resistivity lacks temperature-normalized %IACS basis [79]. Speed is system-dependent [42].

**Pure copper: conductivity–durability trade-off**

Pure copper (unalloyed Cu) contact wire is the overhead current-carrying member; a collector strip rubs against it to transfer traction power, and service life depends on both wear surfaces [46]. Its documented baseline advantage is high conductivity, but cable-specific evidence must not be treated as a catenary specification. Cold drawing increases strength while lowering conductivity: hard-drawn copper is reported at 97.2 %IACS relative to annealed copper (1.724 × 10−7 Ω·m at 20 °C); cited medium- and hard-drawn strengths are 42–60 and 49–67 ksi, not catenary MPa values [44]. Grade, temper, test method and temperature must accompany comparisons.

In 2014 electrical-sliding tests of a carbon-strip/copper-contact-wire pair, friction coefficient was 0.24–0.37; current reduced friction through oxide-layer lubrication but increased arc and thermal wear. At 240 A, pure-carbon-strip wear reached 0.0135 g/km, 14 times the no-current value; at 200 A, the contact couple reached 300 °C [46]. The measured wear is the strip’s, not an isolated copper-wire rate. Other high-speed tests identify normal load, current and speed as controls on abrasive, adhesive and arc-ablation wear [81]; contact-wire stagger and arc-discharge energy were considered factors in localized strip wear [82]. Thus high conductivity and conditionally low interface friction are benefits, while energized heat, arcing, geometry-dependent wear and maintenance are limitations.

The 250–400 km/h figure in a high-speed-system study is context, not a pure-copper rating [172]. Catenary suitability requires mechanical/electrical strength and dynamic pantograph compatibility; critical speed is a coupled system property [43][83]. Open-environment temperature, light and pollution add fault modes, while monitoring can help judge pantograph/catenary condition [114]. No pre-31-March-2022 source represented here supplies universal pure-copper speed, tensile strength in MPa, catenary-wire conductivity or country data. The available Cu-ETP thermal study is dated 29 April 2026 and is excluded from the requested as-of state [137].

Cu–Cr–Zr values and alloy-specific country evidence remain unestablished here; the alloys should not be ranked without like-for-like grade, temper, method and complete-system qualification.

## Part B: Pantograph Slide Materials

### Development history and material classification

**The historical development has overlapping branches.** Early metallic and sintered materials addressed current conduction, strength, and sliding durability. Carbon introduced a different priority: protecting the overhead wire through a less aggressive sliding surface. Metal impregnation then improved carbon’s electrical and mechanical properties, while fiber-reinforced and structured composites offered additional ways to control strength, conductivity, and wear. Shang and colleagues’ 2016 review describes this progression from original pure-metal and powder-metallurgy strips toward widely applied carbon and metal-impregnated carbon, alongside rapidly developing composites. [2]

**Dated milestones clarify the chronology.** Fine Sinter reports Japanese National Railways use of its sintered contact strips from 1951. A 2003 patent background identifies pure carbon as the first carbon-strip class and describes subsequent metal-impregnated carbon development, including Chinese work in the 1980s and locomotive loading tests in the early 1990s. Thus, the requested pure-carbon, powder-metallurgy, impregnated-carbon, and composite profiles are useful material categories, but pure carbon should not be assigned an invented worldwide precedence over all sintered strips. [22][23]

The later milestones are supported by the continuous-fiber patent [23], 2011 high-speed-slider study [24], 2012 impregnated-C/C record [25], and 2020 technical review [6].

**The category boundaries are structural.** Powder metallurgy describes a production route, metal impregnation describes how a second phase enters an existing porous body, and “composite” describes a multiphase material architecture. A copper-impregnated C/C strip can therefore belong to both the impregnated-carbon and composite categories. Fine Sinter’s metal-impregnated C/C product is a concrete example of that overlap. [22]

**The carrier is a separate component.** EuroSpec defines the complete contact strip as contact material attached to a carrier, commonly a metal profile such as an aluminium extrusion. A plain-carbon insert mounted on an aluminium carrier remains a plain-carbon contact material; the presence of the carrier does not make the rubbing surface a metal-impregnated carbon. This distinction matters when comparing composition, electrical paths, attachment integrity, and total strip mass. [26]

### Pure-carbon slides

**Pure carbon prioritizes overhead-wire protection.** Its most important advantages are low aggressiveness toward the contact wire, good arc resistance, and formation of a carbon film that can protect the copper surface. Historical comparisons also describe stable contact and relatively little radio interference. These benefits explain why carbon became important despite its lower strength and conductivity compared with metal-rich alternatives. [23]

**The composition is carbon-rich rather than monolithic.** Conventional formulations use petroleum or refinery coke and graphite, with pitch-derived binder carbon joining the particles after heat treatment. “Pure carbon” distinguishes the class from deliberately metal-impregnated or strongly metal-containing grades; it does not imply that the starting mixture consists of one chemically pure carbon powder. A high-speed-train patent example uses petroleum coke, artificial graphite, coal-tar pitch, and phenolic resin as precursor ingredients. [27][28]

**Manufacture creates the carbon body.** Carbon powders are mixed and kneaded with the binder, then pressed or hot-extruded, shaped, and roasted or carbonized. The binder provides forming behavior initially and becomes part of the carbon bonding structure during heat treatment. Particle distribution, forming conditions, binder conversion, pores, and internal interfaces consequently influence the finished strip’s mechanical integrity and electrical behavior. [27]

**The protective film is an advantage.** The carbon deposited during sliding can reduce direct aggressive interaction with the wire. This makes slide selection a paired-material problem: a strip may be desirable because it sacrifices some of its own material while limiting damage to the much longer overhead conductor. Low wire wear is therefore a more meaningful advantage than a statement about slide wear alone. [23]

**Brittleness is the principal mechanical weakness.** The conventional-carbon background in CN103159495A links weak graphite–binder-carbon interfaces and internal microcracks to low flexural and impact strength, breakage, and unstable performance. A strip must withstand vibration, impacts, and imperfect contact conditions, so a mechanically weak carbon body can lose pieces or fracture even when its basic sliding behavior is favorable. [27]

**Electrical resistance creates a second weakness.** Excessive resistivity can increase local heating and contribute to degradation, particularly under high-current or standstill conditions. Shang and colleagues’ review concludes that conventional pure-carbon strips combine low overhead-line abrasion with mechanical and electrical shortcomings that motivate improved materials. The logical response is not to eliminate carbon’s wire-protective function, but to improve the conductive and load-bearing structure around it. [2]

**Applications include qualified high-speed service.** Pure carbon should not be equated with a universally low-speed material. The copper-foam-modified CFRP study compares its new strip against a pure-carbon strip used on China’s high-speed railway, while the 2021 pure-carbon patent specifically targets high-speed-train pantographs. The applicable scenario is therefore an electric-traction system whose current, contact dynamics, attachment, and impact requirements are compatible with the selected grade. [29][28]

Documented scenarios include common electric-locomotive and electric-traction service, Poland’s 3000 V DC railway context from 2011, and current-collection tests using carbon strips with Cu-ETP contact wire.

**Pure carbon remains a useful reference.** Its strengths are low wire wear, lubrication, and favorable arc behavior. Its weaknesses are brittleness, lower impact strength, and a more restrictive electrical–thermal margin in demanding duties. It is most persuasive where wire preservation is important and the selected grade and assembly satisfy the actual current and mechanical loads.

### Powder-metallurgy slides

**Sintered metal matrices emphasize robustness.** Traditional powder-metallurgy slides use copper-based or iron-based powders as the principal matrix, with other metallic and nonmetallic constituents, including solid lubricants. Their main advantages are high mechanical strength, wear resistance, and substantially better integrated lubrication than a simple pure-metal sliding strip. [23]

**Pressing and sintering create the structure.** Powders are blended, compacted into the required form, and sintered so the particles bond into a load-bearing body. The matrix supplies mechanical support and electrical pathways, while graphite or other lubricating constituents modify sliding behavior. Composition, particle distribution, density, and consolidation therefore determine whether the product behaves as a conductive, self-lubricating contact material or as an excessively aggressive counterface. [23]

**The advantages are supported by service history.** Fine Sinter describes its sintered strip as a composite powder-metallurgy product and lists low power loss, wear resistance, arc resistance, lubricity, and high-speed performance among its characteristics. Its reported Japanese National Railways adoption demonstrates that this class is an established railway technology, rather than merely a laboratory alternative. [22]

**The main disadvantage concerns the wire.** The 2003 historical comparison describes serious copper-wire wear from some powder-metallurgy strips, worsened when external lubrication is difficult to maintain. It also associates arcing with additional mechanical and electrical wear and radio interference. The issue is therefore not simply whether the strip survives: a mechanically durable strip can impose an unfavorable maintenance burden on the overhead conductor. [23]

**Modern products need formulation-specific judgments.** Fine Sinter’s favorable product description and the older patent’s severe-wear criticism address different products and comparison contexts. They are consistent with a broad class whose performance depends on matrix chemistry, lubricant structure, density, and application conditions. Neither “powder metallurgy always damages the wire” nor “sintered material always prevents wire damage” follows from those accounts.

**High-speed research extends the family.** A 2011 study developed a copper-rich slider containing carbon fiber, graphite, and other constituents, using a reported consolidation pressure of 200 MPa and sintering temperature of 880 °C. Although it is also a reinforced composite, its processing demonstrates that powder metallurgy continued to support high-speed-slider development rather than disappearing when carbon-based grades became widespread. [24]

**Weather can change the wear balance.** A 2015 Korean railway study links rain-related lubricant loss to increased friction and accelerated local wear. Its improved slider underwent driving tests during rainy and winter conditions, with no uneven or abnormal wear reported in the described tests. This illustrates why a formulation that performs well under dry laboratory sliding may still need different density or lubrication characteristics for outdoor railway duty. [30]

**Powder metallurgy remains application-specific.** Its strongest case is a railway duty requiring robust mechanical support, effective current conduction, and an engineered lubrication system. Its main selection condition is acceptable paired wear with the actual contact wire. A high strip-life claim is insufficient if the conductor wear, arcing, or environmental response is unfavorable.

### Metal-impregnated carbon slides

**Impregnation improves carbon’s conductive network.** Metal-impregnated carbon starts with a porous carbon body and introduces metal into its pore structure. Historical impregnants include copper, lead, tin, antimony, nickel, and their associated alloys. The metal forms a fine network within the carbon, improving conductivity, heat conduction, strength, and toughness compared with the original carbon body. [23]

**The manufacturing order distinguishes the class.** The carbon skeleton is formed first, after which metal is driven into its pores under elevated temperature and pressure. This differs from merely blending copper powder into the original carbon mixture, plating carbon particles, or impregnating a carbon body with pitch to densify it. All can produce multiphase sliding materials, but only metal infiltration into the existing porous body is the defining mechanism here. [23]

**Its main advantage is improved current transfer.** A connected metallic phase reduces resistance through the body and can lower contact resistance, while higher thermal conductivity helps spread heat away from the contact region. This is particularly valuable when an otherwise attractive carbon surface becomes electrically or thermally restrictive. The carbon skeleton continues to contribute lubrication and wire compatibility, so the purpose is to retain useful carbon behavior while improving its supporting properties.

**Pressure-infiltrated Cu/SC supplies a concrete example.** Li and colleagues’ 2021 study produced copper/sintered-carbon composites by pressure infiltration. Microscopy showed a spatial copper network nearly filling the carbon-matrix pores, with mechanical interlocking between the phases; the reported compressive strength reached 159.81 MPa. This is a compressive-strength result for a particular slider composite, not a tensile-strength counterpart to the wire values in Part A. [31]

**Graphite improves some properties while weakening others.** In that study, increasing graphite content improved the electrical conductivity of the carbon matrices but reduced mechanical properties. Under dry sliding, friction coefficient and wear rate decreased as graphite increased, with a minimum reported friction coefficient of 0.096. The result demonstrates that improving lubrication and electrical behavior can compete with structural strength even within one impregnated-material system. [31]

**Current changes the wear mechanism.** In current-carrying tests, the Cu/SC friction coefficient first increased and then decreased as current rose, while wear rate continued increasing. The authors identified predominantly adhesive wear at 5 A and corrosive wear at 15 A. These are laboratory test currents for the particular contact configuration; their importance lies in showing that friction reduction does not necessarily mean reduced electrically assisted wear. [31]

**Manufacturing complexity is a principal disadvantage.** Producing a sound carbon body and infiltrating it consistently adds process stages and cost. Historical comparisons also report poorer impact resistance than desired and substantially greater copper-wire wear than with pure carbon. “Improved toughness relative to the original carbon” and “still inadequate impact resistance for a demanding application” can both be true, because they describe different comparisons. [23]

**The wear comparison depends on its baseline.** Toyo Tanso describes improved lubricity in its copper-impregnated carbon products relative to conventional contact strips, with reduced trolley-wire wear and use by JR and private railway companies. That supplier account is compatible with the historical criticism: an impregnated carbon can be less aggressive than a conventional metal-rich strip while still wearing the wire more than a suitable plain-carbon grade. [32]

**Applications emphasize current and durability demands.** Metal-impregnated carbon is relevant to locomotive and high-speed current collection where plain carbon’s electrical or mechanical margin is restrictive. The British Morgan MY7D product appears in the historical development account, while Japanese products extend the approach into metal-impregnated C/C architectures. Selection should therefore specify the impregnant, carbon skeleton, finished properties, and assembly—not just the word “impregnated.” [23][22]

**Impregnation is a compromise, not an endpoint.** It improves the electrical and supporting mechanical properties of carbon, but adds manufacturing complexity and can change wire wear and arc-related damage. Its value is greatest when the conductive network solves a demonstrated current-transfer or heating problem without imposing excessive counterface wear.

### Composite slides

**Composites tailor several functions separately.** The broad category includes carbon-fiber-reinforced carbon, carbon–copper materials, copper-infiltrated sintered carbon, and polymer-matrix systems reinforced with carbon fibers, graphite, and metal networks. The central design opportunity is to assign different roles to different phases: fibers for reinforcement, carbon or graphite for sliding behavior, and metal for electrical and thermal conduction. [2]

| Composite architecture | Basic composition | Manufacturing principle | Intended advantage | Controlling issue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C/C | Carbon fibers in carbon matrix | Form, consolidate, and carbonize | Strength with low mass | Arc damage and architecture |
| C/C–metal | C/C skeleton plus infiltrated metal | Carbon-body manufacture, then infiltration | Strength plus conductive network | Metal melting or loss |
| C/Cu or Cu/SC | Carbon/graphite with copper | Powder consolidation or pressure infiltration | Conduction and lubrication | Interfaces and paired wear |
| Metal-network CFRP | Fibers, graphite, resin, copper network | Impregnation and hot pressing | Toughness and low resistance | Matrix and tribolayer stability |

**C/C addresses carbon’s mechanical weakness.** Here C/C means carbon fibers reinforcing a carbon matrix, rather than simply any carbon-filled resin. Fiber architecture provides a way to improve load carrying and damage resistance while preserving a predominantly carbon-based sliding material. Metal impregnation may then be added, so C/C and metal-impregnated carbon are not competing labels in every product. [6]

**A woven route links manufacture and attachment.** Shang and colleagues describe a process using carbon-fiber strands, carbon and metal powders, resin-assisted weaving and lamination, heating and pressing, treatment around 1,200 °C, and copper-alloy impregnation. The review reports a 100,000 km running test in which directly bolted threads showed no rupture or loosening. That example is important because reinforcement improved the practical attachment design, not only a small-specimen strength value. [2]

**Direct bolting illustrates an architectural benefit.** Fine Sinter describes its metal-impregnated C/C strip as sufficiently strengthened by carbon fiber to be tapped and bolted directly to the pantograph head. The product is also described as lightweight and having high bending strength, impact value, and arc resistance. Such features show that material development can change the carrier and fastening arrangement as well as the rubbing surface. [22]

**Carbon–copper interfaces require special attention.** Shang’s review identifies poor mutual wetting between copper and carbon as a key preparation problem. Electroless plating or electroplating copper onto the carbon surface is one response, followed by powder metallurgy, hot-press sintering, or hot-press diffusion bonding. The objective is a sound, connected material rather than disconnected conductive particles in a weak structure. [2]

**Improved conductivity can raise friction.** A copper-plated graphite example reviewed by Shang and colleagues reached 52.5% IACS and a maximum reported hardness of 45.72 HBS. It also had a higher friction coefficient than the non-copper-plated comparison material. This is a useful counterexample to the idea that a more conductive or harder composite must simultaneously be a better sliding surface. [2]

**Formulation examples explain the manufacturing diversity.** One disclosed precursor blend in CN104926347A contains 30% chopped carbon fiber, 30% phenolic resin, 20% pitch coke, 10% flake graphite, and 10% copper powder by mass—a complete 100% starting mixture. Its described molding stage uses 120 °C, 1 MPa, and a two-hour hold before further treatment. These are precursor and processing values for that patent embodiment, rather than the final composition or universal recipe of a carbonized slide. [33]

**Polymer composites form a distinct branch.** The copper-foam-modified CFRP study used a carbon-fiber and flaky-graphite resin mixture introduced into a copper network and consolidated by mold hot pressing. Its electrical resistance was much lower than that of the pure-carbon high-speed reference strip, while added carbon fiber improved impact strength and wear resistance with little change in anti-friction performance. A polymer-matrix composite should therefore remain distinct from C/C when interpreting thermal behavior and manufacture. [29]

**More graphite eventually became detrimental.** Within that study, increasing flaky graphite from 0 to 15 wt% reduced electrical resistance and friction coefficient, with little change in impact strength or wear rate. Increasing graphite further to 20 wt% produced a sharp wear-rate rise, associated with a thick, weakly consolidated tribolayer that curled and detached. A tribolayer is the altered surface layer produced during rubbing; its stability can matter more than the starting ingredient’s lubricity. [29]

**Prototype comparisons demonstrate potential, not class averages.** The 2011 high-speed-slider study compared its copper-rich, carbon-fiber-containing material with the metal-impregnated carbon product Rh82Mb. Its abstract reports a 20% reduction in friction coefficient, a 1.3% reduction in abrasion ratio, and increases described as 1.7-fold in impact toughness and 65-fold in conductance. These are the authors’ relative results for that prototype and comparator, rather than absolute performance specifications for all reinforced slides. [24]

**Vehicle testing supplies stronger application evidence.** The 2020 Japanese review reports actual-vehicle testing of high-strength C/C strips and a 35% improvement in the stated wear characteristics compared with copper-based sintered-alloy strips. The precise improvement index is unspecified in the abstract, so the reported percentage should retain that description rather than become a claim of 35% longer service life. Nevertheless, the vehicle comparison establishes a more application-relevant result than a laboratory strength improvement alone. [6]

**Electrical loading can reverse mechanical gains.** In a 2014 pin-on-disc study pairing C/C with QCr0.5 contact material, wear increased with current and sliding velocity. Friction coefficient increased with velocity and load, while wear decreased with increasing load over the tested range; at fixed current and velocity, 70 N gave the lowest arc rate. The contrasting trends show that friction, contact stability, and electrically assisted wear cannot be reduced to one material hardness or friction value. [34]

**Arc energy attacks the conductive network.** An IEEJ study of copper-alloy-infiltrated C/C found wear proportional to arc electric charge and substantially increased wear when infiltrated alloy melted and was ejected from the strip. It identified oxidative and adhesive wear as main mechanisms. High-strength fibers alone therefore do not guarantee high resistance to electrically driven damage: the stability of the surrounding matrix and infiltrated metal remains decisive. [35]

**Composite disadvantages depend on architecture.** Long processing cycles, cost, poor interfaces, reinforcement damage, unstable conductive paths, and current-dependent surface degradation are recurring concerns in the reviewed materials. Carbon/metal reinforcement can also increase overhead-wire wear when a more extensive metallic phase reaches the sliding surface. The composite label consequently identifies a design opportunity, not a single superior grade. [2]

**The composite-selection judgment is conditional.** C/C is attractive where carbon’s mechanical integrity or attachment design needs improvement; carbon–metal systems are attractive where conduction is restrictive; metal-network polymer systems offer another route to toughness and conductivity. The deciding evidence is the finished architecture’s current-carrying wear, arc response, wire compatibility, and vehicle-level performance—not the presence of reinforcement alone.

### Matching slide materials to operating conditions

**Current demand distinguishes operating scenarios.** For a given DC power demand, lower supply voltage requires greater current, increasing the importance of low-resistance current paths and heat removal. EuroSpec’s 2016 specification addresses this distinction directly: its general two-strip pantograph requirements include 700 A for AC and 2,000 A for DC, with standstill requirements of 200 A at 3 kV DC and 300 A at 1.5 kV DC. These are specified assembly duties, not universal ratings of individual carbon grades. [26]

**Standstill exposes a different thermal problem.** The contact remains concentrated in one location rather than moving along the wire. A 2021 *Energies* study investigated how carbon–metal strip properties affected Cu-ETP contact-wire heating, using a 100 mm² wire, two carbon strips, a 200 A current for 30 minutes, and a measured contact force of 102 N. Its modeled acceptance arrangement used a maximum wire temperature of 120 °C, corresponding to an 80 °C rise from a 40 °C initial condition. [36]

**The strip can overheat the wire.** The study found that its very-low-copper material A exceeded the adopted temperature criterion. For reference material C, the measured average wire temperature was 111.6 °C, but the maximum was 121.8 °C; the finite-element model gave a maximum of 120.6 °C. The distinction between average and maximum is practically important: an acceptable average does not establish compliance with a maximum-temperature requirement. [36]

**Thermal conductivity governed heat spreading.** The authors varied thermal conductivity and specific heat in 15 model combinations and reported a threshold strip thermal conductivity of approximately 23.7 W/(m·K) for their 120 °C wire-temperature criterion. The reference material’s reported conductivity was 24.5 W/(m·K). This result supports selecting the strip’s heat-transfer properties alongside electrical resistivity, while keeping the threshold tied to the study’s geometry, loading, and thermal conditions. [36]

**This explains the impregnation trade-off.** Adding a suitable metal network may reduce electrical resistance and improve heat conduction, helping protect the wire from concentrated heating. Yet increasing metallic content can also alter wire wear, density, and arc behavior. A favorable result therefore comes from balancing the carbon surface, conductive network, and complete heat path—not maximizing metal content without regard to the other consequences.

**Added mass affects the assembly choice.** EuroSpec gives, for a 1,950 mm pantograph-head profile with a 60 mm contact-strip width, a design mass of 3.7 kg for a plain-carbon strip and 4.5 kg for its impregnated counterpart. The difference is approximately 21.6%, calculated as 4.5/3.7 − 1. Because these are complete strip-design masses, they illustrate an assembly-level consequence of material selection rather than a direct comparison of carbon and metal densities. [26]

**Attachment integrity belongs in the material assessment.** EuroSpec requires additional electrical connections for its DC configuration and makes mechanical clamping of the contact material mandatory for DC applications, while permitting it for AC. It also addresses arcing protection for the metal carrier and bonding. A low-resistance, mechanically strong insert still depends on sound current transfer and attachment at the carrier interface. [26]

**Network acceptance remains region-specific.** EuroSpec deliberately excludes contact-material choice from its harmonization effort because permitted materials and network practices differ by area. Poland’s adoption of carbon-based rather than copper strips from 2011 is one regional transition, but the 2021 thermal study concerns carbon–metal compositions within that transition, not proof that every Polish strip was unimpregnated pure carbon. [26][36]

**Maintenance should assess both wearing components.** A wire-protective strip that breaks too readily is unsuitable; a long-lived strip that accelerates conductor wear can also be unattractive. Wire material, strip formulation, contact dynamics, arcing, attachment, and weather jointly determine the outcome. The practical objective is reliable current collection and acceptable combined maintenance burden, rather than maximum life of either consumable considered alone.

**Conditions behind the comparison**

The **IACS basis** is important: 100% IACS refers to annealed electrolytic copper with a volume resistivity of \(1.724\times10^{-7}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}\) at 20°C [44]. Generic conductor references report 100% IACS for soft or annealed copper, whereas the contact-wire specifications above generally use minimum values such as 97%, 80% or 76% [44][174]. Those generic values should not replace grade-specific contact-wire data. Likewise, the Lamifil tables report %IACS but do not disclose their reference temperature, limiting direct comparison [109].

The strength values are **minimum specification values**, not averages, and the numerical sequences are tied to nominal cross-section. A Chinese copper-tin standard gives CuSn0.2 in the Y state a minimum of **520 MPa** and **78 %IACS at 20°C** for a diameter above 1.20–3.00 mm, demonstrating why grade, state and diameter must remain attached to the value [222]. Generic oxygen-free-copper data similarly span **175–496 MPa at 295 K** across different cold-worked plate, bar and wire products; those observations are not a single contact-wire value [174]. A separate silver-content experiment reports **yield strength**, not tensile strength, increasing only from 310 to about 317 MPa as silver increased from 0.03 to 0.85 wt% under 50% cold work [174].

The main composition trade-off is visible most clearly for CuMg. One pre-cutoff preparation increased Mg from **0.025% to 0.58%**, raising tensile strength from **420 to 580 MPa** while reducing conductivity from **98.0 to 63.5 %IACS**; the associated CTMH requirements were at least **530 MPa** and **65 %IACS** [159]. This supports a strength–conductivity trade-off rather than a universal ranking. CuSn likewise has materially different conductivity values for regular and high-conductivity variants [109]. For CuCrZr, continuous-extrusion evidence indicates fine-grained and precipitation-strengthening mechanisms, but it does not supply the table’s contact-wire values [230]. A separate wear experiment found the least wear after ageing at **500°C for 2 h** and the greatest wear after **530°C for 2 h**, but reported neither tensile strength nor railway speed [218].

“Applicable speed” is a **system-level application condition**, not an intrinsic alloy maximum. The contact wire is the solid, pre-sagged overhead conductor supported by the messenger wire and droppers to provide a stable pantograph-contact profile [38]. The California high-speed OCS document specifies systems using copper or copper-alloy contact wire up to **354 km/h** on dedicated very-high-speed sections and **200 km/h** on shared-use corridors, but it does not attribute either value to pure copper or any named alloy [38]. Contact quality also depends on catenary geometry, suspension response, wind, ice, dust and other contaminants [39]. Too little contact force causes intermittent contact, arcing and power interruption; too much accelerates wear of both strip and wire [39]. A later system model used 70 N static contact force and 250/300 km/h simulations while ignoring aerodynamics, but it was published after the cutoff and did not identify a material-specific wire [213].

A manufacturer’s statement of more than 100,000 km installed in more than 30 countries concerns its wires generally and does not identify the material in each country, so it is not assigned to a table row [109]. **Additional documented evidence**

**Development sequence and taxonomy**

Pantograph slides are sliding current-collection components: they must conduct traction current while maintaining a stable, low-wear interface with the contact wire. The main material requirements are low friction, self-lubrication, wear and arc resistance, electrical conductivity, and resistance to impact and fatigue [235]. The chronology below is therefore a relative technology sequence, not a claim that every country adopted the materials in exactly the same order.

The four classes are also not defined by one criterion alone. Pure carbon is primarily a carbon/graphite material; powder-metallurgy slides are defined mainly by powder consolidation and sintering; metal-impregnated carbon combines a porous carbon body with an infiltrated metal phase; and composite slides combine deliberately distinct constituents or structural phases. IEC 62499:2021 broadened its scope from “carbon contact strips” to contact strips generally and introduced separate terminology for metalized and metal contact strips, so product names should not be treated as perfectly interchangeable [232].


**Pure carbon slides**

**Pure carbon slides**—also termed pure carbon slide plates or carbon contact strips—are the earliest carbon-based class in the requested sequence. A pre-cutoff technical source describes the material as carbon particles of different sizes; hard particles contact the copper-alloy contact wire without readily forming metallic adhesion, so the resulting wire/slide wear is mainly abrasive rather than adhesive [236]. A later review gives a more specific representative recipe of pitch coke, petroleum coke and graphite with added carbon black, followed by mixing, kneading, moulding or extrusion, baking and graphitization . Because that detailed recipe comes from a post-31-March-2022 review, it should be treated as an attributed formulation rather than as a universally verified cutoff-bounded composition. The pre-cutoff evidence does not establish one universal binder, particle-size distribution or graphitization condition for all pure-carbon products [411, 517].


Pure carbon’s main service advantage is **self-lubrication with relatively low contact-wire abrasion**. The 2016 technical review describes pure-carbon strips as commonly used and states that their abrasion of the overhead contact line is small [235]. The carbon material also avoids the strong metallic affinity that makes an all-metal slide prone to adhesive wear; the historical account explicitly describes carbon and metal-impregnated carbon as replacing pure-metal slides because of this wear problem . Under high-speed sliding with electric current in a block-on-ring experiment published in 2014, pure carbon showed better conductivity than the tested metal-impregnated carbon, although this was a test-specific comparison rather than a grade-independent rule [233].

Electrical current can produce a mixed tribological effect. In a 2011 pin-on-disc study of a copper contact line and carbon strip, increasing current reduced the friction coefficient, which the authors attributed to an electrical-lubrication effect; however, wear volume increased with current because of arc-discharge ablation. With current, arc erosion, oxidative wear and metal melting were dominant, whereas abrasive and adhesive wear were dominant without current [172]. The same study describes the high-speed railway context as approximately **250–400 km/h** and **8,000–10,000 kW** traction power, but those are system conditions, not a pure-carbon material speed rating [172].

The main disadvantages are mechanical and damage-related. The 2016 review reports relatively low mechanical strength and states that pure carbon could not fully satisfy the requirements of high-speed locomotive development [235]. It also reports a “somewhat high” specific-conductivity value as a limitation, but supplies no test basis or resistivity convention; that wording should not be converted into a new quantitative conductivity claim. The same review records a UK pure-carbon product with low resistance, high strength and a reported wear rate of **2.5–5.4 mm per 10,000 locomotive-km**, showing that grade-specific products can differ materially from the broad class description . These observations should not be averaged: they represent different formulations and evidence conditions.

Under high-speed current-carrying laboratory conditions, increasing vibration increased the wear rates of both pure-carbon and metal-impregnated-carbon samples. The metal-impregnated sample had better wear resistance, while the principal pure-carbon arc-erosion forms were erosion pits and thermal-stress cracks [233]. A railway reliability study further records abrasion and electro-erosion, uneven strip edges, arc melting, detachment of carbon pieces, cracking and peeling. An uneven edge can accelerate or even damage the contact wire; damage depends on contact force, humidity, temperature, ice, material defects and infrastructure quality [237]. A separate 2018 study of pantograph strips identifies localized wear associated with excessive arc discharge and relates wear-profile distribution to contact-wire stagger and arc-energy distribution, but its tested carbon strip was copper-infiltrated rather than pure carbon [82].


- China changed to carbon slide material in **1967**; the cited source treats this as a Chinese service-adoption milestone, not the first invention or worldwide adoption of carbon slides [236].
- In Poland, carbon contact strips became mandatory from **2011** under TSI LOC & PAS, while copper contact strips were prohibited. Polish lines use **3 kV DC**, and foreign strips originally adapted to **15 kV AC** or **25 kV AC** systems were adjusted to local conditions [234]. This is evidence for the broader carbon-contact-strip class, not proof that every such strip was unmodified pure carbon.
- A 2021 Polish service study concerned AKP-4E and 5ZL collectors on EU07 and EU09 locomotives and a **3 kV DC** railway system; it explicitly states that currently used strips were carbon composite. Consequently, its operational damage evidence cannot be generalized automatically to pure-carbon slides [237].
- TB/T 2809—2017, published on **12 October 2017** and implemented on **1 May 2018**, permits pure carbon, metal-impregnated carbon or other carbon-composite slides as wear-test counterbodies. Its table associates CT/CTA contact wires with **≤160 km/h** and CTS/CTSM/CTM with a value transcribed as **“N 200”**, but the slide options are grouped together; the standard does not establish a pure-carbon-only service qualification [164].
- An Indian Railways specification identifies metallised carbon strips for **25 kV AC, 50 Hz** locomotives and EMU/MEMU, with operation up to **250 km/h**, temperatures from **−10°C** to **75°C**, and humid or salt-laden coastal conditions. This is useful adjacent evidence for carbon-strip service but is not evidence for pure carbon; the available document is a later revision containing post-cutoff material [231].

Accordingly, the supported conclusion for pure carbon is that it offers self-lubrication, relatively low contact-wire abrasion and, in some energized sliding tests, favorable conductivity, but its strength, arc damage, uneven wear and vibration sensitivity limit unqualified high-speed claims. It continued to coexist with later classes because it is inexpensive and operationally established, while powder-metallurgy, metal-impregnated and composite technologies were developed to improve particular weaknesses [235].

**Powder-metallurgy slides**

**Powder-metallurgy slides** are a process-defined class rather than a single chemistry. The pre-cutoff literature distinguishes copper-based and iron-based powder-metallurgy slides [236]. A later synthesis further describes iron-based matrices containing iron, nickel and copper with hard phases such as ferro-molybdenum and ferro-chromium, and copper-based matrices containing copper and tin with iron- or ferro-molybdenum hard phases; carbon and lead act as lubricating constituents . Another review summarizes the broader formulation as metal powder—usually copper or iron—combined with lubricants such as carbon, lead and MoS₂ .


The main reason for developing powder-metallurgy slides was to obtain a harder, more wear-resistant and still lubricating alternative to metallic or pure-copper slides. The historical account states that Chinese powder-metallurgy slides could continuously generate a lubricating film, reduce friction, limit their own wear and reduce contact-wire wear . In a source comparison table, copper-based powder-metallurgy slides are reported with density **6.5–8.4 g/cm³**, hardness **62–90 HB**, impact value **≥5 J/cm²**, and a copper-wire wear ratio of **0.02–0.048 mm²/(10,000 passages)**; iron-based slides are reported with density **6.5–8.0 g/cm³**, hardness **90–130 HB**, impact value **≥5 J/cm²**, and a wear ratio of **≥0.05 mm²/(10,000 passages)** [236]. These are source-table values for particular variants and indicators, not harmonized class-wide measurements.

Post-cutoff reviews list earlier powder-metallurgy and composite studies but the supplied excerpts give no results, so they remain verification leads rather than filled cells .


**Conclusion.** The deciding condition is the complete strip–wire–pantograph system, not the class label: powder metallurgy favors mechanical robustness, metal-impregnated carbon a conductivity/wear balance, and composites require formulation, thermal, attachment and route qualification [247][244][185]. Cu-impregnated carbon is nested under metal-impregnated carbon; Cu–Ti C/C is an overlapping hybrid, not a fifth row [244].

**Evidence-qualified comparison**

TB/T 1842.1-2002 names the powder-metallurgy class; cited 1993, 2015 and 2020 studies are listed without their results, so its trade-offs remain conditional. [248]

**Conclusion.** Powder metallurgy favors strength but can sacrifice wire life; impregnated carbon trades low resistance against processing and wire-wear risks; composite performance remains formulation- and test-dependent. Selection must therefore consider the complete wire–slide–network system—voltage, load, speed and arcing—not the material label alone. [249][250][253]
