# The Impact of Protein Intake on Muscle and Bone Health in Older Adults

**Protein targets depend on clinical context.** The WHO/FAO/UNU adult safe intake and EFSA population reference intake are approximately 0.83 g protein/kg body weight/day, including for healthy older adults. Older-adult expert guidance generally recommends 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy ageing and 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day during selected illness or rehabilitation contexts. These higher targets emphasize muscle preservation and function, rather than nitrogen balance alone. [1][2]

**Higher intake helps selectively, not uniformly.** Randomized trials show improvements in some muscle-mass, strength and walking outcomes, particularly when low intake is corrected or nutrition accompanies resistance exercise. Bone evidence is more supportive of adequate protein within a complete nutrition-and-exercise programme than of protein alone as a fracture-prevention treatment. Animal proteins often deliver more digestible essential amino acids per serving, but carefully designed plant-based diets can also support muscle health. [3][4][5]

**The report addresses four practical questions.** Using information published through 2023, within the requested early-2024 cutoff, it first compares recommendations and their foundations; then examines muscle trials; distinguishes bone-density findings from fracture evidence; and finally evaluates protein sources and individual essential amino acids. The population is principally adults aged 65 years and older, including healthy, frail, undernourished and osteosarcopenic groups.

## 1. Summary of Existing Recommended Protein Intakes

### International recommendations and their intended populations

**The main difference is purpose.** Formal dietary reference values describe population-level adequacy, whereas older-adult expert recommendations also address preservation of muscle, recovery from illness and physical functioning. Consequently, 0.83 and 1.2 g/kg/day are not simply competing answers to an identical question.

All daily weight-based values below mean grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day.

| EFSA, 2012 | Healthy adults of both sexes, including older adults | Average requirement 0.66; population reference intake 0.83 g/kg/day | Nitrogen balance and requirement variability |
| PROT-AGE Study Group, 2013 | Healthy adults aged ≥65; active or ill subgroups | Healthy: 1.0–1.2; active: ≥1.2; illness: 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day | Expert synthesis of metabolic, epidemiological and functional evidence |
| ESPEN Expert Group, 2014 | Healthy older adults and those with illness | Healthy: 1.0–1.2; illness: generally 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day | Clinical and muscle-function-oriented evidence synthesis |

The WHO/FAO/UNU and EFSA values are documented in EFSA’s reference comparison and the Dutch Health Council review. PROT-AGE and ESPEN ranges are summarized in the clinical and dietary-protein reviews. [6][1][2][7]

**WHO retains an adult reference.** The 2007 joint WHO/FAO/UNU framework sets a safe intake of 0.83 g/kg/day and applies the adult value to older people: its assessment did not establish a convincing age-related increase in the requirement per kilogram when the criterion was whole-body nitrogen balance. That is an adult recommendation encompassing older adults, rather than an older-adult-specific functional-health target. [6]

**EFSA distinguishes average from protective intake.** Its average requirement, or AR, is 0.66 g/kg/day; its population reference intake, or PRI, is 0.83 g/kg/day. The PRI is intended to cover approximately 97.5% of healthy adults’ requirements and incorporates variability between individuals. EFSA also considered muscle, bone and other health outcomes, but retained nitrogen balance as the quantitative foundation for the reference value. [8]

EFSA’s PRI used the 97.5th percentile of requirements and an assumed 47% efficiency of dietary-protein utilization for maintenance; evidence on bone health, body weight, muscle mass and kidney function was insufficient to derive an alternative PRI.

**PROT-AGE prioritizes maintaining function.** The European Union Geriatric Medicine Society commissioned the group’s evidence-based recommendations for adults aged at least 65 years. Its healthy-ageing range of 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day rises to at least 1.2 g/kg/day for physically active or training older adults, and commonly 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day with acute or chronic disease. The reasoning combines altered protein metabolism with the practical objective of preserving or regaining muscle and physical function. [2]

**ESPEN similarly uses clinical context.** The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism’s expert recommendations place healthy older adults around 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day, with higher amounts during illness. The frequently quoted overall range of 1.0–1.5 g/kg/day therefore encompasses different health states; its upper end is not a universal requirement for every healthy older person. [7]

### Nordic and national recommendations

**National guidance shows genuine variation.** Germany, Austria and Switzerland share the D-A-CH reference framework, while Nordic guidance explicitly separates formal reference values from food-planning targets. Ireland gives particular attention to frailty, sarcopenia and undernutrition.

**Nordic figures describe different recommendation types.** NNR2023 retains the formal adult AR and recommended intake at 0.66 and 0.83 g/kg/day, including in later life. For planning diets for groups of older adults, however, it recommends 18% of energy from protein—approximately 1.2 g/kg/day under its planning assumptions. Its protein chapter also gives an older-adult range of 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day. [9][10]

**Earlier Nordic comparisons use another range.** The Irish report’s comparison of the 2014 Nordic recommendations gives 1.1–1.3 g/kg/day, corresponding to 15–20% of energy; a 2023 PROMISS paper repeats that national-guidance range. These figures should retain their dates and purposes. Percentage of energy does not convert into one fixed g/kg/day value without specifying energy intake and body weight. [11][12]

**D-A-CH increased its older-adult value.** The earlier 2008 framework applied 0.8 g/kg/day to older as well as younger adults. Its derivation started with the FAO/WHO requirement, added an allowance for individual variability and accounted for mixed-diet digestibility. The 2017 revision adopted the higher older-adult reference of 1.0 g/kg/day, representing an age-specific adequacy judgment rather than continued use of the same adult value at every age. [6][4]

**Swiss attribution needs particular care.** The joint D-A-CH older-adult figure is 1.0 g/kg/day, but Kressig’s 2023 overview describes the Swiss Society for Nutrition, or SGE, as still using a general-adult value of 0.8 g/kg/day while an age-specific federal recommendation was being developed. Thus, “the Swiss recommendation” should identify which published framework it means. [2]

**Ireland combines quantities with food guidance.** For healthy older adults, FSAI emphasizes a more protein-dense diet and sufficient high-quality protein at a minimum of two meals daily. Its numerical ranges are especially explicit for at-risk and ill groups. The report also discusses international clinical ranges extending to 2.0 g/kg/day in severe illness, injury or marked undernutrition—situations requiring individualized clinical nutrition. [11]

### Why nitrogen balance and functional evidence produce different answers

**Nitrogen balance measures whole-body maintenance.** Protein contains nitrogen, so investigators compare dietary nitrogen intake with losses through urine, faeces, skin and other routes. In a weight-stable person receiving adequate energy, approximate equality suggests that total body protein is being maintained. It does not directly establish optimal leg strength, walking ability, bone density or resistance to illness. [14]

**Small measurement errors can matter.** Munro’s August 1981 background paper for the joint FAO/WHO/UNU consultation noted that nitrogen balance is a small difference—often around ±2 g nitrogen/day—between much larger intake and loss quantities. Errors in recording intake, measuring excretion, accounting for skin losses or controlling energy balance can therefore change the apparent result. [15]

**Higher targets address another physiological problem.** Older muscle can show “anabolic resistance”: a reduced muscle-building response to a given meal or exercise stimulus. Short-term metabolic studies and epidemiological associations consequently support considering protein amounts above the adult reference. PROT-AGE and ESPEN combine these findings with clinical and functional objectives; their ranges are expert evidence syntheses, not thresholds derived from a single definitive dose-response trial. [16]

**Formal committees apply a different threshold.** NNR2023 judged the evidence insufficient to raise the formal AR, while endorsing a higher older-adult planning target. Similarly, the Dutch Health Council concluded that randomized evidence did not convincingly justify a higher population reference for all healthy older adults, despite possible benefits for lean mass and exercise-associated strength. This disagreement concerns the strength of evidence needed for a universal reference, as well as the outcomes being optimized. [9][1]

**The practical difference is substantial.** For a 70-kg reference adult, the calculated daily amounts are 58.1 g at 0.83 g/kg/day and 84 g at 1.2 g/kg/day: 70 × 0.83 and 70 × 1.2, respectively. Selecting the appropriate target should consider habitual intake, nutritional status, exercise, illness and kidney function. Severe chronic kidney disease warrants individualized advice rather than automatic application of a higher-protein range. [11]

**Recommendation judgment: context comes first.** Approximately 0.8–0.83 g/kg/day remains a formal adult reference, while 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day is a widely supported older-adult planning range and 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day serves selected clinical or training contexts.

## 2. Research on the Impact on Muscle Health

### Key randomized controlled trials

**The trials support selective benefits.** ProMuscle, ProMuscle in Practice and PROMISS provide positive findings for particular muscle or functional outcomes. CALM supplies an important counterexample: substantially increasing intake did not change its measured muscle-protein-synthesis outcomes after one year.

**Different outcomes answer different questions.** A randomized controlled trial, or RCT, compares groups assigned to different interventions. Muscle-protein synthesis, or MPS, measures the production of muscle proteins; lean body mass includes non-fat tissues and is not identical to skeletal-muscle mass. The Short Physical Performance Battery, or SPPB, scores balance, walking and chair-rise performance from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating better performance. [17]

### ProMuscle and its community translation

**ProMuscle tested protein with and without training.** Its original programme used two daily milk-protein drinks, supplying approximately 15 g after breakfast and 15 g after lunch or exercise. The 24-week resistance programme involved supervised progressive exercise twice weekly. The completed 2012 reports distinguish improved physical performance from the additional muscle-mass gain obtained when protein accompanied training. [18]

**The comparison already exceeded 0.8 g/kg/day.** The earlier review describes control intake around 1.0 g/kg/day, making ProMuscle relevant to whether additional protein can help beyond basic adult reference intake. Its approximately 1.2-point SPPB difference at 12 weeks, subsequently used in planning ProMuscle in Practice, is a functional programme benchmark; it should not be read as a numerical estimate of protein’s isolated contribution to muscle-mass gain. [19][20]

**ProMuscle in Practice tested real-world delivery.** Dietitians helped participants reach at least 25 g protein at breakfast, lunch and dinner through ordinary or enriched foods. Physiotherapists supervised two one-hour resistance sessions weekly during the intensive phase. This combined intervention therefore tests a feasible muscle-health service, rather than a protein supplement alone. [17]

The multicentre RCT was conducted in five Dutch municipalities; its planned sample was 200 and the reported baseline comparison included 168 participants (82 intervention and 86 control). Participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥65 years who were frail, prefrail or experiencing strength loss, with 12 weeks of intensive support followed by 12 weeks of moderate support.

**Its gains extended beyond body composition.** At 12 weeks, adjusted intervention-minus-control differences included +36.2 newtons in knee-extension strength, +16.0 kg in estimated leg-press maximum and +11.3 kg in estimated leg-extension maximum. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals were 17.8–54.7 N, 8.5–23.6 kg and 7.6–14.9 kg. Together with the table’s mass and chair-rise findings, these results indicate improvement across several relevant domains. [17]

**Age affected one response more clearly.** In a post hoc analysis, the lean-mass difference was +0.9 kg among participants aged 75 or younger and +0.1 kg among those older than 75. Older participants nevertheless improved several strength outcomes. The practical implication is to assess strength and task performance alongside mass: a small mass response does not automatically mean that an intervention has failed functionally. [17]

### PROMISS and CALM: positive function versus unchanged synthesis

**PROMISS directly targeted lower habitual intake.** Participants were selected for intake below 1.0 g/kg adjusted body weight/day. Advice aimed for at least 1.2 g/kg/day and one daily high-protein meal; the timing arm additionally targeted 7.5–10 g protein within 30 minutes after usual physical activity. Participants were not instructed to increase their activity. [16]

**Its clearest result was faster walking.** The PROT group completed 400 m 12.4 seconds faster relative to control after six months, with a 95% confidence interval of 2.9–21.8 seconds faster. Leg-extension strength also improved. Other secondary outcomes, including body-composition measures, did not show significant intervention effects, illustrating that functional improvement need not coincide with measurable lean-mass gain. [16]

**Extra timing advice did not improve results.** The PROT+TIMING group’s walking difference was −4.9 seconds, with a confidence interval spanning −14.5 to +4.7 seconds; leg-extension strength increased by 24.3 N. These findings do not establish a benefit from adding a strict post-activity timing rule to the broader dietary advice. They also do not show that timing is harmful: the comparison concerns the added advice package. [16]

**Actual dietary change was meaningful but imperfect.** Relative to control, intake increased by approximately 25 g/day and 0.34 g/kg adjusted weight/day in each intervention arm. A subsequent feasibility analysis reported that 47.7% reached the 1.2 g/kg/day target at six months, while 67.1% met the high-protein-meal criterion. People with low overall food intake faced greater practical difficulty. [16][12]

**CALM measured a different biological response.** Its metabolic analysis assessed basal and protein-stimulated MPS and the muscle metabolome—the pattern of small molecules measured in muscle—after 12 months. Despite protein-group intake increases of approximately 23.9–29.0 g/day, reaching roughly 1.1–1.5 g/kg/day, these outcomes remained unchanged. This is evidence against assuming that long-term supplementation necessarily raises the MPS response measured under standardized test conditions. [21]

**A synthesis result is not functional equivalence.** CALM’s reported metabolic analysis included 66 participants, with complete sample sets for 55; per-protocol training subgroups were particularly small. Its findings should therefore remain attached to the measured metabolic outcomes, rather than being translated into a blanket conclusion about every strength, mobility or independence outcome in the wider programme. [21]

### Contrasting dose trials and the overall judgment

**Higher doses have produced opposing results.**

| Study Name | Characteristics of Study Population | Intervention Details | Key Research Findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Park et al., 2018 | Undernourished prefrail and frail older adults | Twelve weeks; 1.5 versus 0.8 g/kg/day; intermediate 1.2 g/kg/day also examined | At 1.5 g/kg/day, appendicular muscle mass and gait speed improved; handgrip strength did not |
| Bhasin et al., 2018 | Functionally limited community-dwelling men aged ≥65; mean BMI 30.3 kg/m² | Six months; controlled diets providing 1.3 versus 0.8 g/kg/day | No improvement in lean mass, strength or walking performance; fat mass decreased |

Both trials are discussed in the PROMISS report, which contrasts their populations, doses and outcomes. [16]

**Baseline need is a plausible moderator.** Park studied undernourished, frail participants; PROMISS deliberately recruited people with lower intake; CALM studied healthy older adults. These differences make correction of nutritional shortfall a reasonable explanation for some benefits. However, ProMuscle’s positive findings above an approximately 1.0 g/kg/day background show that a simple “deficient versus sufficient” distinction does not explain every result.

**Exercise strengthens the overall case.** A 2022 meta-analysis covering 74 RCTs across adult ages found a small additional lean-mass benefit from protein during resistance exercise. In older-adult studies achieving 1.2–1.59 g/kg/day, the standardized mean difference was 0.20, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.02–0.37. A standardized mean difference expresses an effect in standard-deviation units, not kilograms. [3]

**The highest category is not a prescription.** The same analysis found a lower-body-strength signal at intakes of at least 1.6 g/kg/day in adults aged 65 or older: standardized mean difference 0.55, confidence interval 0.04–1.06. That estimate came from only two studies, while handgrip findings were unclear and physical-function effects marginal. An intake category across heterogeneous trials does not establish a universal dose threshold. [3]

**A stricter review reached a cautious conclusion.** The Dutch Health Council evaluated 18 RCTs involving more than 1,300 older adults whose habitual intake averaged at least 0.8 g/kg/day. Seven trials improved at least one lean-mass measure; strength benefits appeared in three of eight exercise-context trials and one of seven without exercise. The committee judged benefits possible for lean mass and exercise-associated strength, but effects on physical performance unlikely in its selected evidence base. [1]

**The reviews answer overlapping, different questions.** A combined programme such as ProMuscle in Practice can improve outcomes even when protein’s incremental contribution remains smaller than the whole programme’s effect. Conversely, reviews requiring protein to be the only intended difference between groups address the narrower question of what extra protein adds. The useful clinical response is to measure habitual intake and functional change, rather than assume that either a supplement or a target guarantees improvement.

**Muscle-health judgment: prioritize need and loading.** Increased protein can improve selected outcomes, especially alongside resistance exercise or correction of low intake, but evidence does not support a uniform muscle-building response above 0.8 g/kg/day in all older adults.

No outcome estimates were available in this protocol; intention-to-treat and per-protocol sensitivity analyses were planned, while lack of blinding, unknown protein biological value and possibly insufficient duration limited interpretation [47].

## 3. Research on the Impact on Bone Health

### Weaver and Kemmler: complementary study designs

**Adequacy appears more defensible than protein-only treatment.** Protein supplies material for bone matrix and supports muscle, but bone mineral density, or BMD, and fracture incidence are different outcomes. BMD measures mineral within a scanned bone area; fractures additionally depend on bone structure, falls, balance and the circumstances of injury.

**The two requested studies differ fundamentally.** Weaver and colleagues’ 2021 Health, Aging, and Body Composition study examined dietary protein, BMD and fracture incidence observationally. Kemmler and colleagues’ 2020 FrOST trial randomized an exercise-and-nutrition programme in men with low muscle and bone mass.

Weaver’s study is *Effect of dietary protein intake on bone mineral density and fracture incidence in older adults in the Health, Aging, and Body Composition Study*, published in 2021. Its positive animal-protein association is cited in the 2023 pooled BMD paper. FrOST’s figures come from its final trial report. [18][22]

**Weaver contributes observational support.** Its relevance is that ordinary dietary exposure can be studied alongside bone measures and incident fractures in older people, without imposing a supplement regimen. The positive animal-protein–BMD association provides a counterexample to the assumption that animal protein necessarily damages bone. It remains an association within a dietary pattern, not an assigned protein-dose effect. [18]

**FrOST improved bone and muscle together.** Osteosarcopenia combines low bone mass with low muscle mass. In FrOST, the intervention also improved skeletal-muscle mass index and handgrip strength, with standardized effects of 1.97 and 0.87, respectively; gait velocity did not improve significantly relative to control. This joint response supports treating muscle and bone as connected clinical targets. [22]

**Its nutritional contrast was above basic adequacy.** Recorded follow-up protein intakes ranged approximately from 1.56–1.61 g/kg/day in the exercise group and 1.21–1.24 g/kg/day in controls. The study therefore does not compare higher protein with an intake of 0.8 g/kg/day. It compares supervised high-intensity training plus additional whey against a nutritionally supported, non-training group. [22]

**Randomization supports the combined programme.** Calcium and vitamin D were supplied to both groups, while exercise and protein differed together. FrOST consequently supports the package’s BMD benefit but does not separate the contribution of training from the additional protein. Its approximately 95% exercise attendance also describes a closely supported intervention, relevant when considering transfer to less supervised care. [22]

**A fracture event is not efficacy evidence.** One control participant missed the final assessment after a hip fracture. This documents a minimum control-group proportion of 1 ÷ 22 = 4.5%, a calculated lower-bound event proportion rather than a comparative fracture-risk estimate. FrOST’s principal evidence concerns BMD and sarcopenia outcomes. [22]

### The 2023 pooled analysis: positive associations, neutral interventions

**A larger analysis found source-specific associations.** Groenendijk and colleagues’ 2023 study combined data from four older-adult trials. Its cross-sectional sample included 1,570 participants, with median age 71 years and approximately 56% women; median protein intake was 1.03 g/kg/day. Total and animal protein were positively associated with total-body and spine BMD, whereas plant protein showed inverse associations. [18]

These are separate statistical models, not additive treatment effects. [18]

**Calcium and vitamin D changed the interpretation.** Among participants consuming more than 950 mg calcium/day and having serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D above 50 nmol/L, total-protein associations strengthened and the inverse plant-protein associations became non-significant. This pattern makes the accompanying nutrient package important: a protein-source association should not automatically be interpreted as an intrinsic effect of that protein. [18]

**The intervention results were different.** In the longitudinal sample of 340 older adults, protein supplementation, alone or combined with resistance exercise, did not significantly improve total-body or spine BMD over 12 or 24 weeks. A 24-week protein-plus-exercise contrast of +0.006 g/cm² in total-body BMD lost significance after correction for multiple comparisons. [18]

**Time and skeletal site both matter.** Bone adaptation is slower than many strength responses, so a short intervention can miss a developing effect. Total-body BMD may also be less responsive to a targeted loading programme than particular weight-bearing sites. FrOST’s 18-month hip and spine findings are therefore informative—but duration is not a complete explanation for every neutral protein trial.

**A longer protein trial was also neutral.** Zhu and colleagues randomized 219 healthy ambulant women aged 70–80 years to a higher-protein drink or control for two years. Final intake was approximately 1.4 versus 1.1 g/kg/day, without significant between-group differences in hip or femoral-neck BMD. Two-year hip changes were −10.8 versus −8.2 mg/cm², respectively, with p=0.46. [23]

**The broader BMD judgment remains mixed.** The 2023 umbrella review found no consistent higher-protein benefit for total-body or total-hip BMD. Lumbar-spine evidence included modest positive findings in some analyses, including a four-RCT synthesis with 322 participants, but inconsistent results across reviews. Thus, the positive FrOST result should influence practice as evidence for its combined intervention, not erase neutral protein-only comparisons. [4]

### Fracture risk: the strongest quantitative association

**Hip-fracture associations are moderately favorable.** A 2019 systematic review restricted to adults aged at least 65 years included 12 prospective cohorts and one RCT. Pooling four cohorts produced a hip-fracture hazard ratio of 0.89, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.84–0.94, for higher versus lower protein intake. This corresponds to an approximately 11% lower relative hazard, not an 11-percentage-point absolute reduction. [23]

**This is a synthesis-level benchmark.** The estimate describes hip-fracture associations across cohorts; it is not a Weaver-specific effect or a randomized treatment result. The 2023 umbrella review’s discussion of an approximately 11% reduction above 0.93 g/kg/day summarizes that observational evidence rather than providing an independent replication or a universally protective cutoff. [4]

**Not every population shows the same pattern.** The 2020 Mr. OS and Ms. OS Hong Kong analysis included 2,997 older adults and fracture follow-up with a median of 9.6 years. Higher total protein was associated with lower major osteoporotic-fracture risk in men—hazard ratio 0.65 per standard-deviation increase—but not women, whose estimate was 1.04. Major osteoporotic fractures included hip, clinical-spine, wrist and humerus fractures. [24]

**Fractures integrate more than bone density.** Preserving muscle strength may reduce fall vulnerability even when BMD changes little. Conversely, greater BMD does not guarantee fewer fractures if balance, vision, medications or environmental hazards remain unfavorable. The observational fracture signal and mixed BMD trials can therefore coexist without being logically contradictory.

### Mechanisms and practical meaning

**Protein has several plausible supporting pathways.** It supplies amino acids for bone-matrix proteins and may increase intestinal calcium absorption and insulin-like growth factor 1, or IGF-1. By preserving muscle, it can also sustain mechanical loading of bone. These mechanisms explain why protein adequacy belongs in musculoskeletal care. [25]

**Urinary calcium does not prove bone loss.** Higher protein can increase urinary calcium, but increased intestinal absorption may contribute to that excretion. The older acid-load argument—more protein necessarily drawing calcium out of bone—therefore does not by itself establish skeletal harm. Net interpretation requires calcium intake, vitamin D status and measured bone outcomes. [23]

**Clinical emphasis should remain comprehensive.** For an older adult at skeletal risk, adequate protein should accompany calcium and vitamin D adequacy, appropriate resistance exercise and attention to falls. A protein-specific bone claim would require a benefit persisting when exercise and accompanying nutrients are comparable; the strongest positive trial here establishes the value of their combination.

**Bone-health judgment: adequacy is part of care.** Higher protein is associated with fewer hip fractures in cohort evidence, while randomized evidence supports some combined nutrition-and-exercise programmes more clearly than protein alone.

Bone health is a core outcome of protein intake in older adults: adequate protein helps preserve bone and muscle mass with ageing, and WHO describes 0.9–1.1 g/kg per day as beneficial for healthy older people [87] [85]. However, the review judged the protein–bone relationship inconclusive, so this range is nutritional context rather than proof of a bone-specific target [94].

Protein intake is potentially beneficial rather than inherently harmful for older-adult bone health: higher intake is associated with higher BMD and slower bone loss, and one synthesis reported an 11% lower hip-fracture risk when calcium intake was adequate [87] [106]. Proposed mechanisms are improved calcium absorption, parathyroid-hormone suppression, increased IGF-1, and lean-mass gain, but heterogeneous evidence leaves the appropriate higher dose uncertain [136] [118] [138].

Permissible evidence supports a qualified, small positive protein–bone relationship rather than demineralization: higher protein showed a small lumbar-spine BMD benefit, and an 18-month combined resistance-training/whey intervention produced BMD SMD 0.72; higher intake did not appear to demineralize bone when calcium intake and kidney function were adequate [135] [53] [138]. Yet fracture evidence is exclusively observational and umbrella-review certainty for total, animal, and plant protein effects is insufficient [70] [134], so protein should be treated as supportive nutrition rather than a stand-alone bone therapy.

Applicability is mainly to healthy, free-living older adults in Nordic-like settings: studies commonly use ≥65 years, frail or disabled people are often excluded, and convenience samples may be healthier than the source population [104] [90] [91]. Evidence also spans healthy, overweight or obese, postmenopausal, and older-men populations, with reported protein exposures of 0.78 to 1.55 g/kg; recommendations distinguish healthy older people from weak or ill people who eat little, so age, illness, frailty, setting, and exposure differences limit generalization [117] [89] [40].

Bone mineral density (BMD) findings in older adults are site-specific: higher protein did not affect total-body or total-hip BMD, and lumbar-spine findings were inconsistent [70]. Meta-analytic mean differences were MD(fixed)=0.04 g/cm2 (0.00 to 0.08) and MD(random)=0.01 g/cm2 (-0.03 to 0.05), while pooled net changes were 0.52%, 0.30%, and −0.14% [139] [83]; a 13-week intervention reported 0.02 g/cm2 (~2%) [112], indicating a small, non-uniform BMD signal rather than a general bone benefit.

Fracture risk is inconsistent: one synthesis reported an 11% lower hip-fracture risk with higher protein [73], whereas another found no relation between higher and lower intake and hip fracture; pooled estimates included RR 0.94 (0.72 to 1.23) and RR 0.98 (0.76 to 1.27) [83] [139]. Because fracture evidence is observational and included RCTs reported no fracture outcomes [100], protein should not be treated as a stand-alone fracture-prevention intervention; use it as part of overall bone-health care while awaiting long-term causal evidence.

## 4. The Importance of Protein Quality

### Animal and plant proteins: useful differences, not absolute categories

**Protein quality concerns usable amino acids.** Essential amino acids must come from the diet. A protein’s quality depends on their amounts and proportions, together with digestibility and bioavailability—how effectively they become available for body-protein synthesis. Food structure and preparation also influence that delivery. [26]

| Characteristic | Animal proteins | Plant proteins |
|---|---|---|
| Common sources | Dairy, eggs, fish, meat and poultry | Legumes, soy, cereals, nuts and seeds |
| Essential-amino-acid pattern | Generally favorable and well balanced | Variable; complementary sources improve balance |
| Digestibility | Generally high | More variable; food structure and inhibitors matter |
| Typical leucine proportion | Approximately 8.5–10% of protein | Approximately 6–8% of protein |
| Processing and food form | Whey, casein and whole foods differ | Cooking, isolates and blends can improve delivery |
| Accompanying nutrients | Dairy can supply calcium; animal foods commonly supply B12 | Legumes and grains supply fibre; fortification can protect displaced nutrients |

These are general patterns, not properties shared identically by every food. Soy and potato are examples of relatively good-quality plant proteins. [7][26]

**A limiting amino acid is relative.** Cereals can be relatively low in lysine, while some legumes have a less favorable sulfur-amino-acid pattern. This does not mean plant foods contain no essential amino acids; it means their proportions may fall short of the reference pattern for efficient use. Combining legumes and cereals can improve the overall pattern. [10]

**Quality scores measure nutritional properties.** The protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score, or PDCAAS, combines amino-acid composition with digestibility. The digestible indispensable amino acid score, or DIAAS, uses digestibility at the end of the small intestine rather than faecal digestibility and is not capped at 100. Illustrative DIAAS values reported for whey isolate and concentrate are 100 and 107, respectively; those scores are not percentages of a dose converted into muscle. [26][5]

PDCAAS is a separate digestibility-corrected scoring method based on amino-acid composition and faecal digestibility; DIAAS uses ileal digestibility and does not truncate scores at 100%.

**Food portions reveal the density challenge.** FSAI’s report gives the following examples, using its listed portion weights and food-composition values. The score pairs are expressed on the report’s 100-point scale. [11]

| Listed food portion | Protein, g/portion | Leucine, g/portion | DIAAS / PDCAAS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beef, 100 g | 33.9 | 2.9 | 91 / 95 |
| Red split lentils, 144 g | 10.9 | 0.9 | 50 / 54 |
| Kidney beans, 120 g | 10.3 | 0.7 | 59 / 65 |

**The lesson concerns meal construction.** These are different-sized food portions, not matched protein doses. A lentil-based meal can still deliver substantial protein, but may need additional legumes, soy foods, grains or a concentrated protein ingredient to provide the same total protein and essential-amino-acid supply. This is especially relevant when appetite limits the amount of food an older person will eat.

### Why older adults need special attention during a plant-based transition

**Anabolic resistance makes small servings consequential.** Acute studies estimate approximately 0.4 g protein/kg per meal to maximize MPS in older adults, compared with approximately 0.24 g/kg in younger adults. For a 70-kg person, the calculated older-adult amount is 0.4 × 70 = 28 g per meal. This explains interest in strengthening breakfast and lunch rather than concentrating almost all protein at dinner. [14]

**Daily adequacy still comes first.** Practical reviews commonly discuss approximately 25–30 g high-quality protein per meal and leucine amounts around 3–4 g. These are related planning concepts, not universally interchangeable quantities: leucine content varies by source, and an acute MPS target is not itself a daily requirement or a guaranteed long-term functional benefit. [27]

**Composition explains some additional serving needs.** Using 3 g leucine as an illustrative meal-design target, the calculated composition estimates are 3 ÷ 0.08 to 3 ÷ 0.06 = 37.5–50 g plant protein, versus 3 ÷ 0.10 to 3 ÷ 0.085 = approximately 30–35 g typical animal protein. These calculations use the reported leucine proportions; they are not clinical dose-response results or a universal plant-protein correction factor. [7]

**Acute source comparisons favor some animal proteins.** Berrazaga and colleagues’ 2019 review summarizes older-men studies in which a given quantity of soy or wheat protein produced approximately 30–40% lower MPS than whey isolate or micellar casein. Larger plant-protein doses or matching relevant amino-acid delivery can reduce the difference. The mechanism concerns the amino acids reaching muscle, rather than the plant label alone. [28]

**Longer training comparisons are more reassuring.** A 2021 synthesis reports no consistent animal-versus-plant difference in resistance-training gains when protein provision is sufficient. Hevia-Larraín and colleagues likewise found comparable muscle-mass and strength gains with exclusively plant-based and omnivorous diets at approximately 1.6 g/kg/day, supported by soy or whey isolates. That study supports the feasibility of well-supplied plant-based eating, while representing a different setting from low-intake frail older adults. [29]

**Plant-based promotion should preserve nutrient density.** Practical approaches include protein-rich soy foods, adequately sized legume portions, complementary cereal–legume combinations, and blends or fortified products when ordinary food volumes become difficult. Cooking can improve legume-protein digestibility, while softer or liquid formats can help people with chewing difficulties. Source, preparation and texture should therefore be considered together. [7]

**The wider nutritional trade-off matters.** Nordic dietary-replacement research found that increasing plant foods could improve fibre and unsaturated-fat intake and reduce LDL cholesterol, while lowering vitamin B12 and iodine intake. Bone-turnover markers also changed in some replacement studies. Such marker changes are not observed fracture outcomes, but they reinforce the need to preserve calcium, vitamin D and other nutrients when animal foods are displaced. [26]

**The appropriate conclusion is conditional equivalence.** Animal protein can be an efficient way to meet a target within a small meal; plant protein can also meet the goal when quantity, amino-acid pattern, digestibility and accompanying nutrients are maintained. Older adults with low appetite require the most careful transition because substituting equal food volumes does not necessarily preserve protein delivery.

### Essential amino acids: different levels of evidence

**The three amino acids are not interchangeable.**

| Amino acid | Muscle-health evidence | Bone-health evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Leucine | Strong acute signaling evidence; inconsistent long-term isolated-supplement benefits | Plausible indirect support through muscle and IGF-1 pathways |
| Valine | Essential building block; circulating-level association with sarcopenia in sensitivity analysis | Association with less hip-BMD decline; fracture association non-significant |
| Tryptophan | Circulating-level associations and experimental signaling evidence | Higher circulating concentration associated with lower major-fracture hazard |

The muscle comparisons draw on supplementation and metabolomics reviews; the bone findings include the prospective Hong Kong cohort. [5][30][24]

### Leucine: an anabolic signal requiring complete nutrition

**Leucine has the clearest muscle mechanism.** It helps activate mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1, or mTORC1, a cellular system involved in initiating protein synthesis. However, switching on synthesis does not provide every amino acid needed to build protein. Leucine-rich complete protein or essential-amino-acid mixtures therefore have a stronger nutritional rationale than treating leucine as a substitute for adequate protein. [5]

**Short-term stimulation does not guarantee growth.** In older adults consuming approximately 0.81 g protein/kg/day, 4 g leucine with each of three daily meals for two weeks improved measured MPS. By contrast, trials supplying 7.5 g leucine/day for three months in healthy older men or six months in older men with diabetes did not improve muscle mass or strength. The results distinguish an acute biological response from sustained clinical benefit. [5]

**Bone benefits remain mainly indirect.** Preserving muscle could support bone loading, and branched-chain amino acids have proposed links with IGF-1 signaling. In the Hong Kong cohort, leucine’s association with less hip-BMD decline approached borderline significance, while its fracture association was non-significant. These findings support studying the muscle–bone pathway, rather than prescribing isolated leucine as an osteoporosis treatment. [4][24]

### Valine: an essential nutrient and possible biomarker

**Valine is required for protein synthesis.** Like leucine and isoleucine, it is a branched-chain essential amino acid. Its necessity as a building block does not establish that additional valine supplementation improves muscle once the overall amino-acid supply is adequate.

**Its muscle association depends on analysis.** Dai and colleagues’ 2021 meta-analysis included six cross-sectional studies with 1,120 adults aged at least 50 years. Valine became significantly lower in people with sarcopenia when analysis was restricted to studies measuring muscle mass by bioelectrical impedance: standardized mean difference −0.294, confidence interval −0.469 to −0.118. This is a circulating biomarker association, not a trial of dietary valine. [30]

**The bone signal is similarly specific.** Higher baseline circulating valine was associated with lower odds of substantial hip-BMD decline over four years in the Hong Kong cohort, but not significantly with major osteoporotic-fracture risk over approximately ten years. Potential explanations include overall nutritional status, amino-acid metabolism and muscle-related loading; the association does not identify which pathway produced it. [24]

### Tryptophan: promising associations without a supplement prescription

**Tryptophan has plausible muscle-related pathways.** Experimental work links it with serotonin-related physiology and IGF1/p70S6K/mTOR signaling, and with expression of genes involved in muscle development. The human meta-analysis found lower circulating tryptophan in sarcopenia, with a standardized mean difference of −0.923 and substantial between-study heterogeneity. [30]

**Its fracture association is quantitatively favorable.** In the Hong Kong cohort, each one-standard-deviation higher circulating tryptophan concentration was associated with a major osteoporotic-fracture hazard ratio of 0.86, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.75–0.98, over median follow-up of 9.6 years. Proposed pathways include IGF-1 and calcium homeostasis, but the exposure was circulating concentration—not an assigned dietary or supplement dose. [24]

**Blood concentrations are not intake targets.** They reflect absorption, metabolism, inflammation and tissue uptake as well as food intake. The metabolomics review itself describes circumstances in which higher circulating amino acids might reflect impaired muscle uptake. Consequently, neither low nor high blood concentrations can be translated directly into a recommendation to take an isolated amino acid. [30]

**Historical requirement estimates answer another question.** The August 1981 consultation background reported plasma-response-curve inflections for tryptophan around 2 mg/kg/day in older subjects versus 3 mg/kg/day in younger subjects, while characterizing ageing amino-acid requirement evidence as fragmentary and contradictory. Those metabolic observations are distinct from doses intended to preserve strength, raise BMD or prevent fracture. [15]

**The evidence has defined measurement boundaries.** This report uses guidance and studies published through 2023. Daily intake refers to actual body weight unless adjusted weight is specified for PROMISS; labelled reference-weight and meal-prescription calculations are estimates, not achieved trial intakes. BMD associations, standardized effects and fracture hazard ratios measure different quantities. Cohort associations, combined interventions and acute synthesis studies support different causal conclusions, with the least direct clinical evidence applying to isolated valine and tryptophan supplementation.

**Protein-quality judgment: preserve the whole supply.** Older adults benefit from attention to sufficient total protein, usable essential amino acids and bone-related nutrients when diets change; leucine helps explain meal design, while valine and tryptophan remain biological clues rather than established stand-alone treatments.

Protein quality combines indispensable-amino-acid profile, digestibility, absorption and cellular roles; PDCAAS is determined by the first limiting amino acid and corrected for true digestibility [152]. Fast, leucine-rich whey sometimes yielded greater short-term postprandial accretion than casein or soy, but casein and soy did not differ acutely; this does not prove animal-protein superiority [157].

Leucine can acutely stimulate older-adult muscle-protein synthesis; a cited meal threshold is 25–30 g protein plus 2.5–2.8 g leucine, yet long-term leucine alone did not consistently improve mass or strength [170,172]. A 24-week, 150-g/day multi-nutrient preparation in 65 postmenopausal patients aged 50–60 improved bone-metabolism markers, but not an independent leucine effect [174]. Valine was more abundant in older sedentary than young active muscle and related to kynurenine metabolism; tryptophan-pathway metabolites tracked age, activity and muscle energetics, but these observational findings showed no dietary or bone benefit [161].

Protein quality is digestibility plus EAA profile; PDCAAS/DIAAS <100 signals a limiting amino acid [64]. Animal foods generally provide all nine EAAs and more leucine; plant proteins may be less balanced/digestible, so combine sources and distribute protein [64]. In older adults, 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day is advised; leucine may stimulate MPS, while valine and tryptophan are EAAs without separate muscle/bone evidence [153][64].

Protein quality matters because total protein grams do not reveal the digestible, absorbable indispensable-amino-acid supply; it includes amino-acid composition, digestibility, bioavailability and meal context and can be assessed with PDCAAS or DIAAS [179][181][182][155]. For older adults, higher-quality protein containing all essential amino acids supports muscle-protein synthesis, preservation of muscle mass and strength, while animal-protein digestibility is reported as >90% [176][160]; the supplied evidence is less direct for a distinct quality-specific bone effect [184].

Older adults require special attention because ageing causes anabolic resistance and lower energy intake, while appetite, chewing and swallowing problems can further reduce protein consumption [149][159][40][37]. Guidance cited here gives 1.0–1.2 g/kg/d and about 0.4 g/kg bw/meal [187][155]; in plant-based patterns, lower protein and amino-acid availability can make adequacy harder, so source variety and monitoring are important [190][185].

Animal proteins generally provide higher digestibility and essential-amino-acid and BCAA content, including leucine, isoleucine and valine; digestibility coefficients are reported as >90%, supporting a stronger anabolic signal for muscle [156][172][160]. They are therefore convenient for older-adult muscle protein delivery, but an animal-specific bone or long-term muscle advantage is not established by composition alone: a nitrogen-balance meta-regression found no difference in slope or intercept among animal, vegetable and mixed proteins [185].

Plant proteins can contribute to older-adult diets, but they often provide less leucine and other indispensable amino acids and have lower or more variable digestibility, partly because fibre and antinutritional factors can limit digestion and absorption [156][186]. One comparison reported 11% lower EAA content and 7.1% ± 0.8% leucine content for plant protein [175]; complementary legumes, cereals, nuts and seeds can address limiting amino acids, while plant foods may also provide vitamins, minerals and antioxidants [181][188][169].

Plant-based diets merit extra attention because ageing increases splanchnic amino-acid sequestration and reduces peripheral availability, while appetite, illness and social factors can lower intake; replacing animal foods with plant foods often lowers total protein and may provide less digestible indispensable amino acids [156][154][184]. Planning should therefore address amount, distribution across complementary sources, digestibility and food selection—an older-adult range cited here is 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day rather than the general 0.8 g/kg/day—and include nutrient-dense variety and monitoring; varied vegetarian diets can still meet protein needs when energy needs are met [150][162][181][158][159].

Leucine is an essential BCAA signal nutrient that activates mTORC1-related translation and stimulates muscle-protein synthesis, which is relevant because older muscle is less responsive to dietary protein [151][170][148]; reported older-adult meal amounts are 2.0–2.5 g or 2.5–2.8 g [152]. Its bone relevance is preliminary: leucine appears among metabolites associated with low BMD and in a BCAA pathway that may affect bone, so this suggests a potential mechanism rather than a proven bone benefit [178].

Valine, an essential BCAA, may support muscle through BCAA-related signalling and may affect bone through LAT1 transport and mTORC1; higher circulating valine was positively associated with grip strength, walking speed, BMD and bone microstructure, whereas low valine was associated with higher hip-fracture risk [147][177][166]. The reported risk estimates were HR 0.79 (95% CI 0.73–0.84) and OR 0.84 (95% CI 0.80–0.88) per SD increase [177], but these are mainly observational or mechanistic associations, and human evidence gives stronger support to leucine than valine [147].
