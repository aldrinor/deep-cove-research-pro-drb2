# Cislunar Exploration Through May 2024: National Programs, Commercial Services, and Emerging Hotspots

**The surge is becoming an infrastructure campaign.** By 31 May 2024, lunar exploration was expanding from individual spacecraft missions into connected systems of landers, rovers, sample-return vehicles, communications relays, commercial deliveries, and planned human habitats. The United States had completed Artemis I and was developing a crewed exploration architecture; China had demonstrated robotic landing and sample return and launched Chang’e-6 toward the lunar far side; Russia was rebuilding its Luna campaign after Luna-25’s failure. NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services initiative had produced its first two flights: Astrobotic’s unsuccessful Peregrine delivery and Intuitive Machines’ successful but operationally constrained IM-1 landing. [1][2], and NASA’s CLPS record [3] establish these different stages of progress.

**The principal hotspots are linked.** On the surface, the lunar South Pole attracts missions because potentially volatile-bearing cold traps lie near terrain offering useful illumination, scientifically valuable geology, and possible operating sites. Around the Moon, near-rectilinear halo orbit, low polar orbits, and communications-relay orbits support different parts of the emerging exploration network. NASA’s south-polar rationale [4] and Gateway orbit analysis [5] explain why surface geography and orbital infrastructure must be considered together.

**The report follows four questions.** What had actually flown during the global mission window of **1 January 2001–31 May 2024**, including lunar-surface activity and near-Moon orbits? How do the leading national programs intend to establish lasting capabilities? What do commercial delivery and water-prospecting missions contribute? Finally, which future missions and locations were shaping the next phase?

## Scope and the meaning of the recent surge

**The important change is functional diversity.** A lunar orbiter that maps terrain, a relay that connects the far side to Earth, a rover that investigates subsurface hydrogen, and a spacecraft that transports astronauts perform different but complementary jobs. This report therefore includes dedicated lunar exploration, relevant Earth–Moon trajectories, lunar orbiters, surface missions, sample return, relay spacecraft, and infrastructure intended to support sustained operations. It does not treat every spacecraft passing the Moon on its way elsewhere as a central lunar exploration mission.

**Several motivations reinforce one another.** Kaitlyn Johnson’s 2022 CSIS assessment identifies scientific and economic potential, domestic space-industrial development, international partnerships, national prestige, and strategically useful locations as drivers of national and commercial investment. Prospective resources add another incentive, particularly where local materials might support future exploration rather than having to be transported from Earth. CSIS’s worldwide assessment [6] provides this broader explanation.

**Recent activity illustrates that widening portfolio.** The opening months of 2024 included Peregrine’s launch, SLIM’s landing, IM-1’s launch and landing, Queqiao-2’s deployment, and Chang’e-6’s launch. These were not repetitions of one mission type: they represented commercial delivery, precision landing, communications infrastructure, and robotic sample-return development. NASA’s mission chronology [7] and the 2024 launch record [8] document the breadth of this activity.

**Status matters as much as ambition.** Throughout the report, “completed” means a flight or defined campaign had ended; “operating” means hardware was already performing its mission; “launched” means the spacecraft had departed Earth but had not necessarily achieved its principal objective. Failed delivery, constrained operations, advanced preflight preparation, and longer-term program planning are distinguished explicitly. Chang’e-6, for example, had reached lunar orbit by the cutoff, while its surface sampling and Earth return remained future operations.

**Sustained presence means repeatable capability.** It need not initially mean continuous human occupation. Long-lived robotic systems, dependable communications, repeated transport, reusable mobility, power provision, and periodically occupied habitats can all contribute to sustained lunar activity. The transition becomes substantial when successive missions can use and extend earlier capabilities instead of rebuilding every necessary service for each flight.

**The sequence shows accumulating capabilities.** Early-century orbital reconnaissance established the terrain, composition, gravity, and environmental knowledge needed for more demanding missions. Later flights added roving, farside communications, lunar ascent, orbital docking, sample transfer, precision landing, and commercial delivery. Success is therefore better understood as a growing set of operational capabilities than as a simple count of landers.

**Failures remain part of that record.** Lunar Flashlight did not reach its intended prospecting orbit; Luna-25 failed before surface operations; Peregrine did not land; and IM-1’s orientation restricted its operations after touchdown. These outcomes distinguish successful launch, successful arrival, successful landing, and successful execution of an entire payload program—milestones that should not be treated as interchangeable.

## The United States: Artemis and an integrated exploration architecture

### Objectives and achievements through May 2024

**Artemis seeks sustained exploration, not one return.** NASA’s stated objectives combine returning astronauts to the Moon, conducting science, establishing a foundation for long-term lunar operations, and testing systems and operating practices needed for eventual human missions to Mars. Its architecture joins government transportation systems with commercial landers, robotic deliveries, international infrastructure, spacesuits, and surface mobility. NASA’s sustained-presence plan [13] describes this combined purpose.

**Artemis I validated the transportation backbone.** Launched on 16 November 2022 and recovered on 11 December, the uncrewed mission was the first integrated flight test of the Space Launch System, Orion, and Exploration Ground Systems. Its duration was 25 days, 10 hours, and 53 minutes, commonly rounded to **25.5 days**. Orion reached 268,563 miles from Earth and traveled 43,471 miles beyond the Moon’s far side. NASA’s Artemis I record [14] supplies these flight results.

**The engineering return was substantial.** NASA’s postflight analysis recorded 161 Orion test objectives accomplished, including 20 added during flight. The European-built service module generated approximately 20% more power than initially expected and consumed about 25% less than predicted. These results supported the underlying spacecraft design and provided operating margins relevant to later missions. NASA’s postflight assessment [15] reports those findings.

**Successful testing still exposed development work.** Orion’s heat-shield material wore away differently from predictions, and engineers investigated uncommanded openings of electrical current-limiting devices. Ground systems also sustained launch damage requiring repair and modification. These were concrete reasons to assess crew readiness separately from the successful completion of the uncrewed flight. NASA’s engineering review [15] describes the continuing investigations.

**The cutoff schedule separated three next steps.** NASA’s January 2024 update targeted September 2025 for Artemis II, September 2026 for Artemis III, and 2028 for Artemis IV. Artemis II was the first planned crewed Artemis flight around the Moon; Artemis III was intended to land astronauts near the South Pole; Artemis IV was associated with the first crewed Gateway mission. These were the relevant dated agency targets for this report. NASA’s 2024 schedule announcement [1] states all three.

### How the transportation and landing systems fit together

**SLS and Orion handle Earth–Moon transport.** SLS supplies the launch and departure capability, while Orion provides the crew spacecraft for travel to the lunar vicinity and return to Earth. This division does not make Orion a lunar lander: a separate Human Landing System, or HLS, is required to move astronauts between lunar orbit and the surface. NASA’s Artemis architecture [16] identifies these distinct vehicle roles.

**Artemis III did not require Gateway first.** NASA’s site-selection architecture described Orion rendezvousing directly with SpaceX’s Starship HLS in near-rectilinear halo orbit. Two astronauts would descend to the surface while the other two remained in Orion. Gateway was intended to expand the architecture for subsequent missions, rather than being a prerequisite for the initial Artemis landing. NASA’s mission-availability analysis [17] and ESA’s mission description [18] explain this arrangement.

**The landing mission adds new dependencies.** A crew-capable lunar lander, suitable spacesuits, rendezvous and docking operations, surface communications, and an acceptable landing opportunity all have to function together. NASA’s April 2024 status report described analysis of the route from NRHO to low lunar orbit and then the surface, including abort availability and maneuver margins. That is a more demanding integration problem than repeating Artemis I with astronauts aboard. NASA’s Moon-to-Mars status report [19] records this development work.

**Site selection is an end-to-end decision.** A scientifically attractive location must also provide an acceptable landing area, illumination during the expedition, communications access, and a workable return opportunity. NASA’s architecture analysis used approximately 6–6.5 days of sunlight as an initial landing-site requirement. Consequently, a desirable crater or ridge cannot be selected solely because orbital data suggest nearby volatiles. NASA’s lunar site-selection paper [17] connects these operating constraints.

### Gateway, surface mobility, and longer stays

**Gateway adds a reusable orbital destination.** The NASA-led station was planned as a staging, communications, science, and crew-support platform in lunar orbit, with a minimum design lifespan of 15 years. Its initial elements were the Power and Propulsion Element, or PPE, and the Habitation and Logistics Outpost, or HALO. PPE was designed as a 60-kilowatt solar-electric spacecraft providing power, communications, attitude control, orbit maintenance, and transfer capability. NASA’s Gateway description [20] sets out these functions.

**International contributions distribute major responsibilities.** Canada’s planned Canadarm3 supplies external robotics; ESA contributes habitation, communications, and refueling-related elements; Japan contributes life-support, thermal-control, battery, and logistics capabilities. In January 2024, NASA and the United Arab Emirates announced an arrangement for a crew and science airlock. These are practical contributions to station operation, not simply national flags attached to a U.S. spacecraft. Gateway’s partnership and program history [20] records the contributions.

**Gateway is crew-tended rather than continuously occupied.** Its intended operating model differs from the International Space Station’s continuous human presence. Gateway would conduct substantial portions of its mission without astronauts aboard, making autonomous operation, robotic maintenance, and reliable remote control important features. The May 2024 Gateway account [21] explains this distinction.

**The surface architecture extends expedition range.** NASA’s Artemis Base Camp concept identified three principal elements: an unpressurized lunar terrain vehicle, a habitable mobility platform for longer journeys, and a foundation surface habitat supporting short stays by four crew members. Power, communications, resource investigations, and logistics would underpin these elements. Base Camp was an architecture for progressively extending surface operations, not an installed facility at the cutoff. NASA’s sustained-presence plan [13] describes the concept.

**Mobility development had reached commercial selection.** In April 2024, NASA selected Intuitive Machines, Lunar Outpost, and Venturi Astrolab for initial lunar terrain vehicle design work. Japan’s May 2024 architecture report also distinguished the unpressurized vehicle from a Japanese crewed pressurized rover, which would provide habitation and wider-area mobility. The vehicles were intended to complement one another, including an emergency return role for the smaller vehicle. JAXA’s May 2024 report [22] describes this allocation of functions.

**Artemis’ strength creates an integration challenge.** Its combination of transportation, landers, orbital infrastructure, surface systems, commercial delivery, and international contributions offers a credible route beyond isolated visits. The same breadth means that progress depends on interfaces and readiness across multiple organizations. Artemis I established an important foundation; the decisive transition toward sustained presence would come from repeatedly connecting that foundation to reliable landings and longer surface operations.

## China: Chang’e’s progression from reconnaissance to polar infrastructure

### The completed robotic capability ladder

**Chang’e followed a deliberate three-stage progression.** China’s Lunar Exploration Program, initiated in 2004, was organized around orbiting, landing, and returning samples. By December 2020, Chang’e-5 had completed that original sequence, while earlier missions had added roving and farside operations. The next phase extended those capabilities toward polar exploration, resource-use experiments, and a proposed International Lunar Research Station. CNSA’s program description [23] and the 2024 Chang’e-7 scientific review [10] explain the progression.

**Chang’e-1 established orbital reconnaissance.** Launched in October 2007, it mapped lunar topography and composition, investigated soil depth, and observed the Earth–Moon space environment. Chang’e-2, launched in October 2010, added detailed reconnaissance relevant to landing-site selection. After lunar mapping, it demonstrated operations at Earth–Sun L2 and later flew past asteroid Toutatis, extending China’s tracking and control experience beyond the lunar campaign. The Planetary Society’s mission history [9] summarizes these achievements.

**Chang’e-3 added surface access and mobility.** Its lander and Yutu rover reached the surface on 14 December 2013, achieving China’s first soft landing on another celestial body. The mission investigated geology and shallow structure at the landing area. This moved the program from observing terrain remotely to operating instruments directly within it. Xinhua’s May 2024 history [2] records the mission’s role.

**Chang’e-4 made relay infrastructure indispensable.** Its lander and Yutu-2 rover achieved the first lunar farside soft landing on 3 January 2019, in Von Kármán crater within the South Pole–Aitken basin. Because the Moon blocks a direct Earth link from the far side, Queqiao provided the communications connection. The mission therefore demonstrated a surface-and-orbit system rather than a self-contained lander alone. CNSA’s Chang’e-4 announcement [23] describes both the landing and relay support.

**Farside operations opened several scientific opportunities.** Chang’e-4’s tasks included mineral-composition measurements, shallow subsurface investigation, terrain surveys, neutron and neutral-atom measurements, and low-frequency radio observations. These objectives illustrate why the far side matters independently of polar resources: its geology, environment, and isolation from direct terrestrial radio emissions offer different scientific opportunities. CNSA’s mission account [23] identifies these investigations.

**Chang’e-5 demonstrated the complete return chain.** In 2020 it returned **1,731 grams** of lunar material from Oceanus Procellarum. The mission required landing, sampling, ascent, lunar-orbit rendezvous and docking, sample transfer, and Earth return. Those engineering achievements were as important to later mission design as the returned material was to laboratory science. The Chang’e-7 review [10] reports the sample mass, while Xinhua’s chronology [2] describes the sequence.

### Chang’e-6’s position at the cutoff

**Chang’e-6 was launched, not yet completed.** It departed Wenchang on 3 May 2024 and entered circumlunar orbit on 8 May. Its principal objective was the first return of samples from the lunar far side. At 31 May, the achievement within this report’s boundary was successful launch and arrival in lunar orbit; surface sampling and the returned-sample result were still ahead. CNSA’s dated mission timeline [24] establishes those milestones.

**Its target was southern Apollo crater.** The planned landing area lay within the farside South Pole–Aitken basin, an ancient impact structure of major geological interest. The mission aimed to collect approximately 2 kilograms of material using both a scoop and a drill, then transfer the samples through an ascender, orbiter, and return capsule. Its planned duration was about 53 days. The Planetary Society’s April 2024 preview [25] describes the target and sampling plan.

**The scientific purpose was comparative geology.** All earlier returned lunar samples came from the near side. Material from the South Pole–Aitken region could help test interpretations of the Moon’s deep composition, its near-side/farside differences, and the timing of ancient impacts. In particular, sample dating could help distinguish an early impact spike from a more prolonged decline in impact activity. The prelaunch science account [25] explains these competing interpretations.

**Queqiao-2 supplied the enabling connection.** Launched in March 2024, the relay was intended to support Chang’e-6 and later polar missions. Its role was not incidental: telemetry and communications had to be maintained while the sampling spacecraft operated beyond Earth’s direct view. CNSA also identified retrograde-orbit control, intelligent sampling, and farside ascent as central technical challenges. CNSA’s expert explanation [26] sets out these dependencies.

**International payloads broadened the mission.** Chang’e-6 carried France’s DORN radon experiment, the Swedish-developed NILS negative-ion investigation under ESA cooperation, Italy’s INRRI laser retroreflector, and Pakistan’s ICUBE-Q satellite. Their objectives included surface–environment interactions, ranging, imaging, and related measurements. Their presence illustrates cooperation within a nationally led mission without changing its primary Chinese affiliation. The international payload description [25] identifies the instruments and purposes.

### Chang’e-7, Chang’e-8, and the long-term presence plan

**Chang’e-7 advances from access to prospecting.** Officially approved and entering implementation in 2022, it was scheduled for launch in 2026. Its architecture combined an orbiter, lander, rover, and mini-flying probe, with a preferred landing area on illuminated terrain near Shackleton crater. This combination was intended to connect regional mapping with direct investigation of difficult polar terrain. Wang and colleagues’ Chang’e-7 mission paper [10] describes the approved architecture.

**The payload suite links remote and local measurements.** The mission paper allocated 18 scientific payloads across the relay, orbiter, lander, rover, and flying probe. Orbital radar and neutron/gamma measurements would characterize terrain and prospective volatiles; the rover would investigate surface materials and gases; the flying probe’s Lunar Soil Water Molecule Analyzer would investigate water molecules and hydrogen isotopes in shadowed terrain. These were intended measurements, with instrument specifications describing design capability rather than lunar results. The payload configuration [10] explains the measurement chain.

**Chang’e-8 moves toward resource-use demonstrations.** Its proposed south-polar experiments included processing lunar regolith into construction bricks and investigating plants and microbes relevant to life-support systems. In-situ resource utilization, or **ISRU**, means using materials found at the destination rather than importing every required input from Earth. Chang’e-8’s significance lies in testing parts of that approach, not in establishing an operating lunar construction industry immediately. The April 2024 roadmap [25] describes these experiments.

**The research station is the broader destination.** China’s roadmap combined the robotic progression with a separate goal of landing humans by 2030 and developing the International Lunar Research Station with partners. Russian reporting on the bilateral project described surface and orbital facilities capable of long-duration uncrewed operation, with eventual human presence. China’s stated ambitions [25] and the May 2024 station agreement account [27] describe these complementary paths.

**China’s strongest position was robotic integration.** By the cutoff, the program had demonstrated a broad chain from orbital reconnaissance to roving, relay-supported farside operations, and sample return. Its next transition depended on turning those achievements into repeated polar operations, useful resource characterization, and durable infrastructure. The robotic record supports that direction, while the research-station and human-landing goals remained future capabilities.

## Russia: Luna’s return, failure recovery, and proposed follow-on missions

### Luna-25’s objectives and outcome

**The modern Luna program sought capability restoration.** Its intended sequence combined a polar landing, orbital mapping and relay, subsurface drilling, polar sample return, and eventual infrastructure associated with Russian–Chinese cooperation. Luna-25 was the first lunar landing attempt by modern Russia, following the Soviet program’s last lunar mission in 1976. The Russian program account [28] and mission history [9] place it in that context.

**Luna-25 reached orbit but failed before landing.** It launched on 10 August 2023 UTC—11 August in Russian local reporting—and entered lunar orbit on 16 August. An anomalous orbit-lowering maneuver on 19 August ended in a collision with the Moon, before the planned 21 August touchdown. Its primary proposed landing area was near Boguslawsky crater in the southern high-latitude region. The outcome record [12] and preflight landing-site account [29] distinguish the actual outcome from the intended destination.

**The science campaign focused on local materials.** A manipulator was intended to excavate regolith and deliver it to a laser mass spectrometer, while infrared spectroscopy would investigate material for possible water-ice signatures. Other objectives included the radiation and electromagnetic environment. These instruments were meant to characterize conditions relevant to later landers and resource investigations. The Luna-25 instrument account [29] explains the sampling design.

**Two failure descriptions address different levels.** Roscosmos head Yury Borisov reported an engine operation of 127 seconds rather than the planned 84 seconds during the maneuver. Later commission-related reporting identified abnormal onboard-control-system operation as the most likely underlying cause. The first describes the excessive burn; the second addresses why the spacecraft’s control chain may have produced it. Interfax’s incident report [30] and RIA’s commission account [31] should therefore be read together.

**Corrective work remained active in May.** A government response reported on 27 May 2024 described software and hardware modifications, revisions to device-interaction protocols, and additional checks using simulators and flight hardware. Official results of that work were expected in September 2024. This placed the program in a recovery-and-redesign phase at the cutoff. The May government response [32] records that status.

### Luna-26, Luna-27, and Luna-28

**Luna-26 supplies reconnaissance and communications.** In the dated April 2024 Russian Academy of Sciences account, it was targeted for 2027 and described as an orbiter mapping the Moon in multiple wavelength ranges. Its communications role would support later landed assets. The project description specifies a low polar phase at approximately 60–80 kilometers altitude for stereo imaging, with at least one year intended in that phase. The April program statement [33] and IKI’s mission description [34] explain the functions.

**Luna-27 is the intended drilling step.** The dated Russian schedule placed it in 2028, following Luna-26. Its central purpose was a more capable polar landing with drilling, soil-composition investigations, and assessment of conditions relevant to a future base site. In this sequence, the orbiter reduces uncertainty and provides support before the more demanding surface mission. Roscosmos-related planning [35] and the April 2024 Academy statement [33] describe that relationship.

**The two-lander alternative was still a proposal.** On 8 May 2024, TASS reported continuing discussion of Luna-27a and Luna-27b. The first would target the South Pole; after its success, the second might investigate the North Pole or far side. IKI scientific head Lev Zeleny estimated that two landers would cost 30–35% more than one. The May 2024 report [36] identifies both the proposed architecture and its conditional destination.

**Duplication does not eliminate shared faults.** Two spacecraft could improve geographic coverage and offer another landing opportunity, but identical software or hardware can carry the same failure mechanism. The practical value of duplication would therefore depend on incorporating Luna-25’s corrective lessons, rather than simply producing another copy. This is particularly important when the failure investigation concerns interactions within the control system.

**Luna-28 extends the sequence to Earth return.** Russian planning described it as a polar-regolith sample-return mission, targeted for 2030 or later. Its intended affiliation was Roscosmos with Russian scientific institutes; its mission would require polar landing, sample collection, ascent, and return to Earth for laboratory analysis. It belonged to the longer-range roadmap rather than the same readiness category as hardware undergoing final preflight tests. The Russian schedule [30] and IKI scientist’s mission sequence [29] support this distinction.

### Cooperation and the long-term presence strategy

**The former European partnership had ended.** ESA terminated bilateral cooperation on Luna-25, Luna-26, and Luna-27 in April 2022 and retrieved its hardware. The earlier European PROSPECT drill-and-laboratory concept explains the former mission’s water-investigation ambitions, but it should not be presented as a confirmed European payload on the Russian Luna-27 at the May 2024 cutoff. ESA’s cooperation statement [37] makes that institutional change explicit.

**China became central to infrastructure planning.** On 28 May 2024, Russia’s State Duma ratified the bilateral agreement for the International Scientific Lunar Station, commonly discussed within the broader ILRS initiative. The reported plan envisioned development in two stages during 2025–2035, with multiple modules, surface and orbital research facilities, long-duration robotic operation, and the prospect of human presence. The agreement report [27] describes this intended architecture.

**Coordination included specific robotic missions.** Russian and Chinese statements described a joint data-exchange center and coordination between Luna-26 and Chang’e-7. They also discussed a nuclear-power installation around 2033–2035 and further expansion after 2036. The operational logic is clear: reliable power and data links would make long-duration lunar facilities more useful than isolated scientific packages. These remained program plans rather than deployed systems. The May cooperation account [38] reports the proposed connections.

**Russia’s near-term test was execution reliability.** Its follow-on sequence remained scientifically coherent: reconnaissance and relay, then drilling, then sample return. However, Luna-25’s failure meant that restoring dependable flight and landing operations was a necessary step before the long-term station ambition could be judged by delivered capability.

## Other programs broaden the global picture

### India, Japan, Europe, and South Korea

**India connected orbital discovery with surface access.** Chandrayaan-1’s observations helped establish evidence for lunar hydration and polar water ice. Chandrayaan-2 subsequently maintained an orbital scientific mission despite the failure of its lander, while Chandrayaan-3 achieved a successful landing and rover deployment on 23 August 2023 at approximately 69.37°S, 32.35°E. India thereby became the fourth country to achieve a successful lunar soft landing. The mission inventory [7] and 2024 scientific review [10] document this progression.

**“Near the South Pole” needs geographic precision.** Chandrayaan-3’s landing was a major southern-high-latitude achievement, but its latitude was not the geographic pole at 90°S. Nor does that landing itself establish a measurement of ice within a permanently shadowed region. Its relevance is the demonstrated landing and surface-operation capability that can support progressively more demanding exploration.

**Japan emphasized reconnaissance and landing accuracy.** SELENE/Kaguya, launched in 2007, conducted mineralogical, topographic, magnetic, and gravitational observations. SLIM then demonstrated precision landing in January 2024, reaching within approximately 100 meters of its target despite an unfavorable attitude that initially constrained solar power. This addresses an important problem for science missions: landing close enough to a particular outcrop or terrain unit to investigate it directly. The orbital mission record [12] and JAXA’s SLIM program description [39] explain the different roles.

**Europe combined an independent mission with partnerships.** SMART-1 became the first European mission to the Moon, entering lunar orbit in 2004 after its 2003 launch. By the report cutoff, Europe’s broader lunar role also included Orion service modules, Gateway contributions, international instruments, and communications-related development. These contributions show that significant lunar participation does not always require a separately branded national lander. NASA’s mission chronology [7] and Gateway partnership record [20] support this view.

**South Korea established an orbital foothold.** Danuri, also called the Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter, entered lunar orbit in December 2022. Its program combined spacecraft, payload, and ground-control demonstrations with lunar science, resource-survey objectives, and topographic mapping relevant to future landing-site selection. Its position was therefore an operating reconnaissance mission, rather than a completed resource-extraction capability. Danuri’s mission description [12] identifies these objectives.

### Commercial activity beyond NASA’s delivery program

**Commercial lunar exploration was already international.** Israel’s privately funded Beresheet reached lunar orbit but crashed during its April 2019 landing attempt. Japan’s ispace launched HAKUTO-R Mission 1 in December 2022, reached lunar orbit in March 2023, and lost the lander during its April landing attempt. The UAE’s Rashid rover traveled aboard that mission and did not begin its planned surface campaign. The mission histories [12] record these outcomes.

**CLPS is therefore one commercial model.** It is a U.S. government procurement initiative within a wider international field of commercial spacecraft, privately funded projects, payload customers, and agency–industry partnerships. That distinction matters when evaluating “commercial success”: winning a NASA task order, attracting customers, reaching lunar orbit, landing, and completing payload operations are separate accomplishments.

## Commercial Lunar Payload Services: what NASA buys

### Purpose, procurement, and program scale

**CLPS purchases a complete delivery service.** NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services initiative, established in 2018, allows American companies to compete to carry scientific, exploration, and technology payloads to the Moon. The provider is responsible for payload integration, launch arrangements, its lander design, mission operations, landing, and the contracted delivery functions. NASA specifies its payloads and requirements while allowing providers to carry other customers’ payloads. NASA’s CLPS overview [3] defines the model.

**Eligibility is different from winning a mission.** NASA initially selected nine eligible vendors and added five in 2019, producing a pool of 14. Companies in that pool could compete for individual task orders; membership did not itself constitute an awarded lunar delivery. NASA used indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contracts, under which particular services were purchased through task orders. The contracting description [3] explains the distinction.

**The January 2024 snapshot shows intended scale.** NASA reported 14 eligible providers, nine task orders awarded to five providers, and more than 40 payloads planned for lunar-surface delivery during 2024–2026. The combined contract ceiling was **US$2.6 billion through 2028**. That ceiling was an upper contracting limit, not a statement that the entire amount had already been spent or that all listed payloads had flown. NASA’s January 2024 press kit [40] supplies these figures.

**NASA intended to become one customer among many.** Its February 2024 brochure described a lunar marketplace capable of supporting flights without NASA instruments on every mission. In the nearer term, the program’s deliveries would supply science, test technologies, investigate resources, and reduce risks before crewed Artemis operations. Three of the nine deliveries described in that brochure were slated for the South Pole. NASA’s CLPS–Artemis brochure [41] states these ambitions.

**The model exchanges control for experimentation.** Compared with a tightly managed government spacecraft development, buying a commercial service permits providers to make more design and operating choices. Multiple companies can pursue different vehicles and business models while NASA buys defined outcomes. NASA explicitly described the early program as a lower-cost, higher-risk approach for uncrewed missions. The January press kit [40] explains this risk posture.

### Intuitive Machines: demonstrated landing and planned polar drilling

- **Missions and award position.** NASA’s January 2024 record identified three awarded Intuitive Machines deliveries: IM-1, IM-2 carrying PRIME-1, and IM-3. These represented separate landing-technology, polar-resource, and lunar-science campaigns. The contemporary contract history [40] identifies the three awards.

- **Objectives and principal payloads.** IM-1 carried six NASA payloads addressing navigation, precision descent, propellant measurement, radio observations, plume–surface interactions, and laser-ranging markers. IM-2 was intended to carry a drill and mass spectrometer for subsurface volatile investigations; IM-3 targeted the Reiner Gamma lunar swirl and magnetic anomaly. The mission and payload plans [40] describe these roles.

- **Status at the cutoff.** IM-1’s Odysseus lander touched down near Malapert A on 22 February 2024. NASA reported approximately seven days of science and technology operations and more than 500 megabytes of science, technology, and spacecraft data downloaded by its February status update. IM-2 and IM-3 remained future flights. NASA’s IM-1 results [42] document the achieved delivery.

**IM-1 demonstrated access without perfect operations.** The lander tipped, compromising communications and some activities. NASA also reported that SCALPSS, the stereo-camera experiment intended to observe landing-plume interactions, was not successfully commanded to capture that event. Consequently, the first successful CLPS landing should not be described as every payload completing every planned objective. The NASA results account [42] makes that operational distinction.

**Its payload suite supported future landing systems.** Navigation Doppler Lidar measured velocity and altitude; Lunar Node-1 investigated navigation and communications links; the Radio Frequency Mass Gauge examined cryogenic propellant measurement. ROLSES investigated the near-surface electron environment and radio emissions, while the Laser Retroreflector Array supplied a passive location marker. These were useful Artemis precursors even though IM-1 was not a dedicated water-prospecting mission. The IM-1 payload descriptions [40] explain the six NASA investigations.

### Astrobotic: Peregrine and the planned Griffin delivery

- **Missions and award position.** Astrobotic’s first CLPS flight was Peregrine Mission One. Its larger Griffin lander was separately contracted to deliver NASA’s VIPER rover to the South Pole. NASA’s development announcement quoted approximately US$226.5 million for the VIPER delivery contract. The VIPER delivery announcement [43] describes the commercial service.

- **Objectives and principal payloads.** Peregrine’s updated NASA manifest contained five payloads: NSS, NIRVSS, PITMS, LETS, and a Laser Retroreflector Array. Its intended investigations covered volatiles, radiation, and the lunar exosphere. Griffin’s principal NASA payload was the mobile, drilling-equipped VIPER resource-prospecting rover. The updated Peregrine manifest [44] identifies the five instruments.

- **Status at the cutoff.** Peregrine launched on 8 January 2024 but suffered a propulsion problem that prevented landing. It ended in a controlled South Pacific atmospheric reentry on 18 January after 10 days and 13 hours in space. VIPER and Griffin remained in preflight development. NASA’s mission conclusion [45] records the flight outcome.

**Peregrine still produced useful instrument experience.** Four powered NASA investigations—LETS, NIRVSS, NSS, and PITMS—collected in-flight data. The fifth payload, the retroreflector, was passive and required a lunar-surface installation for its planned use. Thus, “four of five collected data” does not mean one powered instrument failed; the fifth had a fundamentally different operating requirement. NASA’s conclusion report [45] explains the result.

### Firefly Aerospace: geophysics and farside infrastructure

- **Missions and award position.** NASA awarded Firefly approximately US$93.3 million for its first Blue Ghost delivery, covering 10 investigations and technology demonstrations totaling about 94 kilograms. A second awarded mission added farside delivery and an orbital relay role. NASA’s first Firefly award [46] describes the initial contract.

- **Objectives and principal payloads.** Blue Ghost Mission 1 targeted Mare Crisium for heat-flow, regolith, dust, plume-interaction, navigation, and computing investigations. Its LISTER instrument was designed to obtain thermal measurements at approximately 2–3 meters depth; LuGRE would test reception of satellite-navigation signals at lunar distances. The payload announcement [46] describes the experiments.

- **Status at the cutoff.** Both Blue Ghost missions were future deliveries. Mission 1 appeared in the 2024 delivery plan; Mission 2 was planned for 2026, including two farside NASA payloads and a communications/data-relay satellite in lunar orbit. NASA’s January manifest [40] gives that planning position.

**Firefly illustrates the breadth of CLPS.** Its first mission was not principally an ice hunt. It addressed lunar geophysics and surface technologies, while its second connected scientific delivery with communications infrastructure. Both contribute to sustained exploration, but through different services from the polar drilling missions.

### Team Draper and the earlier Masten award

- **Team Draper’s contracted mission.** Draper’s farside delivery targeted Schrödinger Basin using the ispace-U.S. APEX 1.0 lander architecture, also described in earlier planning as Series-2. The payload objectives included long-lived seismology, heat-flow investigation, drilling, and near-subsurface electrical conductivity. It remained a future contracted mission, with 2025–2026 dates appearing across the planning descriptions. NASA’s mission manifest [40] and provider description [47] identify the mission.

- **Masten’s historical polar award.** Masten Space Systems received the earlier TO19C polar delivery assignment, intended to investigate volatile composition, regolith properties, thermal conditions, and radiation, including instruments relevant to VIPER preparation. Its XL-1 delivery project belongs to the canceled rather than flown mission line. NASA’s early task-order presentation [48] records the award and objectives; the mission inventory [12] identifies XL-1 among canceled concepts.

**The commercial record was promising but mixed.** By May 2024, NASA had evidence that a commercial provider could deliver instruments to the lunar surface, alongside a failed first delivery and several substantial missions still in development. Commercial scaling would depend on repeated operational performance and customer demand, not on the number of eligible companies or announced contracts alone.

## CLPS and lunar water: instruments, methods, and actual results

### What the water evidence already established

**Water evidence predates the commercial flights.** Chandrayaan-1 contributed spectral observations of OH/H₂O and polar ice, while LCROSS’s October 2009 impact into the permanently shadowed Cabeus region exposed water and other volatiles for observation. LRO added thermal, hydrogen, imaging, and terrain data that made polar prospecting more targeted. These earlier investigations explain why later missions were designed to characterize water at finer spatial and depth scales. The lunar-volatiles review [10] and LRO’s scientific results [11] summarize this foundation.

**A detection is not a usable reserve.** Exploration planners need concentration, depth, distribution, physical form, terrain access, and information about how material behaves during excavation. Water chemically bound in minerals, dispersed molecules on grains, buried ice, and concentrated ice deposits present different scientific and engineering problems. A hydrogen-rich patch can justify prospecting without establishing that extraction there would be practical.

**Imaging provides a useful counterexample.** Bickel and colleagues’ 2021 HORUS study processed 62 images covering 17 permanently shadowed regions in five south-polar areas. It recovered terrain features a few meters across but found no visible surface frost or obvious exposed ice patches in those observations. That result is compatible with patchy, mixed, or buried ice rather than a continuous exposed ice sheet. The HORUS study [49] explains both its terrain-imaging achievement and the interpretation of the optical result.

**Different instruments answer different questions.** Neutron measurements infer hydrogen; near-infrared spectra help distinguish chemical species; drilling establishes depth context and exposes material; mass spectrometry identifies gases released during operations. Combining them is more informative than treating all “water instruments” as equivalent detectors.

### Peregrine’s water and volatile instruments

**NSS: hydrogen inferred from neutron behavior.** The Neutron Spectrometer System flew on Astrobotic’s Peregrine, with a related instrument planned for VIPER aboard Griffin.

- **Measurement method and objective.** Two helium gas-proportional counters measured thermal and epithermal neutrons generated by cosmic-ray interactions. Hydrogen changes neutron energies, allowing the instrument to infer hydrogen-bearing material in roughly the upper three feet, or about 0.9 meters, of regolith. NASA’s NSS description [44] explains the method.
- **Result at the cutoff.** Peregrine’s NSS measured the radiation environment during flight. Its intended landed measurements of subsurface hydrogen and changes over a lunar day were not performed because the delivery failed. NASA’s January results update [50] identifies the returned observations.

**NIRVSS: identifying hydration and other volatiles.** The Near-Infrared Volatiles Spectrometer System also flew on Peregrine, with another version intended for VIPER.

- **Measurement method and objective.** A near-infrared spectrometer, context imager, and thermal-calibration sensor combine reflected-light spectra, images, and temperature information. The instrument was designed to investigate H₂O, hydroxyl—OH—and other compounds including carbon dioxide and methane. On a rover, observations could accompany traverses and drilling, including analysis of newly exposed material. NASA’s NIRVSS description [44] sets out these functions.
- **Result at the cutoff.** NIRVSS acquired images and spectra around Peregrine during flight. NASA attributed detected compounds in those spectra to lander outgassing and leaked fuel, rather than a measurement of lunar surface water. The January 2024 instrument update [50] gives this interpretation.

**PITMS: tracking gases above the surface.** The Peregrine Ion-Trap Mass Spectrometer was a NASA, Open University, RAL Space, and ESA partnership aboard Astrobotic’s lander.

- **Measurement method and objective.** Ion-trap mass spectrometry identifies gas species by mass-to-charge behavior. PITMS was intended to measure the thin lunar exosphere—the extremely tenuous population of gases above the surface—and investigate the release and movement of water, OH, noble gases, and other volatile species over a lunar day. NASA’s PITMS description [44] explains the investigation.
- **Result at the cutoff.** The instrument acquired multiple mass spectra before and after opening its protective cover, demonstrating healthy operation in space. Those observations characterized instrument performance and the spacecraft vicinity rather than the planned lunar-surface exosphere. NASA’s flight update [50] reports the result.

**Contamination is part of the measurement problem.** Peregrine’s spectra demonstrate why a chemical signal must be interpreted in its physical setting. Spacecraft outgassing, leaking propellant, and landing exhaust can introduce molecules that instruments also seek in the natural environment. Instrument survival and successful detection of compounds are valuable results, but chemical identification alone does not establish a lunar source.

**The flown manifest controls instrument attribution.** NASA’s April 2023 update moved several originally assigned instruments to later opportunities, including MSolo. Therefore, MSolo should not be counted among Peregrine’s five flown NASA science payloads merely because it appeared in earlier descriptions. The actual water-related instruments were NSS, NIRVSS, and PITMS. The revised NASA manifest [44] explicitly records the reallocations.

### PRIME-1 on Intuitive Machines’ IM-2

**PRIME-1 was a focused drilling demonstration.** The Polar Resources Ice Mining Experiment-1 was assigned to Intuitive Machines’ second delivery. In the contemporary 2024 plan, its destination was Shackleton Connecting Ridge near the South Pole. It combined the TRIDENT drill and MSolo mass spectrometer to investigate subsurface volatiles and test operating methods relevant to future resource use. NASA’s IM-2 plan [40] describes the delivery.

**TRIDENT supplies depth-resolved access.** The Regolith and Ice Drill for Exploring New Terrains uses rotary-percussive drilling to access material down to approximately one meter. PRIME-1’s operating concept called for one hole, drilled in approximately 7–10-centimeter increments, during a planned 72-hour surface campaign. Successive increments would connect observations to depth rather than mixing the entire sampled column into one undifferentiated result. Kleinhenz and colleagues’ 2024 presentation [51] describes the plan.

**MSolo measures gases released during excavation.** The Mass Spectrometer Observing Lunar Operations was intended to observe volatile gases before, during, and after drilling. Its longer analysis sequence would measure how a water signal decayed, informing interpretation of diffusion and desorption—the release of molecules from regolith grains. Those processes matter because excavation can change the sample being measured. The PRIME-1 analysis plan [51] connects the measurements to ISRU.

**The experiment was not an industrial mine.** Its “ice mining” name described a technology and prospecting demonstration. One meter-scale borehole and gas measurements could establish local conditions and test methods, but would not by themselves demonstrate a sustained extraction, purification, storage, and delivery system. At the cutoff, both drilling and lunar volatile measurements remained planned operations.

### VIPER on Astrobotic’s Griffin

**VIPER was the mobile resource-reconnaissance step.** NASA’s Volatiles Investigating Polar Exploration Rover was planned for delivery by Astrobotic’s Griffin to the Mons Mouton region near Nobile crater. Its purpose was to characterize the distribution and physical state of polar water and other volatiles across different terrain and thermal settings. NASA’s mission plan described approximately 100 Earth days of operations. NASA’s VIPER description [43] and Griffin mission plan [52] explain the campaign.

**Four instruments form one prospecting sequence.**

1. **NSS identifies promising hydrogen concentrations.** It measures neutron behavior while the rover moves, helping select places for detailed investigation.
2. **TRIDENT accesses the subsurface.** The one-meter drill retrieves cuttings from below the surface.
3. **NIRVSS distinguishes material composition.** Spectroscopy helps determine whether hydrogen-bearing material includes water, hydroxyl, or other relevant substances.
4. **MSolo investigates liberated gases.** Mass spectrometry complements the solid-material observations by assessing volatile species released near the rover and exposed cuttings.

NASA describes this coordinated sequence in its VIPER instrument overview [53].

**Mobility changes the scientific question.** A stationary lander can characterize one local environment; a rover can investigate how conditions change between illuminated ground, shadowed terrain, and different ice-stability settings. That spatial comparison is important because resource planning depends on how representative a promising measurement is beyond the immediate instrument footprint.

**PRIME-1 and VIPER offer complementary timing.** PRIME-1’s longer observation at one hole was intended to support a fuller analysis of signal decay. VIPER’s traverse and sampling schedule allowed less time at each location, so its planned interpretation would use shorter observations together with calibration and modeling informed by ground work and PRIME-1. The 2024 resource-prospecting presentation [51] explains this relationship.

**VIPER was advanced hardware, not lunar science yet.** NASA’s March 2024 update reported assembly more than 80% complete. A May project summary reported more than 90% completion and movement toward environmental testing after test-readiness work. This is stronger evidence of implementation maturity than a general roadmap announcement, while remaining distinctly preflight. The March NASA update [54] and May project summary [51] establish that status.

**CLPS had prepared the next water measurements.** By 31 May 2024, its water-specific flight record consisted of in-space instrument operations, while its principal polar drilling and mobile water-mapping investigations remained ahead. The intended advance was from evidence that water exists somewhere in a region to measurements of where it occurs, in what form, at what depth, and under what operating conditions.

## Comparing the routes toward long-term lunar presence

### Objectives and delivery approaches

**The programs pursue different immediate outcomes.** Artemis centers on restoring and extending human exploration, with robotic missions and commercial services supporting that campaign. Chang’e’s completed progression centers on robotic reconnaissance, landing, roving, and sample return, followed by polar investigations and research-station technologies. Luna’s immediate task is to restore dependable Russian lunar operations and advance toward drilling and sample return. CLPS, by contrast, is a procurement mechanism that delivers individual scientific and technological capabilities across many missions.

**Their organizational approaches also differ.** Artemis combines government crew transportation with commercial landing and surface services and substantial international contributions. Chang’e follows a staged national robotic program with selected international payloads and supporting relays. Luna links a renewed domestic robotic sequence to Chinese cooperation. CLPS distributes delivery work across competing providers while NASA remains the purchaser of defined services. The practical contrast is between managing a broad exploration architecture, building capabilities through a mission ladder, restoring a sequence after failure, and purchasing access through a supplier market.

### Demonstrated capabilities and remaining transitions

**There is no single meaningful overall ranking.** Artemis I demonstrated the integrated uncrewed SLS–Orion system; Chang’e-5 demonstrated robotic lunar ascent, docking, and sample return; IM-1 demonstrated commercial surface delivery; Luna-25 demonstrated transit and lunar-orbit access but not landing. Those are different achievements against different objectives. Ranking them simply by launch count or national prestige would obscure the operational distinctions.

**All routes require reusable supporting functions.** Reliable communications, power, navigation, thermal survival, mobility, logistics, and operating experience matter across national and commercial programs. China’s relays already demonstrated how infrastructure enables a new class of mission. Gateway was intended to extend that principle to crewed operations, while CLPS could provide repeated delivery opportunities and Russia’s Luna-26 could support later surface assets.

**Resources are an enabler, not a prerequisite for every mission.** Scientific instruments, short-duration landers, and initial human expeditions can operate with supplies brought from Earth. Local water and oxygen could become more important as operating duration and transport demand increase. Therefore, a weak result at one ice-prospecting site would change resource planning without erasing the scientific value of lunar exploration or the utility of communications and transport infrastructure.

**The comparative judgment is capability-specific.** The United States was developing the broadest explicitly integrated crewed architecture among the programs reviewed; China had demonstrated a particularly extensive robotic capability chain; Russia faced a central reliability-recovery task; and CLPS had established commercial landing feasibility without yet establishing routine delivery.

### Schedule relationships and readiness

**Gateway’s schedule exposed an integration tension.** The May 2024 account reported that the former 2025 launch target for PPE/HALO was under review. If launch occurred in December 2027, arrival would be approximately December 2028—a **conditional estimate: December 2027 plus one year**—later than the separately stated September 2028 Artemis IV target. The May Gateway schedule report [21] supplies the inputs.

**That calculation is not a revised launch forecast.** It shows why published milestones must be examined as a connected sequence. NASA said it was managing toward a more aggressive schedule than the conservative baseline; the baseline’s 70% joint confidence level concerned its cost-and-schedule assessment, not a probability that the entire Artemis campaign would succeed. Scientifically, it would complement stationary drilling and rover reconnaissance by connecting resource measurements with surface-operation technology. JAXA’s LUPEX description [39] states the planning target and purposes.

**Mission maturity matters more than calendar proximity.** VIPER’s assembly and environmental-test status, Chang’e-7’s approved implementation, and awarded commercial deliveries are tangible development evidence. A research-station roadmap or a proposed duplicate lander represents a different level of commitment. The outlook is therefore most useful as a map of capabilities and dependencies, not as an assumption that every listed flight would occur on its earliest advertised date.

## Exploration hotspots and why missions concentrate there

### The South Pole: resources, illumination, and operations

**The strongest surface hotspot is south-polar terrain.** The Moon’s low axial tilt keeps the Sun close to the horizon near the poles. Local topography consequently places some crater floors in permanent shadow while elevated terrain can receive sunlight for long intervals. This juxtaposition attracts missions seeking both cold-trapped volatiles and operating locations suitable for solar-powered hardware. NASA’s south-polar white paper [4] explains the connection.

**The attraction is proximity between unlike environments.** A prospective base needs power, communications, manageable slopes, and thermal conditions; a volatile investigation may need access to extremely cold darkness. These requirements are not usually satisfied at precisely the same spot. Mission planning therefore favors locations from which useful terrain can be reached, rather than assuming the coldest crater floor is automatically the best landing or habitation site.

**Shackleton is important but physically demanding.** The approximately 21-kilometer-wide crater has a deeply shadowed interior and illuminated portions of its rim. It is central to Chang’e-7’s preferred exploration area and appears in broader polar exploration discussions. Its steep walls and substantial depth, however, make access to the floor a separate engineering problem from landing near the rim. The Chang’e-7 site analysis [10] describes the illumination and terrain.

**Connecting Ridge links several exploration interests.** Shackleton Connecting Ridge was the planned PRIME-1 drilling destination. Connecting Ridge and Peak Near Shackleton also performed strongly in Butler and Lee’s 2024 comparison of Artemis candidate regions, which examined access to ancient basin material and ice-bearing shadowed terrain. These locations illustrate how science, resource access, and future operating concepts can converge geographically. The PRIME-1 plan [51] and candidate-region comparison [55] support their prominence.

**Science rankings are not landing selections.** Butler and Lee assessed 13 candidate regions and ranked DeGerlache–Kocher Massif highest overall on their selected scientific criteria. Connecting Ridge and Peak Near Shackleton led among the regions nearest the pole and were their leading Base Camp candidates. The study explicitly excluded mission-specific safety, engineering, and operational constraints, so its ranking should not be mistaken for NASA’s final site decision. The 2024 assessment [55] defines that comparison.

**Malapert A supplied an operating test case.** IM-1’s destination was selected partly because it offered relatively favorable landing terrain within the rugged southern highlands. Earth’s low position above the local horizon also made the site valuable for studying communications and data return under challenging polar geometry. Its importance was therefore operational as well as geographic. NASA’s landing-site rationale [40] explains the choice.

**Mons Mouton–Nobile supported mobile prospecting.** The planned Griffin/VIPER campaign targeted this region because it offered access to cold traps and different polar environments suitable for comparing volatile distribution. The central advantage was a rover-scale investigation across terrain, rather than one measurement assumed to represent the entire South Pole. The VIPER delivery plan [52] describes the location.

### Ancient farside terrain and complementary science sites

**South Pole–Aitken is a geological hotspot.** Chang’e-4’s Von Kármán site and Chang’e-6’s southern Apollo target lie within this ancient farside basin. The basin may preserve or expose material relevant to the Moon’s deeper structure and early impact history. Its attraction is principally geological and chronological, not simply proximity to polar ice. The Chang’e-6 science preview [25] explains the sampling rationale.

**A farside basin is not a polar cold trap.** The name South Pole–Aitken covers an enormous region, including locations far from the immediate pole. Chang’e-6’s sampling objective and a mission entering a permanently shadowed crater investigate different environments. Keeping those geographic categories separate prevents an overly simple account in which every southern or farside mission becomes a water-mining precursor.

**Other sites answer different scientific questions.** Mare Crisium’s planned Blue Ghost mission addressed regolith, heat flow, dust, and surface technologies. Reiner Gamma attracted IM-3 because of its bright swirl and associated crustal magnetic anomaly. Schrödinger Basin attracted Team Draper’s seismology, heat-flow, and subsurface investigations. Together these destinations show why the lunar campaign remains scientifically broader than a race for polar resources. NASA’s commercial mission descriptions [47] identify these distinct objectives.

### Near-Moon orbits: staging, mapping, and relay

**NRHO is the principal human-staging hotspot.** Gateway’s selected southern-family near-rectilinear halo orbit has an approximately 6.5-day period and a 9:2 resonance with the lunar synodic cycle. Its geometry supports Earth communications and limits long eclipses, benefiting power and thermal management. It also provides a common operating environment for Orion, HLS, and Gateway. NASA’s NRHO analysis [5] explains the selection.

**Low polar orbit serves a different purpose.** Passing over both poles while the Moon rotates underneath supports broad mapping and repeated polar observations. LRO demonstrated the value of this approach, while Luna-26’s planned 60–80-kilometer phase emphasized detailed stereo imaging. Low orbit offers close observation; NRHO offers different advantages for staging and long-duration infrastructure. LRO’s operating history [11] and Luna-26’s orbit plan [34] illustrate the contrast.

**Farside relay orbits overcome an absolute obstruction.** The Moon blocks direct Earth communications from its far side. Queqiao’s Earth–Moon L2-region operation enabled Chang’e-4, while Queqiao-2’s lunar-orbit role supported the next generation of farside and polar missions. A relay can therefore determine whether an entire class of surface mission is practical, rather than merely improving its download speed. CNSA’s relay-dependent architecture [26] demonstrates that relationship.

**Elliptical frozen orbits favor persistent coverage.** These elongated lunar orbits are designed to maintain useful geometry despite the Moon’s irregular gravity field. Placing the distant portion of an orbit over the southern region can increase the time a relay or navigation spacecraft spends viewing that area. Lunar Pathfinder and prospective navigation systems illustrate this service-oriented approach. The orbital database’s definitions and examples [56] explain the orbital principle.

**More spacecraft increase shared-service demands.** A NASA advisory committee’s 8 May 2024 report warned that Deep Space Network capability might not support the expected combination of cislunar, Lagrange-region, and deeper-space missions without expansion. This is a concrete example of the surge’s infrastructure implications: landing more spacecraft is not enough if navigation, commanding, and data return become bottlenecks. The May committee report [57] records that warning.

**Coordination will matter most at concentrated sites.** Several missions seeking illuminated ridges, access to cold traps, or favorable relay coverage may compete for operating opportunities even while contributing complementary science. Sharing location information, coordinating radio use and landing operations, and accounting for exhaust or dust effects would help preserve both safety and scientific interpretation. The Artemis Accords provide one set of civil-exploration principles, while the ILRS initiative represents another major channel of international cooperation. NASA’s Accords description [16] and the ILRS agreement account [27] establish those institutional settings.

**The evidence uses several measurement bases.** The tables select major campaigns rather than counting every spacecraft or secondary payload; grouped missions retain separate results. Sample masses describe returned material, instrument depths describe design reach, and contract figures refer to particular announcements rather than comparable lifetime mission costs. Undated project-page schedules are identified as alternative planning references, not substituted for dated 2024 announcements. The Gateway arrival example is a conditional calculation, while prospective instruments, infrastructure, and resource benefits remain distinct from measurements completed by 31 May 2024.

**The central judgment is practical.** The cislunar surge was real, global, and increasingly interconnected, but sustained presence was still being assembled from individual capabilities. Its next decisive advances would depend on repeatable landing performance, effective prospecting, reliable power, and sufficient communications—not simply more mission announcements. The South Pole and supporting orbital regimes attract so much attention because they bring those scientific, operational, and strategic requirements together.

Draper’s 2022 CP‑12 targets Schrödinger Basin in 2026 with seismometers, a heat-flow drill and conductivity probes [390]. Peregrine’s Sinus Viscositatis water searches—surface, subsurface and exosphere—beside the Gruithuisen Domes ended without a lunar landing or confirmed water detection [225][390]. Mare Crisium’s 2.5–3.3-billion-year basalts drew Blue Ghost heat-flow, plume and regolith tests; IM‑3 planned Lunar Vertex magnetic science at Reiner Gamma [390][394]. Chang’e 4’s pre-cutoff rover, radar, solar-wind and cosmic-ray work, Chang’e 6’s SPA mantle-sample aim, and Jinchan’s planned water-ice spectroscopy add far-side demand [238][403].

NRHO supports Artemis III HLS checkout and Orion docking, followed by later Gateway docking; Gateway stages lunar-orbit science [171][152]. Artemis I ran 16 November–11 December 2022 for 25 days with two lunar flybys; Artemis II was planned as a 10-day flyby no earlier than September 2025 [152][171]. Firefly’s planned CLPS‑19D Blue Ghost included 10 NASA payloads (>150 kg) at Mare Crisium and roughly 14 Earth days of lunar-night operations; its far-side follow-on included a relay [379][225]. Repeating Lagrange-point orbits offer predictable links [362]. CLPS provides rapid, affordable end-to-end delivery; a 2023 status listed 11 manifests and 8 awards, while historical selections were 9 vendors in 2018 plus 5 in 2019; the cited “13” pool is not cutoff-specific [394][153][209]. Blue Origin planned Mark 1 for 2025 (3,000 kg) and held a $3.4B Artemis V HLS contract for 2029; no fixed site is cited [200].

China’s Chang’e 1/2 orbited (2007/10), 3 landed near Sinus Iridum for geology/resource surveys (Dec 2013), 4 landed at Von Kármán (3 Jan 2019), 5 returned samples (2020), and 6 launched on 3 May 2024 (result unknown at cutoff); 7/8 were planned for 2026/28 [78][375][376]. Queqiao-1 enabled far-side relay; Queqiao-2 launched on 20 March 2024 into frozen lunar orbit for 6/7/8, the far side and south pole [402]. These link the far side, South Pole–Aitken region, south pole and L1/L2 relay regimes; 7/8 add ice, ISRU and ILRS objectives [183][402].

Russia’s Luna 25 targeted the south pole, entered orbit on 16 August 2023 and crashed on 19 August after a failed maneuver [214]; polar water was framed as a source of station water, oxygen and hydrogen fuel [190]. Luna 26 has no supported objective here; Luna 27A is listed for 2029 without one [396][214]. Luna 3 photographed the far side in 1959, while Mare Crisium records Luna 15’s 1969 crash and Luna 24’s 1976 sample return [377]. ESA flags Luna cooperation’s launch/landing dependence as a risk [163].

Moonlight focuses on south-polar, multi-site communications/navigation and Artemis compatibility; milestones were implementation in February 2024, Pathfinder in 2026, initial service in 2027 and full service in 2030 [164]. Blue Moon MK1 is described at 3 metric tons anywhere on the surface; MK2 is a four-crew/up-to-30-metric-ton cargo lander, with no named hotspot [166].

NASA CLPS: commercial integration, launch, landing and operations; 14 vendors and at least two planned deliveries per year were reported for 2021 [385]. Intuitive Machines/CP-11 targeted Reiner Gamma for its magnetic anomaly, swirl and mini-magnetosphere [388]. Firefly/Blue Ghost 1 had an approximately $93.3m award (2021) for Mare Crisium; LISTER, Lunar PlanetVac and SCALPSS were demonstrations, not reported water detections [204]. Mare Crisium also supports seismic, geodetic, heat-flow and electromagnetic measurements [367]. Peregrine was CLPS, lacked a supported specific hotspot and did not land; PITMS planned exosphere-volatile measurements [329][205].

The far side offers radio-quiet science but no Earth line of sight, requiring relay infrastructure; Firefly/Lunar Pathfinder illustrates this pairing [381]. Schrödinger Basin—about 200 miles wide near the south pole—exposes deep crust/mantle and volcanic material; Team Draper/APEX 1.0 planned geophysics and relay satellites [383][384][350]. The Gruithuisen Domes drew planned Peregrine/Lunar-VISE geology investigations [374]. NASA Trailblazer planned a 60-mile (100-km), 12-orbit/day polar mapping mission for water, minerals and temperature [387]. Low-lunar-orbit attraction is unestablished by pre-cutoff evidence; later Blue Moon power and Shackleton Connecting Ridge viewshed claims are excluded [361][410][411].

As of 31 May 2024, the South Pole was a leading hotspot: IM‑1 moved to Malapert A to test terrain, communications and low-horizon links for Artemis; NASA called it promising for sustained presence [407]. PRIME‑1/VIPER targeted water/ice, while LCROSS targeted Cabeus water vapor [156][283]; Chandrayaan‑1’s M3 detected polar water/hydroxyl signatures [61]. IM‑1 used low lunar orbit for propellant measurement; Chandrayaan‑1 mapped at 100 km [404][61]. CLPS/Team Draper CP‑12 planned three payloads for lunar orbit and far-side polar regions [389]. Gateway’s one-week NRHO combines access, fuel efficiency and continuous Earth communication; CAPSTONE was designed to characterize it [373]. L1/L2 support staging/teleoperation, but instability, fuel costs and possible far-side radio interference constrain them [227][357][360].

South Pole—cold traps/PSRs drive water-volatile searches; illumination maps power sites [398]. VIPER planned a 1‑m drill/mass spectrometer; LUPEX (JAXA–ISRO) had seven volatile-sensing instrument types [340][395]. Lunar Flashlight (11 Dec 2022) failed to reach lunar orbit/South Pole: no ice detection [83]. Intuitive Machines IM‑1 landed near Malapert A (22 Feb 2024); LRO imaged it at ~90 km (24 Feb) [302][342]. Griffin/VIPER–Mons Mouton and IM‑2/PRIME‑1–Shackleton Connecting Ridge remained planned [126][132].

NRHO/Gateway provides staging, Earth-facing communications, South-Pole coverage and lower lander cost [366][368]. Artemis II: September 2025, four astronauts/NRHO; Artemis III site unfixed [179]. Low/polar orbit supports mapping/ice proxies (Chandrayaan‑1/Danuri: ~100/100 km), with higher maintenance [62][230][366]. Trailblazer status unresolved [386]; Sinus planned [340]; a post-cutoff OIG report says Gruithuisen’s silica-rich minerals may require water [167]. Astrobotic Peregrine failed after its January 2024 leak [125]; Firefly/Blue Origin hotspot links remained unestablished [179][405].

At 31 May 2024, Artemis III targeted the lunar South Pole; site unfixed, with 13 candidate regions within 6° and planned crewed polar-volatile/PSR sampling [85][258]. CLPS tied the hotspot to polar-resource studies and Artemis preparation; the far side offered unique science but needed relay communications [370]. Gateway’s southern-L2 NRHO was planned (6.5 days), offering Earth line-of-sight, South-Pole views, eclipses, staging and transfers [393][351]. CAPSTONE completed its 18-month NRHO mission by 29 May 2024, testing navigation/Gateway operations [174]. Chang’e 7 (~2026) targeted South-Pole ice; Chang’e 8 (~2028) ISRU; Chang’e 4 first landed far-side (2019), and Chang’e 5 returned 1,731 g (2020) [100][246][235][97].

SLIM Shioli pinpoint landing [286]; Danuri 100-km PSR imaging [193]; CAPSTONE NRHO/Gateway [168]; Chang’e4 far-side landing [251]; South pole ice/light [372]

By 31 May 2024, hotspots were south-polar PSRs (possible water; amount/form unknown) [399]; Malapert A (IM-1 landed 22 Feb with six payloads) [392]; far side/SPA (Chang'e 4 landed; Chang'e 6 launched, relay-dependent) [245][287]. NRHO/Gateway: communications/operations; polar orbit: mapping [363][284]; L1/L2: transfers [298].

Using a national–commercial–international model across mission, location, objective, cadence, and presence capability, Artemis/Gateway is the longer-lived infrastructure layer—lunar-orbit/surface access, long-term human/robotic presence, and deep-space validation—whereas CLPS is the higher-cadence layer, buying rapid, affordable, frequent contractor delivery to multiple lunar sites, including water-ice searches [364][378][209][391]. This complement explains the surge: CAPSTONE shows NASA coordination/funding combined with commercial development, launch, and operations to validate NRHO/Gateway; Gateway’s planned minimum 15-year design life supports durable presence, while CLPS’s provider flexibility carries higher risk because NASA has limited oversight [169][364][209].

Artemis links the lunar South Pole—including permanently shadowed, potentially ice-bearing regions—to its permanent-base objective and Artemis Base Camp, and links Gateway to the Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbit through CAPSTONE [401][362][168]. NRHO’s surface access and fuel efficiency, plus Artemis/LunaNet communications and surface-orbital infrastructure, make the pole a resource-and-presence hotspot and NRHO an access-and-communications hotspot [373][356][362].
