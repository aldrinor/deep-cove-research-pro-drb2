# Video Activity Recognition Through Mid-2019: Techniques, Benchmarks, and Trade-offs

**Deep learning led general-purpose RGB recognition.** By June 2019, pretrained networks combining appearance, motion, and temporal modeling provided the strongest broadly established RGB-video baselines. Two-stream I3D, for example, reported 98.0% on UCF101 and 80.7% on HMDB51 with ImageNet-plus-Kinetics pretraining. Hand-crafted trajectories remained competitive and useful, while depth-based methods offered direct 3-D information and reduced sensitivity to appearance under suitable sensing conditions. [88][89]

**The choice remains application-dependent.** The review below covers publications and publicly posted methods through approximately June 2019. It presents the three requested technical catalogs, protocol-aware accuracy comparisons, and a final advantages-and-disadvantages table. Large reported percentages are interpreted alongside their datasets, inputs, pretraining, and evaluation procedures.

| Requested area | Decisive evidence | Main judgment |
|---|---|---|

| Depth Information-Based Methods | Multi-temporal DMM–Fisher: 95.97% MSRAction3D | Useful geometry under controlled sensing |
| Deep Learning-Based Methods | I3D: 98.0% UCF101; 80.7% HMDB51 | Strong pretrained RGB reference |

The iDT and I3D figures follow the configuration-specific comparison in the I3D paper; the depth and EigenJoints values come from their respective studies. [88][90][91][92][93]

**The review resolves three questions.** First, what information does each method represent, and how does it turn that information into an activity label? Second, what do the reported benchmark scores actually compare? Third, which category best fits limited training data, controlled RGB-D sensing, unconstrained RGB video, or demanding runtime constraints?

## 1. Technical Survey

**Representation and modality are different axes.** “Hand-crafted” and “deep learning” describe how a representation is constructed; “depth information” describes the input or intermediate information being used. Consequently, DMM-HOG is both depth-based and hand-crafted, while Action-Fusion is both depth-based and deep-learning-based. The tables retain the requested three-category organization and identify such hybrids through their technical descriptions. The distinction is consistent with the 2019 survey’s separation of data modalities from feature-construction strategies. [89]

**Recognition here chiefly means classification.** Most benchmark results concern a segmented clip assigned to an action category. Detecting when an action starts and ends, locating its actor, and recognizing actions continuously introduce additional requirements. Online depth methods and adaptive frame selection are included because they show how the literature was beginning to address these practical differences. [89][94]

### Hand-crafted Motion Features

**Trajectories became the strongest engineered baseline.** The progression was from local space-time detections and gradient histograms toward motion-following descriptors, camera-motion compensation, saliency weighting, and temporal aggregation over multiple lengths. These advances improved the information presented to a classifier without replacing the manually specified descriptor itself. [95][96]

**Local descriptors avoid full-body reconstruction.** A space-time interest point, or STIP, identifies a location with informative variation across image position and time. A surrounding cuboid is described using appearance or motion measurements, and many such descriptions are aggregated into one video representation. This local strategy can tolerate partial occlusion because recognition need not depend on reconstructing an entire silhouette or skeleton. [89]

**The histogram families capture different evidence.** A histogram of oriented gradients, or HOG, records local image structure; a histogram of optical flow, or HOF, records apparent displacement directions. Motion boundary histograms, or MBH, describe spatial derivatives of the horizontal and vertical flow components. MBH therefore emphasizes changes in motion rather than simply recording a large common displacement across the image. [95]

**Encoding supplies a fixed video representation.** Bag-of-words methods count assignments to a learned vocabulary of local descriptors. Fisher vectors retain information about how descriptors differ from a fitted probabilistic model, while VLAD aggregates residuals relative to vocabulary centers. These encoders are important: a comparison between two feature pipelines can reflect both their descriptors and their aggregation schemes. [103]

**Color STIPs improve photometric discrimination.** Everts and colleagues showed that discarding chromatic information can hide useful temporal changes and make detections respond unnecessarily to highlights or shadows. Their opponent-color formulations alter both detection and description, rather than merely appending a color histogram afterward. On UCF50, their best reported Color STIP configuration achieved 72.9%, illustrating a useful improvement within the sparse local-feature family. [98]

**Invariance can remove useful information.** The same study found that stronger photometric invariance did not automatically produce better recognition. Highly invariant hue-based representations suppressed disturbances but also discarded discriminative image structure. A practical lesson is that robustness should target a nuisance affecting the application; removing every appearance difference can also remove evidence distinguishing its actions. [98]

**Dense tracking changes the sampling strategy.** Sparse detectors select exceptional space-time events; dense trajectories instead follow many sampled points. Descriptor support regions move with those points, helping preserve a local motion pattern even when it changes image position. This improves coverage of an action but introduces optical-flow computation, tracking, descriptor storage, and aggregation costs. [95]

**iDT directly addresses camera movement.** Wang and Schmid match frame features using complementary SURF descriptors and dense optical flow, then robustly estimate a homography using RANSAC. They remove trajectories consistent with the estimated camera movement and compensate the flow used by motion descriptors. This distinction matters in unconstrained video: otherwise, camera movement can overwhelm the movement of the person. [96]

**Compensation is not universal motion separation.** A global homography is an effective model for substantial background motion, but it remains a model of the observed scene rather than a semantic guarantee that every retained trajectory belongs to the actor. The resulting recommendation is to use iDT as a strong engineered motion baseline, especially where camera motion is material, rather than assume that stabilization solves clutter, occlusion, and viewpoint changes simultaneously. The broader survey identifies all three as continuing difficulties. [89]

**Saliency and neighborhood methods change emphasis.** Improved Salient Dense Trajectories favor movement considered informative by the saliency model and modify vocabulary learning accordingly. TMNF instead describes relations among trajectories, addressing information that an independent-descriptor representation can neglect. Their contrast is useful: one improves which motion observations dominate the representation; the other improves how relationships between observations are represented. [97][100]

**Temporal scale is another design choice.** LSTMF pools features from blocks of different lengths because the same action can be performed at different speeds. Its experiments show why simply making every trajectory longer is not equivalent: on HMDB51, a single-length 90-frame configuration reached only 18.0% mean accuracy, whereas the multi-length representation reached 63.7%. Long blocks can become unsuitable for short clips or difficult to track, while combining lengths retains shorter informative primitives. [99]

**Trajectory-only recognition remains a counterexample.** The 2017 Trajectory-Set preprint reported 85.4% on HMDB51 while deliberately omitting appearance descriptors. That result prevents a categorical claim that learned appearance-and-motion networks always outperform hand-crafted motion. Its practical importance is as a strong reported counterexample whose comparison depends on the associated experiment, rather than as evidence that one representation must dominate every benchmark setup. [92]

**Hand-crafted does not necessarily mean cheap.** Sparse STIP pipelines can be relatively compact, but dense trajectories can demand substantial processing and storage. The convolutional-pooling study reported approximately 160 GB of extracted iDT features for HMDB51 and 679 GB for UCF101, while the ISDT study explicitly identified high complexity as an obstacle to real-time processing. A small classifier at the end does not imply an inexpensive complete system. [103][97]

**The category suits controlled feature design.** Hand-crafted methods are attractive when a team needs an interpretable representation, has limited capacity for training a large network, or wants a complementary motion branch. That judgment changes when a suitable pretrained temporal model provides a clear matched-protocol gain within the available runtime budget. The strongest conclusion is therefore that engineered features remained important baselines and complementary representations through mid-2019, not that they were uniformly lightweight or obsolete.

### Depth Information-Based Methods

**Depth adds geometry rather than color.** Depth maps describe scene structure and movement toward or away from the camera, while skeleton sequences provide compact body-joint configurations. Methods range from projected motion maps and local depth descriptors to joint-based models and learned depth–skeleton fusion. Their principal attraction is reduced dependence on clothing, texture, and ordinary RGB illumination variation. [89][104]

**Three-view projection makes depth compact.** DMM-HOG projects a depth sequence onto front, side, and top views, accumulates movement, and represents the resulting images with HOG. It converts a variable-length 3-D movement sequence into a small number of fixed-size maps suitable for a conventional classifier. The method therefore trades some temporal detail for compactness and straightforward processing. [105]

**Accumulation explains temporal-order loss.** In the absolute-difference DMM construction used by Chen and colleagues, a projected map is accumulated as follows, where \(P_v(D_t)\) is depth frame \(t\) projected into view \(v\). [106]

$$\mathrm{DMM}_v=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left|P_v(D_t)-P_v(D_{t-1})\right|.$$

Reversing the sequence preserves the same set of absolute consecutive differences, so this accumulated map alone does not retain their order. This is a representational reason that “stand up” and “sit down” can be difficult to distinguish, not merely a weakness of the downstream classifier. The online-recognition and AH-DMM studies explicitly identify that problem. [94][113]

**Temporal subdivisions recover discriminative structure.** Multi-temporal DMMs construct maps over overlapping segments and different durations rather than compressing the entire sequence once. AH-DMMs use motion-dependent hierarchical windows, while LASC first identifies multi-scale sub-actions using normalized motion energy. These approaches share a mechanism: preserve some ordering or local temporal context before performing aggregation. Their additional temporal levels also increase computation. [90][113][112]

**HON4D integrates shape and movement.** Rather than compute a static shape descriptor and a separate motion descriptor, HON4D represents surface-normal orientations in a four-dimensional space containing image coordinates, depth, and time. Its histogram therefore reflects how local geometry evolves. This is especially relevant when similar poses participate in different movements or when an action’s identity depends on the joint relationship between shape and temporal change. [107]

**SNV extends local geometric aggregation.** Yang and Tian’s journal treatment forms polynormals from neighboring hypersurface normals and aggregates them into Super Normal Vectors. An adaptive spatiotemporal pyramid then contributes spatial layout and temporal ordering. Compared with a whole-sequence DMM, this approach retains richer local geometry and organizes it across space and time, at the cost of a more elaborate descriptor pipeline. [109]

**Skeletons provide compact body structure.** EigenJoints models differences between joints within a frame and across frames, capturing posture, movement, and overall dynamics. PCA reduces redundancy and noise, accumulated motion energy selects informative observations, and a non-parametric nearest-neighbor classifier performs recognition. HOJ3D instead quantizes joint locations into posture histograms and models their sequence using a hidden Markov model. [91][89]

**Skeleton compactness removes object evidence.** Body-joint trajectories can describe a hand approaching the head without fully describing the object being held. Rahmani and Bennamoun consequently argue that skeletons alone are insufficient for some human–object interactions and combine skeletal structure with depth appearances of body parts and nearby objects. The resulting distinction is between describing how the body moves and describing what the body interacts with. [104]

**Fusion can combine complementary errors.** Zhu, Chen and Guo obtained 94.3% on MSRAction3D by combining spatiotemporal and skeleton features, compared with 77.5% for their STIP representation and 90.9% for skeleton features. The improvement supports combining local movement evidence with body structure in that experiment. It does not imply that every added modality helps: useful fusion requires that the extra representation contributes information rather than predominantly noise or redundancy. [108]

**Depth descriptors can support modest hardware.** Chen, Jafari and Kehtarnavaz reported real-time DMM-LBP processing on a desktop implementation. Their MSRAction3D timings included 3.98 ± 0.32 milliseconds per frame for DMM construction and 10.39 ± 1.08 milliseconds per sequence for LBP extraction. These units matter: map construction is incremental, whereas extracting the final descriptor and classifying it are sequence-level operations. [106]

**Early recognition differs from continuous recognition.** DMM-HOG reported that 30–35 observed frames were sufficient for results comparable with complete clips in most tested MSRAction3D settings. EigenJoints similarly found useful recognition from approximately the first 30–40% of a sequence. Those observations concern prefixes of actions; continuous recognition must additionally decide where actions occur and handle transitions or undefined movement. [105][91]

**Online methods expose a second latency.** The 2018 online study distinguishes computational latency from observational latency: a method may process frames quickly but still wait for many frames before making a decision. Its DMM extension introduces stream segmentation and skeletal position/velocity to address static actions and temporal order. On MSR 3D Online Action, its LBP-and-SVM method ran at 62 frames per second, while the compared DNBC method ran at seven frames per second but achieved higher accuracy. [94]

**The sensor remains part of the method.** Depth reduces sensitivity to ordinary color and texture variation, but active depth sensors have operating-range and environmental restrictions. Structured-light and time-of-flight systems can suffer under outdoor lighting, and occlusion can produce incorrect or missing joints. These are acquisition failures upstream of classification, so a sophisticated recognition model cannot simply assume that its geometric input remains reliable. [89]

**The category suits controlled geometric observation.** Depth and skeleton methods are particularly attractive when the sensor can observe the relevant body parts reliably and 3-D movement matters more than fine RGB appearance. The recommendation weakens for unconstrained outdoor cameras, distant actors, or interactions whose identifying objects are poorly represented. The section’s central judgment is that depth is a valuable sensing advantage under appropriate conditions, not a universally superior substitute for RGB.

**Temporal modeling and pretraining drove progress.** The important transition was not simply replacing an SVM with a neural classifier. Successful models changed how appearance and motion were separated, how information was combined across time, and how representations were initialized using large image or video collections. The catalogs below distinguish these mechanisms rather than treating every CNN-based method as equivalent. [116][88]

**Two streams make motion explicit.** Simonyan and Zisserman’s spatial network recognizes information in individual RGB frames, while the temporal network receives stacked optical-flow fields. With ten flow fields, horizontal and vertical components produce 20 input channels. The architecture avoids requiring an early RGB-only network to discover useful displacement representations entirely from a small action dataset. [95]

**Appearance and motion make different mistakes.** In the original study, Hammering was confused with HeadMassage by the appearance stream because both often contained prominent faces. Its motion stream confused Hammering with BrushingTeeth because both contained recurring up-and-down hand movement. Combining streams helps because object, scene, posture, and displacement evidence are informative in different ways, although the example also shows that motion alone is not a complete semantic description. [95]

**Optical flow shifts rather than eliminates cost.** The original implementation reported approximately 0.06 seconds to compute a frame-pair flow field using its GPU implementation and therefore precomputed flow before training. Compressing those fields reduced the UCF101 flow storage from approximately 1.5 TB to 27 GB. A two-stream network can be convenient to train once its inputs exist, yet its full acquisition-to-prediction pipeline includes substantial preprocessing. [95]

**Pooling connects engineered and learned representations.** TDD and the pooled ConvNet/VLAD framework retain trajectories as a guide for aggregating learned feature maps. The latter also proposes line pooling: aggregate features at fixed feature-map locations through time, avoiding trajectory extraction. It reported 93.78%/65.62% on UCF101/HMDB51 with trajectory pooling and 90.55%/62.24% with line pooling, illustrating an accuracy–processing trade-off within one framework. [103]

**Fusion location changes the information available.** Late fusion combines decisions already made independently by different streams. Feature-level fusion permits a later network to learn interactions between appearance and motion before classification. This motivates convolutional two-stream fusion, spatiotemporal residual coupling, and later multilevel fusion designs. Their common objective is not merely to add scores, but to learn how spatial and temporal evidence should influence each other. [88][126]

**More semantic channels can hurt.** Wang and colleagues’ semantic-region CNN used detected people, objects, and scene information, but one spatial-stream experiment fell from 80.46% with integrated scene-and-person information to 79.71% after adding its object channel. The authors associated this with imperfect object selection and dominance among the integrated channels. The example favors carefully controlled fusion over the assumption that adding another representation must improve recognition. [117]

**Three-dimensional convolution learns local evolution.** C3D applies convolution and pooling across both space and time, directly constructing spatiotemporal features from short video clips. Its temporal window captures local movement, but a local clip representation does not automatically model an entire long action. LTC extends temporal support, while temporal-segment approaches combine evidence from separated portions of a video. [88][119]

**Long duration is not one mechanism.** LTC increases the duration seen by convolutional filters; TSN samples multiple portions of a video and forms video-level consensus; recurrent models carry a state through an ordered sequence. These alternatives allocate computation differently and preserve different forms of temporal information. Selecting among them should depend on whether the task needs fine local dynamics, coverage of a long clip, or explicit dependence on preceding observations. [116]

**I3D makes image pretraining reusable.** Carreira and Zisserman inflate a two-dimensional image network into a three-dimensional video network. For a temporal kernel length \(N\), a 2-D filter can be repeated over time and divided by \(N\), preserving its response on a video made by repeating one image. This provides a principled initialization rather than requiring every spatiotemporal filter to be learned from scratch. [88]

$$W_{\mathrm{3D}}(:,:, \tau)=\frac{1}{N}W_{\mathrm{2D}},\qquad \tau=1,\ldots,N.$$

**The initialization has a clear interpretation.** For a constant-frame input, summing the responses of the \(N\) repeated, rescaled filters reproduces the original image-filter response. Training can then adapt those filters to actual temporal variation. I3D combines this initialization with longer video snippets and large-scale video pretraining, making its result a joint advance in architecture and training regime. [88]

**Model size is architecture-dependent.** Three-dimensional processing can be expensive, but the I3D study’s implementations show that parameter count does not increase monotonically with temporal sophistication. Its two-stream I3D used fewer parameters than its C3D-like comparison despite processing longer snippets.

|---|---:|---|---:|
| ConvNet + LSTM | 9 million | 25 RGB frames | 5.00 seconds |
| Two-stream | 12 million | 1 RGB + 10 flow frames | 0.40 seconds |

**Video pretraining supplies movement examples.** Kinetics gave the I3D study approximately 240,000 training videos across 400 action classes, with clips around ten seconds long. That collection offers substantially more opportunities to learn temporal variation than the target UCF101 and HMDB51 training sets. The study found that all tested architectures benefited from Kinetics pretraining, although the magnitude varied markedly by architecture. [88]

**ConvLSTM preserves spatially organized state.** A conventional fully connected LSTM commonly receives flattened CNN features. The 2019 ConvLSTM model instead fuses convolutional maps before the fully connected layer and uses convolutional recurrent gates. It retains where features occur while learning how they evolve, addressing information loss caused by compressing a spatial map into a single vector too early. [125]

**Its gains require a specific comparison.** The ConvLSTM paper’s 93.9% UCF101 and 69.4% HMDB51 headline results were positioned against CNN-plus-recurrent methods without Kinetics pretraining. The study also reported approximately 30 hours to train its recurrent component on each HMDB51 split using a GTX1060 GPU with 6 GB memory. This is a useful example of improving temporal organization without matching the much larger pretraining regime of I3D. [125]

**OFF targets the preprocessing bottleneck.** Sun and colleagues derive a motion-sensitive representation from CNN feature-map spatial gradients and temporal differences, guided by the optical-flow constraint. Their RGB-only OFF network reported 93.3% on UCF101 and a 15-fold speed advantage over the two-stream comparison used in that experiment. The separate 96.0%/74.2% UCF101/HMDB51 result belongs to OFF integrated into a stronger recognition framework. [121]

**Adaptive sampling spends computation selectively.** AdaFrame, presented at CVPR on 15–20 June 2019, uses an LSTM with global memory and policy-gradient training to choose relevant frames for each input. It matched its all-frame comparison using averages of 8.21 frames on FCVID and 8.65 on ActivityNet. Its significance is conditional computation: an input need not receive the same frame-processing budget as every other input. [128]

**Rate robustness is separate from accuracy.** T-RECS trains models using temporal resampling so that action identity is less dependent on execution speed. It evaluated 15 resampling factors from 0.2 to 3.0 and demonstrated substantial reductions in speed-induced performance drops across several backbones. This addresses a different question from whether a network achieves its highest score at the benchmark’s original playback rate. [124]

**Synthetic data changes the labeling trade-off.** PHAV provided 39,982 procedurally generated videos across 35 categories, including 14 procedurally defined synthetic actions. Its multi-task training mixed synthetic and real video, even where their categories differed. The approach reduces dependence on collecting every useful movement example manually, but its benefit still depends on whether the generated variation transfers to real target videos. [120]

**Weak supervision changes the scale.** Ghadiyaram, Tran and Mahajan trained video representations from hashtag-labeled social-media collections, avoiding manual annotation of their pretraining labels. The source collections illustrate the sizes and label vocabularies involved.

**Estimated depth is a learned intermediate.** The AF-plus-MF network applies a fine-tuned depth-estimation model to RGB frames, extracts appearance features from its estimated maps, and obtains motion features from depth temporal gradients. It therefore belongs in the deep-learning catalog while also using depth-like information. Unlike a physical depth camera, its intermediate representation is inferred from the original RGB signal and inherits the assumptions of that inference. [129]

**Hybrid features remained useful in 2019.** Inception-ResNet-v2 plus WVLGTP combines learned appearance with manually constructed temporal texture. On KTH, the study reported 94.9% for its deep features, 94.4% for WVLGTP, and 96.5% for their fusion. The result supports complementary representations within that experiment, while its Hollywood2 comparison—where the iDT fusion exceeded the proposed WVLGTP fusion—shows that the best engineered complement can change with the dataset. [101]

**The category suits transferable representations.** Deep learning is the preferred general starting point when suitable pretrained weights, sufficient target examples, and adequate compute are available. The recommendation changes when preprocessing latency, domain mismatch, weak sensing, or small-data overfitting dominates. By mid-2019, the most consequential progress came from coordinating temporal representation, training data, and computation—not from network depth alone.

Bag-of-3D-Points changes from 94.2% to 74.7%, a 19.5-point difference. These changes show that recognizing new repetitions and recognizing new people place materially different demands on the representation. [91]

**Action subsets also change the conclusion.** DMM-HOG’s cross-subject results range from 84.1% on AS2 to 96.2% on AS1. Its apparently uniform “high accuracy” therefore masks a calculated 12.1-point spread across subsets. This is why a single reported average should not substitute for asking which actions were confused and how the action groups were constructed. [105]

**The DMM values describe different experiments.** The 88.73% DMM entry in the 2016 comparison, the original subset-specific DMM-HOG scores, and the 85.5% 20-action result in the 2015 DMM-LBP study are separate reported measurements. Replacing one with another would erase rather than resolve their experimental differences. Padilla-López and colleagues specifically organized MSRAction3D comparisons by validation methodology because the literature used differing tests. [90][106][133]

**Repeated partitions reveal split sensitivity.** DMM-LBP-DF reports 93.0% on the fixed 20-action subject split but 87.3 ± 2.7% across all 252 ways of choosing five of ten subjects for training. DMM-LBP-FF similarly reports 91.94% and 87.9 ± 2.9%. Those repeated-partition results are valuable because they show how much the result depends on who appears in training and testing. [106]

**Sequence inclusion changes the denominator.** MSRAction3D originally contained 567 sequences, while a widely used experimental version contains 557 after ten sequences with missing or erroneous skeletons were excluded. The dataset page also corrects a previously stated count of 402 to 557. Such exclusions are especially relevant when comparing depth-image methods against methods requiring valid skeletons. [131]

**Richer features help for identifiable reasons.** DMM-LBP replaces coarse map description with local texture encoding and fusion; multi-temporal DMMs preserve local temporal structure before aggregation; skeleton–depth fusion adds body configuration to local motion. Their reported improvements are consistent with these mechanisms, but a cross-paper score difference does not isolate any one mechanism’s causal contribution. Within-study ablations provide the cleaner evidence for deciding which component to retain.

**The defensible conclusion is protocol-specific.** The 95.97% entry is the largest value in the reproduced 2016 comparison, not a universal MSRAction3D winner. A unified ranking would require identical action classes, subject assignments, sequence inclusion, preprocessing, and scoring. For practical selection, prioritize the evaluation whose generalization conditions resemble the intended application.

**Early deep learning was not uniformly superior.** The original two-stream comparison reported 88.0% on UCF101 and 59.4% on HMDB51. Its iDT-plus-Fisher baseline achieved 85.9% and 57.2%, while higher-dimensional iDT encodings reached 87.9% and 61.1%. Thus, the learned model exceeded the basic trajectory baseline but did not dominate every stronger engineered encoding on both datasets. [95]

**Later gains were substantially larger.** Using the I3D paper’s comparison configurations, ImageNet-plus-Kinetics two-stream I3D exceeded iDT by a calculated 11.6 percentage points on UCF101, from 86.4% to 98.0%, and 19.0 points on HMDB51, from 61.7% to 80.7%. These are differences between complete systems; they combine architecture, input processing, and training-data advantages. [88]

**Error reduction clarifies near-ceiling gains.** Against ST-ResNet plus iDT, I3D’s UCF101 improvement from 94.6% to 98.0% reduces error from 5.4% to 2.0%. The calculated relative reduction is \(3.4/5.4 \approx 63.0\%\). For HMDB51, using the same I3D configuration, moving from 70.3% to 80.7% reduces error by \(10.4/29.7 \approx 35.0\%\). This is more informative than saying that the improvements are “only” 3.4 and 10.4 points. [88]

**The strongest explanation combines data and architecture.** I3D’s own experiments show that Kinetics pretraining benefits multiple architectures, with particularly large gains for its spatiotemporal model. The authors connect its transfer performance to higher temporal resolution, longer snippets, greater depth, and reuse of image-network initialization. The evidence therefore supports coordinated improvements rather than attributing the entire gain either to “3-D convolution” or to “more data.” [88]

**Two-stream C3D isolates motion’s contribution.** In its own reported experiment, adding the flow stream increased UCF101 accuracy from 82.37% to 88.79%, a calculated 6.42-point gain, and HMDB51 from 48.43% to 62.54%, a 14.11-point gain. This is stronger evidence for the value of complementary motion in that architecture than comparing unrelated papers’ highest figures. It also illustrates that the same architectural change can have different effects across datasets. [122]

**HMDB51 is not a smaller UCF101.** It has fewer classes and clips, but those facts do not make it an easier classification task. The I3D study notes deliberately difficult examples and similar scenes containing different action labels. Limited training examples and ambiguous appearance therefore provide plausible reasons why many methods score substantially lower on HMDB51 than on UCF101. [88]

**The unusual HMDB51 reports remain visible.** Trajectory-Set’s 85.4% is above I3D’s 80.9% HMDB51 configuration, and the depth-estimation network reports 96.03% ARR. Those values are the strongest supported partial answer to a raw-score comparison; they do not justify deleting the broader evidence favoring pretrained temporal networks. They instead require distinguishing what each paper reports from what a matched, independently repeated comparison would establish. [92][129]

**The depth-network gain is system-level evidence.** Its appearance-only HMDB51 result was 87.8%, motion-only was 83.7%, and their combination was 96.03%. The paper also fine-tuned its depth estimator using 10,000 NYU depth images and used pretrained feature-extraction networks. These details support a claim about its complete estimated-depth recognition pipeline; they do not isolate how much of the final score comes specifically from geometric estimation, feature aggregation, or classifier training. [129]

**Family maxima are not single algorithms.** The Local STIP row combines the highest results attributed to a feature family across datasets, and the Dense Trajectory row is likewise a secondary category summary. In particular, the 97.3% UCF101 family maximum should not be read as the standalone 2011 Dense Trajectories algorithm outperforming the explicitly identified iDT configurations. These rows answer what the secondary summary reports, not which fixed implementation produces all listed scores. [134]

**Weizmann demonstrates benchmark saturation.** A near-perfect result on simple single-person actions against a static background addresses a narrower visual-recognition problem than UCF101 or HMDB51. Hassner’s 2013 benchmark review already listed a 100% Weizmann result, while later reviews continued to report perfect scores. The meaningful conclusion is that this controlled benchmark had limited ability to separate strong modern recognizers, not that realistic activity understanding had been solved. [135][93]

**Benchmark variants must retain their identity.** The low-quality-video silhouette framework evaluates manually degraded Weizmann, KTH, and Ballet sequences. Those experiments are relevant to illumination robustness but address a changed input distribution. Similarly, DHA includes action categories following Weizmann definitions but is a separate depth benchmark, so a DHA result is not a Weizmann accuracy. [102][136]

**Official splits protect against easy leakage.** UCF101 videos are grouped, and clips in one group can originate from the same longer video. Its official guidance explicitly warns that sharing such groups between training and testing can inflate results and requests the three prescribed train/test splits. A random video partition therefore asks a different question from the benchmark’s grouped protocol. [132]

**Hybrid evaluations deserve protocol-specific reading.** The Inception-ResNet-v2-plus-WVLGTP paper reports 94.9% on UCF101 using its stated 70%/30% partitioning and five-fold validation procedure. That figure is retained, but it should be interpreted as that study’s result rather than silently placed on an official-three-split ranking. The distinction concerns experimental design; the available text does not establish that any particular leakage actually occurred. [101]

**Preprocessing can materially change a backbone.** Under the standardized T-RECS pipeline, I3D’s baseline was 88.95% on UCF101 and 60.92% on HMDB51, rather than the original I3D paper’s transfer results. Sinusoidal resampling increased these to 91.83% and 65.42%, calculated gains of 2.88 and 4.50 points. The authors explicitly attribute differences from original-author results to their changed preprocessing, making within-study comparisons the appropriate interpretation. [124]

**Score transcription follows stated configurations.** I3D’s comparison table associates 80.7% HMDB51 with ImageNet-plus-Kinetics and 80.9% with Kinetics-only, although its introductory summary combines headline values differently. The estimated-depth paper gives 97.7% UCF101 in its table and 97.71% in its conclusion. The ConvLSTM abstract reports 69.4% HMDB51, while its final-performance prose says 69.3%; Table 5 retains the abstract figure and treats that tenth-point difference as a reporting discrepancy rather than a demonstrated effect. [88][129][125]

**The performance judgment is conditional but useful.** For a reproducible starting point on realistic RGB clips, pretrained I3D, TSN-style temporal sampling, and clearly specified two-stream configurations are stronger reference choices than an undifferentiated collection of maxima. For deployment, OFF, adaptive sampling, and compact alternatives can be preferable once latency and preprocessing enter the objective. A method’s suitability changes when the target domain, allowable input modalities, or training resources change.

## 3. Qualitative Comparison

**Three resource questions decide the category.** What can the camera reliably measure? What information distinguishes the target actions? What training and inference resources are affordable? A method that represents the relevant evidence reliably is generally a better starting point than one chosen solely because it achieved a larger percentage on a different benchmark.

**Training burden has several components.** Hand-crafted features still require labeled examples for supervised classification, but their low-level representation is not learned through a large end-to-end optimization. Depth methods can reduce visual nuisance variation while adding sensor deployment and pose-quality management. Deep models move much of the representation-design burden into pretraining, optimization, data collection, and model selection. [111][89][93]

**Annotation requirements depend on the task.** Clip classification can use video-level action labels, whereas detection, pose supervision, and object-aware interaction models can require more detailed annotation. The online depth study notes that manually labeling action-related objects is laborious; the weak-pretraining study instead uses hashtags at scale, accepting label noise. Thus, “deep learning needs more labels” is too crude: the source, precision, and granularity of supervision matter as much as the number of examples. [94][130]

**Runtime must cover the complete pipeline.** Sparse local descriptors, dense optical flow, skeleton estimation, deep feature extraction, and stream fusion consume resources at different stages. A classifier’s prediction time omits those preceding operations. Likewise, a high frame-processing rate does not establish low observational latency if the representation requires an entire clip before it can classify an action. [95][94]

**Representation should follow the discriminating cue.** For actions distinguished chiefly by body movement, trajectories or skeleton sequences can suppress irrelevant appearance. For actions distinguished by manipulated objects, removing appearance too aggressively can remove the decisive evidence. For reverse-order actions, preserving temporal order is essential; a whole-sequence accumulated motion image may be inadequate even if its spatial descriptor is excellent. [104][94]

**Table 6. Advantages and Disadvantages of Various Techniques**

| Technique Category | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Hand-crafted Motion Features | Explicit feature design; sparse variants are compact; modest representation-training burden; useful complementary motion cues | Manual design and limited transfer; camera-motion/tracking sensitivity; dense extraction can be expensive |
| Depth Information | Direct 3-D geometry; reduced color/texture dependence; easier foreground separation; compact skeletons; efficient recognition options | Sensor range and environment constraints; depth noise and occlusion; skeleton errors; basic DMMs lose temporal order |
| Deep Learning | Learns hierarchical representations; supports temporal modeling and fusion; strong pretrained RGB results; adaptive computation options | Training, memory, and annotation demands; flow preprocessing costs; pretraining/domain dependence; complex optimization |

The category-level trade-offs follow the local-feature, depth-sensing, and deep-learning analyses cited throughout the review. [98][89][116]

**Limited training favors established representations.** A strong engineered descriptor or frozen pretrained network can be preferable to training a high-capacity video model from scratch. The LTC and original two-stream studies both show the importance of initialization and input representation on small action datasets. This favors comparing realistic training regimes rather than treating model families independently of their available weights. [119][95]

**Controlled indoor geometry favors depth.** A reliably positioned RGB-D camera observing a person at an appropriate range can make skeletons, DMMs, or depth–skeleton fusion compelling. The choice changes if the relevant joints are repeatedly occluded, the sensor cannot measure the environment reliably, or the action is distinguished by an object whose appearance is lost. In those cases, RGB or multimodal recognition can retain evidence that a geometry-only representation omits. [89][104]

**Unconstrained RGB favors pretrained temporal learning.** I3D’s transfer results make a strong case for pretrained spatiotemporal networks where the application resembles realistic web-video classification and the processing budget permits them. OFF and AdaFrame show that the next decision need not be “accuracy or nothing”: one can target flow-free motion features or input-dependent frame budgets. These alternatives change the cost of extracting evidence rather than simply shrinking the final classifier. [88][121][128]

**Advantages and Disadvantages of Various Techniques**

These are tendencies, not causal rankings. MSRAction3D is a 20-action, 10-subject depth benchmark with multiple settings [181]; UCF101 is in-the-wild, with 101 classes and 13,320 clips [183]; HMDB51 has 51 actions and 6,766 clips, while its official page reports 6,849 [340][233]; Weizmann has 9 actions and 81 clips, while another account reports 10 categories [255][218]. Split, metric, chance level, pretraining and dataset size therefore bound every category claim [255][221]. Post-2019 sources are retrospective, not cutoff evidence [232][240][241][350][371].

The evidence supports a balanced comparison of video activity-recognition techniques using recognition accuracy, computational complexity, temporal-modelling capability, and real-world suitability rather than accuracy alone [371]; the review also covers methods, evaluation datasets, comparative results, and research directions . It contrasts hand-crafted motion descriptors with deep systems that combine CNN-derived static and motion features with LSTM temporal modelling [353], while occlusion, environmental, dataset, computation, explainability, and protocol-variation risks make conditional trade-offs more defensible than a universal category ranking .

Hand-crafted Motion Features provide explicit, interpretable local and global descriptors—optical flow, silhouettes, trajectories, HOG, HOF, and MBH—and local representations are used where global descriptors are vulnerable to clutter, illumination change, and occlusion; they remain competitive with deep representations and can perform better on smaller datasets [357][361][343][230][260]. Their costs are ad-hoc, domain-specific design and intensive preprocessing, high-dimensional or dataset-specific descriptors with limited semantics and discriminative capacity, plus sensitivity to tracking, alignment, motion duration, and viewpoint; in a reported detector comparison, the hand-crafted detector was faster than its deep counterpart [364][169][362][267][357][228][329].

Depth Information-Based Methods add 3-D structural, shape, and posture cues, reduce sensitivity to illumination and actor appearance, and aid segmentation and occlusion handling; compared with RGB-based methods, they can describe body motion more accurately and support real-time recognition [281][328][172][366]. The trade-offs are sensor cost and operational complexity, noisy or missing depth values, difficulty for handcrafted methods in finding motion in depth sequences, and weaker CNN discrimination when depth lacks color and texture [224][332][331][225]; their benefit is therefore conditional on sensor quality, representation, and deployment setting.

Deep Learning-Based Methods automatically learn hierarchical, task-dependent representations from raw or multimodal data; CNN and two-stream models combine appearance and motion, while RNN/LSTM models preserve temporal context without manual feature extraction [327][368][147][253][138][227][330]. This flexibility is offset by high computational complexity, large data requirements, and limited interpretability, while single-frame CNNs fail to capture motion unless temporal modelling is added [180][266]; the implication is stronger learned representation at the cost of heavier training, deployment, and explanation burdens.

MSRAction3D is a depth-map benchmark with twenty actions, so it provides evidence about depth and skeletal recognition under a depth-sequence, cross-subject setting, but not a category-wide comparison with RGB hand-crafted or general deep-learning methods [207][213]. Its comparatively easy subject–background segmentation and dataset-specific conditions limit external validity, and its reported error patterns caution against treating performance on MSRAction3D as a universal ranking [362][344].

UCF101 supplies RGB benchmark evidence with variation in camera movement, object appearance, position and scale, viewpoint, cluttered backgrounds, and illumination, making it useful for probing robustness across visual methods [369]. However, randomly mixing videos from the same group across training and testing can inflate accuracy through shared backgrounds and viewpoints, while the sports-video setting and limited size prevent category-wide conclusions and make the dataset too small for optimizing deep CNN representations from scratch [201][369][221].

HMDB51 contributes a challenging RGB benchmark: its clips are low-resolution and noisy and span different temporal and spatial scales, camera motion and angle, and participant counts, so it tests robustness beyond clean imagery [244][348]. Its limited scale, context bias, and overfitting risk, together with its controlled-setting nature, limit transfer to real-world surveillance; scores also cannot be assumed comparable when testing protocols and performance measures differ [370][371][255].

Weizmann supplies an early silhouette/video benchmark and supports a specific space–time silhouette result that is fast and requires no video alignment [351][341][312]. Evidence from only Weizmann is preliminary, and a Sparsey result is configuration-specific, so it cannot establish a category-wide advantage; depth and shape methods also remain vulnerable to occlusion, confusing backgrounds, classifier implementation, and viewpoint, while differing validation protocols limit score comparisons [216][217][367][179].
