# China’s Green Bond External Reviewers: Types, Market Structure and Risks Through 2022

**An established but uneven market:** By 31 December 2022, China had an established green bond external-review market, with **18 institutions registered** through the Green Bond Standards Committee’s market-based evaluation process. External reviews covered **81% of issuance volume and 68% of deals** among Chinese green bonds aligned with the Climate Bonds Initiative’s definition, and second-party opinions were the most common review method. Nevertheless, independent green-credentials review was generally encouraged rather than required across every bond category. The registration record and issuance statistics establish substantial market participation, but not a uniformly mandatory review system. [1][2]

**Different products answer different questions:** Certification offers the clearest standardization and comparability within a particular standard; second-party opinions usually provide the richest qualitative explanation; verification tests specified claims or controls; and green ratings or scores provide structured comparisons within a methodology. China’s competition data show substantial concentration—its two largest reviewers held a combined 50% share in 2018 and 44% in 2019—but also meaningful changes in leadership. The principal risks arise when concentrated market access, issuer-paid mandates and weak continuing scrutiny reduce incentives to challenge green claims.

## 1. GBER Types and Comparison

### Four review products and their institutional providers

**Product type differs from institution:** The International Capital Market Association’s June 2022 external-review guidelines recognize second-party opinions, verification, certification and green/social/sustainability bond scoring or rating. These are categories of service, not mutually exclusive categories of organization: an accounting firm, consultancy or research provider can offer several products, and one bond can receive more than one review. [3]

| Review type | Definition | Typical providers | Main advantage | Main disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Second-party opinion | Independent assessment of a framework’s alignment and environmental credentials | Sustainability research institutes and consultancies | Detailed explanation of strengths, weaknesses and greenness | Methods vary; follow-up is not inherent |
| Certification | Formal conformity with a recognized external standard or label | Certification schemes supported by approved verifiers | Public criteria and a recognizable label | Binary outcome; restricted flexibility and sector coverage |
| Verification | Independent testing against designated criteria | Accounting firms, assurance specialists and accredited verifiers | Focused testing of specific claims and controls | Assurance extends only to the engagement’s scope |
| Green rating/scoring | Environmental assessment using an established rating methodology | Rating agencies and specialist research providers | Structured, potentially quantitative comparison | Different models and scales limit interoperability |

**Four underlying components connect them:** For use-of-proceeds green bonds, the common reference points are use of proceeds, project evaluation and selection, management of proceeds, and reporting. An external review can assess all four or a specified subset. Consequently, the title on a report is less informative than the combination of its criteria, coverage, timing and conclusion. [3]

### Second-party opinion: explanatory depth with methodological discretion

**Definition and typical institutional providers:** A second-party opinion, or SPO, is an assessment by an institution with environmental or sustainability expertise that is independent of the issuer. It normally evaluates whether the issuer’s green bond framework aligns with recognized principles and whether the proposed activities have credible environmental characteristics. ICMA also expects independence from the framework adviser, or appropriate information barriers where relevant services sit within the same institution. [3]

**Research and consultancy firms predominate:** Typical providers include CICERO, ISS-Oekom, Sustainalytics and Vigeo Eiris, alongside sustainability practices within firms such as Deloitte and EY. The defining capability is environmental analysis rather than a particular legal form. Dorfleitner, Utz and Zhang’s study, published in *Review of Managerial Science* in 2022, describes SPOs as the most popular external review and identifies independent environmental research institutions as characteristic providers. [4][5]

**Interpretation is the main strength:** A full SPO can explain why a framework is credible, where its project categories are strong, and which provisions create environmental or implementation risks. This is particularly useful when an issuer finances several activities with different environmental profiles. Instead of merely stating that requirements have been met, an SPO can distinguish ambitious long-term solutions from incremental improvements.

**Named cases demonstrate that nuance:** CICERO’s assessments of KfW illustrate the information an SPO can provide beyond a green label. The 2015 framework received a dark-green assessment because of its renewable-energy focus and project-screening procedures. The 2019 framework received medium green: although it supported renewable energy and energy-efficient buildings, the assessment identified less complete exclusion of fossil-fuel involvement. The judgment therefore reflected framework content, not simply the issuer’s reputation. [4]

**Methodological freedom limits direct comparison:** Providers select their own analytical frameworks, thresholds and presentation formats. A favorable conclusion from one firm may therefore embody a different level of environmental ambition from a favorable conclusion issued elsewhere. CBI’s comparison of review products also identifies proprietary methods, uneven transparency and the absence of a single SPO methodology as obstacles to comparison across issuers and industries. [6]

**Issuance opinions do not ensure follow-through:** An SPO generally assesses a framework and intended allocation at a particular time. It does not automatically establish that money subsequently reached the intended assets or that projected environmental benefits occurred. The BIS’s 2017 discussion of CICERO explicitly distinguishes issuance-time framework assessment from subsequent monitoring undertaken at an issuer’s request. [7]

**The resulting practical judgment:** An SPO is especially valuable when investors need to understand environmental ambition, governance and weaknesses. Its value increases when the report identifies its criteria and gives a differentiated conclusion; continuing allocation and impact verification remains a separate question.

### Certification: common criteria and a recognizable label

**Definition and division of responsibility:** Certification formally establishes that a bond, framework or use of proceeds conforms to a recognized external standard or label. The standard defines the requirements, a qualified third party tests conformity, and the certification scheme awards the label. Under the Climate Bonds Standard, the approved verifier and CBI therefore perform related but distinct functions. [3][6]

**Several institutional classes supply verification:** Accounting firms, inspection and certification organizations, environmental consultancies and sustainability specialists can act as approved verifiers. Historically documented examples include EY, KPMG, Bureau Veritas, DNV-GL, Trucost and SynTao Green Finance. These organizations assess compliance; their involvement should not be confused with ownership of the certification standard. [8]

**Public standards strengthen screening:** Certification’s main advantage is an external benchmark that is more prescriptive than general process principles. The Climate Bonds Standard applies science-based, sector-specific criteria alongside requirements for issuer controls and proceeds management. Investors can consequently screen for conformity with a known standard without independently reconstructing every reviewer’s underlying definition of green. [7]

**Continuing requirements can add substance:** The CBI–CCDC report for 2018 describes certification before issuance and further assurance after issuance and allocation have begun. CBI’s 2019 global report describes annual post-issuance verification to maintain certified status. Its retrospective 2022 comparison likewise emphasizes post-issuance verification and annual reporting, making certification more than an initial label when those scheme requirements are followed. [9][10]

**Binary labels compress environmental differences:** Two certified bonds can satisfy the same standard while differing considerably in expected environmental benefit, implementation difficulty or long-term ambition. Certification establishes that the threshold has been met; it need not rank every passing bond. The BIS identifies this binary nature as a reason investors may also value more granular assessments. [7]

**Specific requirements reduce flexibility:** Certification standards do not necessarily cover every potentially beneficial activity. Their prescriptive nature can make novel projects difficult to accommodate until sector criteria are developed. CBI’s comparison identifies incomplete economic coverage and limited flexibility as disadvantages of certification relative to principles-based opinions. [6]

**The resulting practical judgment:** Certification is the strongest option for investors seeking a recognizable common-standard filter. Its conclusion should be read as conformity with specified criteria, supplemented where necessary by more detailed information on environmental performance and implementation.

### Verification: a defined test rather than a general endorsement

**Definition and typical institutional providers:** Verification is an independent check against a designated set of criteria. It may examine project eligibility, allocation of proceeds, internal tracking, environmental-impact statements or reporting alignment. Accounting firms such as KPMG and PwC, accredited verifiers and specialist assurance organizations commonly provide this service. [3][4]

**Bounded testing improves evidential clarity:** A verification engagement specifies what is being tested and against which criteria. For example, a verifier may check whether an issuer’s allocation statement reconciles with its records and whether the financed assets meet stated eligibility conditions. This produces a more focused conclusion than an open-ended discussion of the issuer’s sustainability strategy.

**Post-issuance work is particularly important:** Verification can test conduct rather than intention. It can examine whether proceeds were allocated as promised, whether reporting follows the designated requirements, and whether environmental claims are supported by evidence. CPI’s review of China’s monitoring, reporting and verification system identifies precisely these continuing functions. [11]

**Narrow scope creates the main weakness:** A positive verification conclusion answers the engagement’s question, not every question an investor might ask. Verification of proceeds tracking does not necessarily assess the environmental ambition of the project-selection framework. Similarly, verification of an impact calculation need not establish that its assumptions represent the most demanding available environmental benchmark.

**Objectivity still depends on design:** Predefined procedures make verification more criteria-bound than many SPOs, but the choice of criteria, assurance level and evidence remains consequential. Dorfleitner, Utz and Zhang describe verification reports as generally shorter and less detailed than SPOs. That brevity can improve focus, but it can also leave broader environmental questions outside the report. [4]

**The resulting practical judgment:** Verification is particularly useful for checking actual allocation, reporting and defined environmental claims. Investors should identify the tested statement and procedures before treating the conclusion as an endorsement of the bond’s overall greenness.

### Green bond rating or scoring: structured environmental differentiation

**Definition and distinction from credit:** Green bond rating or scoring evaluates a bond, framework or environmental feature through an established methodology. The output may be a grade, numerical score or structured assessment of environmental performance and processes. ICMA explicitly distinguishes this product from a credit rating, which concerns financial risk and creditworthiness. [3]

**Rating agencies are characteristic providers:** International examples include Moody’s and S&P; China’s early domestic providers included China Chengxin and China Bond Rating. CBI–CCDC’s 2018 China report records 11 bonds receiving green ratings and identifies those two domestic institutions among the earliest developers. It also records Moody’s GB1, or Excellent, Green Bond Assessment for Wuhan Metro’s senior perpetual green bond. [9]

**Structured outputs support internal comparison:** Numerical factors and explicit weighting can make assessments easier to reproduce and compare within a provider’s system. Moody’s first public Green Bond Assessment methodology, introduced in March 2016, used quantifiable factors to assess the likelihood that proceeds would support environmentally friendly projects. S&P’s Green Evaluations, introduced in 2017, used a 0–100 score reflecting expected lifetime environmental impact, adjusted for governance and transparency. [7]

**Different models measure different things:** A score emphasizing allocation governance is not automatically equivalent to one emphasizing lifetime environmental benefit. Numbers make a conclusion compact; they do not make unlike concepts interchangeable. Cross-agency comparisons therefore require attention to the assessed object, environmental baseline, factor weights and treatment of uncertainty.

**Continuity varies with the product:** The BIS describes S&P’s 2017 evaluation as point-in-time, with scores removed from its website after at most 18 months unless a separate evaluation supported further assessment. By contrast, Moody’s methodology anticipated regular review. This difference shows why a rating product should not be assumed to provide continuous assurance merely because conventional credit ratings are frequently monitored. [7]

**The resulting practical judgment:** Green ratings and scores are useful for structured differentiation, especially within one methodology. They should remain separate from credit analysis and be accompanied by an explicit understanding of update frequency and the environmental questions the model actually measures.

### Comparison across the three requested dimensions

**Certification leads common-standard comparison:** The following matrix gives qualitative comparative judgments, rather than numerical quality scores. Standardization concerns how clearly criteria are fixed; comparability concerns whether two outputs convey equivalent meanings; information quantity concerns the breadth, detail and continuing usefulness of the information supplied. The distinctions follow ICMA’s definitions, the BIS comparison and the review literature. [3][7]

| Review type | Standardization level | Comparability | Information quantity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second-party opinion | Relatively low; provider-specific interpretation | Low across providers and sectors | High qualitative detail, usually concentrated at issuance |
| Certification | Highest explicit standardization within a scheme | High within the same standard; binary result | Medium–high lifecycle documentation; compact final label |
| Verification | Medium–high; fixed criteria but engagement-dependent | Medium when scope and criteria match | Medium and targeted to specified claims |
| Green rating/scoring | Medium–high within an established agency model | Medium overall; stronger within one methodology | Medium–high structured or quantitative information |

**Standardization does not equal ambition:** A consistently applied standard can make bonds comparable even when it captures only a minimum threshold. Conversely, an SPO may use more discretionary reasoning yet identify important weaknesses that a threshold-based label does not communicate. Investors should therefore distinguish consistency of assessment from environmental stringency.

**Comparability requires matching assessment objects:** Two verifications are meaningfully comparable only if they test similar claims using similar procedures. Two green ratings are comparable only if their scales and assessed concepts align. Certification simplifies this task within one scheme, while SPO comparisons usually require reading the underlying reasoning rather than matching the final wording.

**Information has a time dimension:** An extensive issuance-time report may explain a framework better than a short allocation assurance statement. After funds have been deployed, however, that short statement may provide more decision-relevant evidence about what actually happened. Certification can combine a compact label with a substantial documentary record, so its information content should not be judged solely by the label’s binary presentation.

**Assurance strength differs from length:** A long narrative does not necessarily contain more independently tested evidence than a short assurance report. The relevant distinction is between explanation, conformity testing and evidence about implementation. This is why the four products often complement one another rather than form a simple hierarchy.

**Market pricing supplies a counterexample:** Dorfleitner, Utz and Zhang examined 250 matched green bonds and 500 conventional bonds, using 92,774 daily observations from 2011–2020. Their main models associated SPOs and verification with green yield premiums of roughly 3–4 basis points, while finding no corresponding significant incremental premium for CBI certification or traditional-agency green ratings. Dark-green SPOs had an approximately 5-basis-point premium relative to bonds without an SPO. These international results illustrate investor demand for information; they are not a China-specific estimate or evidence that a certification standard lacks environmental value. [4]

**The practical selection rule:** Use certification for a common-standard label, an SPO for interpretive depth, verification for a defined factual or control test, and ratings for structured comparison within a coherent methodology; stronger transparency and common methods would improve comparability across all four.

**Scope and taxonomy**

A green-bond external reviewer (GBER) is an external institution that assesses a bond, framework, proceeds, impacts, or related green claim; its deliverable may be an opinion, certification, verification, rating, or score, with the assurance level depending on the service. ICMA’s 2020 Guidelines group independent external reviews into four types: **Second-party opinion, Verification, Certification, and Green bond scoring/rating** [40]. This is a service taxonomy, not a firm taxonomy: one provider may offer several services. Reviews may cover a framework or programme, an individual issue, underlying assets, or procedures, and may be partial or full; confidentiality can affect timing and publication [40]. External review remains optional in most international frameworks, subject to exceptions such as the Climate Bonds Standard [41].

An undated account reports **32** reviewers mentioned by ICMA and **59** approved by CBI, with **14** appearing on both lists, implying a union of **77** organizations; it also describes competence requirements covering debt instruments, assurance, standards, geographic coverage, and technical expertise. Because the account does not specify an observation date or complete universe, these figures are contextual rather than an end-2022 roster [41].

**Second-party opinion**

**Second-party opinion (SPO)** is an assessment by an independent institution with environmental, social, or sustainability expertise of an issuer’s framework or issue against the Principles, including relevant objectives, policies, processes, and intended projects [40]. Typical providers are environmental or ESG consultants and research organizations; named examples include **Sustainalytics, Cicero, Vigeo Eiris, and Deloitte**, while accounting firms and regulated credit-rating agencies may also provide external reviews [38][40][41]. A typical SPO is a **five-to-ten-page** document and normally reviews the framework rather than each bond issued under that framework [38].

The main advantage of an SPO is investor-oriented explanation: it is generally published when the issuer begins marketing the bond, helps investors understand the framework and sustainability-policy analysis, may satisfy a portfolio criterion, and can support eligibility for a green-specific securities list [38]. Internationally, more than **85% of green, social, or sustainability bonds by number of issuances in 2019** obtained an SPO; this is an international figure, not a China-specific market share [38].

The principal limitations are voluntariness, variable scope, and independence risk. The Green Bond Principles recommend, but do not require, an external review; some investors may rely mainly on the existence or reputation of the provider rather than the substance of the analysis [38]. Providers should be independent, but many SPO firms lack stand-alone independence requirements, and some providers or affiliates also advise prospective issuers. Information barriers and disclosure of independence concerns are therefore important safeguards [38][40].

**Certification**

**Certification** means certifying a bond, framework, or use of proceeds against a recognized external green standard or label; qualified or accredited third parties test conformity with the specified criteria [40]. One account describes **CBI** as issuing certification under its specific scheme while requiring the verification work to be performed by an approved verifier [41]. Certification’s main benefit is conformity to a named standard and the resulting legitimacy, with possible effects on marketability, circulation, and the bond premium [41].

A study of **285 green bonds issued from 2016 to 2019** reports that third-party Certification significantly reduced green-bond spreads and financing costs, with a stronger effect for non-state-owned enterprises [37]. The study recommends stronger certification standards and implementation, but it does not establish a universal provider structure, a general disadvantage profile, or a China-wide causal effect. Certification also depends on the content of the recognized standard and on access to qualified or approved verifiers; the cited account says that most bonds followed CBI or ICMA standards, highlighting the role of standard-setter gatekeeping [40][41].

**Verification**

**Verification** is independent testing or assurance against designated criteria. It can cover internal or external standards, issuer claims, underlying assets, use-of-proceeds tracking and allocation, environmental-impact statements, or reporting alignment with the Principles [40]. Providers may be independent external reviewers, including accounting firms and regulated rating agencies; examples named for verification include **Agusto&Co, GFC, and Arup** [40][41]. Its advantage is process, proceeds, impact, and reporting assurance, which can strengthen the credibility of a green claim [40][41].

Its limitation is methodological variability: criteria may be internal or external, and the absence of fixed universal green categories can produce different assessments and credibility concerns [41]. A **2025 European Union** source further illustrates verification as a shift from asset-performance checking toward process assurance and describes risks of regulatory invalidation; because it is post-cutoff and EU-specific, it does not establish China’s GBER status through 2022 [36].

**Green bond rating/scoring and preliminary comparison**

**Green bond rating/scoring** evaluates a bond, framework, or use-of-proceeds feature under an established methodology. Specialized research providers or rating agencies may assess environmental or social performance data, process against the Principles, or another benchmark; this output is distinct from an ordinary credit rating [40]. Named providers include **S&P, Moody’s, and ISS** [41]. Its strength is a structured signal, while its principal limitation is that differing scales and categories can make ratings difficult to compare [41].

For a common low–medium–high rubric, **standardization** means uniformity of criteria and process; **comparability** means cross-provider comparability of outputs; and **information quantity** means breadth and granularity. The evidence supports a conditional comparison rather than an unconditional ranking: Certification can be highly standardized when a fixed external standard applies; rating/scoring is methodology-explicit but only moderately comparable across different scales; Verification is moderately standardized and can provide the most lifecycle information when it covers allocation, impacts, and reporting; and SPOs are generally more flexible, with lower-to-medium comparability. Information quantity ultimately depends on review scope, since ICMA expressly permits partial and full reviews [40][41].

ICMA’s 2018 Green Bond Principles identify four independent-review types—Second Party Opinion, Verification, Certification, and Green Bond Scoring/Rating [46]. **S/C/I** denotes criteria/process uniformity, cross-provider comparability, and information breadth/granularity; levels are a synthesis of the cited evidence [46][42].

**Second-party opinion (SPO).** It assesses an issuer’s framework and eligible assets, sometimes adding a qualitative rating. ESG firms, scientists, consultants, research institutes, Big Four divisions and specialists such as CICERO, Sustainalytics and DNV GL provide it. Its detailed project/sector analysis and targets/metrics are useful, but variable content and omitted metrics reduce comparability [84][87]. Audit-oversight evidence does not itself establish GBER activity [88].

**Certification.** Approved verifiers or certification bodies perform scheme-based conformity assessment, including HKQAA’s GFCS and an offshore ADBC example using China Energy Conservation and Environmental Protection Group. It signals scientific or sector compliance, but covers proceeds’ climate attributes—not creditworthiness or legal compliance—and costs can burden small or first-time issuers. CBI’s 2019 screen treated more than **5% of proceeds** for working capital, or insufficient use-of-proceeds information, as non-aligned; the GBP recommends but does not require certification [85][89][86][84].

**Verification.** Audit firms, consultancies and approved verifiers provide pre- or post-issuance third-party assurance at limited/negative or reasonable/positive levels. It supports independent credibility and proceeds monitoring/reporting, but reports may be private, variable or “tick-box.” A non-China sample of **800 reviewed versus 229 unreviewed bonds** found an **8-basis-point versus approximately 9-basis-point greenium**, an insignificant gap; association therefore does not establish causation [84][87][89][86].

**Rating/scoring.** Credit-rating agencies and ESG providers quantify project, sector or issuer sustainability, using scales such as CICERO Light–Dark, Moody’s GB1–GB5 and S&P E-1–E-4. Quantification improves legibility, mitigates information asymmetry and can standardize assets; differing objects and scales, issuer-pays conflicts, reliance and downgrade risks limit it [87][83].

The evidence reports inadequate standardization but no complete ordinal scores for comparability or information quantity. Unclear definitions and information asymmetry raise costs; templates, oversight, transparency, qualifications and conflict controls address greenwashing and adverse selection [101].

**SPO:** Independent pre-issuance opinion on framework/issuer alignment and sustainability credentials; it reviews eligibility, selection, proceeds and reporting and issues a report—not impact/allocation assurance [110][107]. A Chinese sample had 35 CBI versus 13 PBOC certifications; investors preferred PBOC, but spread effects were undetermined [103]. Issuer-pays, collusion and uneven competence threaten independence [104].

Internationally, SPOs assure eligible-project climate claims; verification/external review covers proceeds and greenwashing, while heterogeneous reporting limits comparability [117]. Thus formal scheme standardization is visible, but common cross-provider levels for standardization, comparability and information quantity remain unestablished. The evidence also does not show accounting firms or consultants as typical providers; a later US report instead notes shortages of third-party auditors and data and an August 2022 CSRC–MOF/PCAOB framework for public accounting firms [141].

China is segmented: PBOC/NAFMII guide the interbank market, CSRC oversees the exchange market, and NDRC regulates enterprise bonds. Green financial bonds require annual and quarterly UoP reports, with 31 August and 31 October deadlines; green corporate bonds require regular issuer reports and an annual trustee report [115].

**Green bond rating/scoring**

Green bond rating/scoring produces multi-level ordinal information: credit-rating agencies (CRAs) rate default/solvency, while green evaluation and certification agencies (GECAs) evaluate environmental benefits. They are complementary, non-substitutable functions before and during bond life [139]. Data-driven ratings seek to differentiate net environmental benefits and address absent premiums; major-firm systems provide alternative frameworks [140].

Benefits include directing capital to green projects and improving information under aligned incentives and constraints [139]. Risks include issuer-pays pressure for higher ratings/lower costs, inflated ratings, greenwashing, weak environmental differentiation and regulatory duplication or gaps [139]. A China sample of 327 bonds (2016–2021), reduced to 143, found incentives positively associated with ratings at 10% and certifications at 5%; CSRC constraints lowered ratings at 5% but not certification, while combined policies lowered ratings without significant certification effects [139]. The study reports AA-or-higher ratings for 99.22% of corporate green bonds in 2016–2022 and AAA ratings for 89% in 2021; it also reports 485 2021 bonds worth CNY6,075.42 billion and CNY279.27 billion of non-green use among 311 defined-use bonds (8.32%) [139]. Collusion is modeled, not observed: issuers may influence results through incentives and agencies may collude for profit; penalties and greater environmental weighting are proposed mitigations [139]. No common cross-provider levels are supplied for the three comparison dimensions [139]. General—not SPO-specific—evidence reports positive financial and environmental performance but pricing differentials and lower returns [134].

Evidence supports Certification as highest on standardization/comparability; Verification on lifecycle information. Climate Bonds Standard/sector criteria set eligibility, approved verifiers test compliance, and annual review maintains status [148][152]. Verification has ethical/report rules and pre/post checks, but ICMA mapping is voluntary/non-endorsing [150]. SPOs—auditors, ESG/scientific/environmental providers—assess frameworks/assets and may add ratings [149][152]; differing impact assumptions limit comparison [147]. Rating/scoring—rating agencies—gives a compact environmental signal separate from credit ratings [152]. China’s 2019 enterprise-bond standards differed from the 2015 catalogue for other regimes [151]; in 2018 Big Four >50% of reviewed-bond amount and 14% lacked review under NDRC’s non-mandatory guidance [149].

2019: SPOs=38% of Chinese reviews by volume [155]; 2018: Big Four >50% by review amount [154]. Certification: Climate Bonds criteria/approved verifiers [154]; verification: pre/post issuance [155].

Certification covers climate/use-of-proceeds attributes, not creditworthiness or legal compliance [156]. H1 2018: SPO/assurance covered 54% of issuance and certified deals 20%; 2019: four CBI-certified deals worth USD3bn [156][158]. These are analytical judgments, not universal source rankings; third-party verification was not compulsory in China [157]. By 2022-09-29, CGBSC had registered 18 institutions; it was self-regulatory, not statutory, and GBERs were third-party “gatekeepers” [160].

CGBSC was a standards/self-regulatory, not statutory, committee: its evaluation passed 2 June 2022; its 21 September register listed 18 institutions, with annual self-checks reported by 31 March [162]. NAFMII’s 2 November notice let 11 reviewers practise independently; 7 had to cite two experts [161]. An SPO is a descriptive framework review by an environmental specialist: credibility benefits are offset by issuer-assisted design and no post-issuance tracking, weakening independence/comparability [169]. Verification is China’s third-party form, supplied by accounting, rating and consulting institutions; H1 2019: 55 issues, 12 reviewers, 65% coverage; shares 38/36/20% (consultants/ratings/accountants), United Equator led [170]. Certification is conformity assessment by an approved, independent body [175].

Service boundaries are clear. A Second-party opinion (SPO) is an independent instrument/framework assessment of alignment with sustainability principles and expected sustainable-development contribution—not an issuer ESG or credit rating; it is usually point-in-time, with post-issuance review of allocation, reporting quality and framework consistency [176]. SPOs support communication, transparency and independent investor assessment [176]. Verification tests project eligibility, pre/post-issuance compliance, annual allocation and impact reports, and continuing reporting; EQA is a Climate Bonds Standard-approved verifier issuing reports [177]. Green-bond rating evaluates an issuance framework or use of proceeds [166], while Moody’s SPO includes an SQS from SQ1 (best) to SQ5 (worst) [176].

Certification is a standard-linked label: CBI certification is a voluntary Climate Bonds Standard label supported by approved reviewers [176]; a bond seeking CBS certification requires recognized third-party verification/certification [166]. China’s interbank guidance encouraged, rather than required, third-party assessment of green debt-financing instruments, with opinions and disclosure, and required competence, experience, credibility, impartiality, independence, consistency and completeness [168].

Provider categories overlap: Moody’s is a credit-rating/research firm offering SPOs; EQA is an ESG verifier/certifier offering SPO and verification [176][177]. Accounting firms provide independent audit assurance, distinct from an SPO [122]; CSRC’s 2015 evidence records 21 accounting-firm and 45 registered-accountant regulatory-action instances, but no green-bond GBER mandate [165]. Rating-agency research flags conflicts of interest and weak transparency [174]. International examples pair CICERO SPOs with Fannie Mae green frameworks in 2018 and 2020 [178].

ICMA guidance recommends pre-issuance review of use of proceeds, project selection, proceeds management and reporting, with credentials and scope disclosed [180]. China’s 2017 PBoC–CSRC interim guidelines covered assessment/certification; the 29 July 2022 China Green Bond Principles marked initial domestic unification and convergence, while China Merchants Bank listed 2021–2022 post-issuance certification and proceeds-use assurance reports [164][181][179]. Credit-rating agencies are rating/scoring providers; among 7,941 Chinese local-government bonds (2015–2021), reputable agencies were associated with over 2% lower risk premiums, but this is not green-bond evidence; CPA safeguards include independent review, disclosure and consent, while GBER-conflict prevalence is unestablished [82][173].

Accounting firms: China services and 2022 CSRC–MOF/PCAOB protocol, not GBER-specific [167]. Consulting firms: NDRC may solicit consultants in ratification cases, potential conflict; no 2022 GBER status shown [167]. Credit-rating agencies: ratings/info certification; issuer-pays/ancillary/rating-shopping can bias ratings and investor information; PBOC/CSRC/NAFMII gate access; Dagong sanctioned Aug 2018 [90]. CGBSC: 18 agencies designated Sep 2022; anti-greenwashing role rose Nov 2022 [163][171]

Using a common ordinal rubric—low when reviewer methods or criteria vary, medium when a methodology is defined but scheme- or provider-bound, and high when criteria, procedures, and reporting are externally prescribed—Second-party opinion is low, Certification is high within its selected scheme, Verification is high under CBI but less uniform where guidance is voluntary, and Green bond rating/scoring is low across providers because green-project definitions and review standardization remain weak [147][114][61][47][101].

On a common comparability rubric—low means weak cross-issuer comparison, medium means comparability mainly within a scheme or provider, and high means standardized outputs—Verification is relatively high and Certification high within a defined scheme, while Second-party opinion is low because issuers make different use-of-proceeds assumptions and Green bond rating/scoring is low across providers because the objects and methodologies differ [146][175][147][101][87].

On information quantity and granularity, Second-party opinion is high and broad across the framework and eligible assets, Certification is high and lifecycle-detailed, Verification is high and granular across eligibility, allocation, monitoring and reporting, and Green bond rating/scoring is high but concentrates information into quantified environmental differentiation [152][114][61][42].

ICMA’s Guidelines for External Reviews distinguish Second Party Opinion, Verification, Certification, and Green Bond Scoring/Rating [40]. Green Bond External Reviewers are third-party institutions whose work can span pre-issuance, post-issuance, and regular proceeds reporting [78][159]: SPOs provide specialist framework or greenness opinions, Verification checks designated criteria, Certification tests conformity with a recognized label through qualified parties, and rating/scoring applies a data-driven environmental methodology [54][61][140]; on a common low/medium/high rubric, Certification and CBI Verification are high within their schemes, while SPO and rating/scoring are less uniform, leaving comparability and greenwashing risks [41][101].

Green Bond External Reviewers are third-party professional institutions that assess and certify whether green-bond proceeds are green [159]. In China’s landscape, SPOs can provide a Sustainability Quality Score from SQ1, best, to SQ5, worst [176], Certification is a legally qualified conformity assessment under unified supervision [130][175], Verification covers project eligibility and pre- and post-issuance compliance and reporting [177], rating/scoring uses credit-rating expertise, and accounting firms are provider categories [123][170]; 12 institutions were recorded in the first half of 2019 [170]. These functions reduce information-search costs [111], but nonstandard SPO assumptions constrain comparison [147], making competition and independence safeguards important.

The ICMA/GBP framework separates SPO, Certification, and Verification as distinct external-review roles [40]; SPO practice can attach an SQS from SQ1, best, to SQ5, worst, but methods are not standardized [176][147], Certification tests a recognized label through qualified accredited parties [54][57], and the CBI Taxonomy calls for third-party Verification while one Chinese rule encouraged rather than mandated external review [44][168]. These distinctions make all three relevant to China’s GBER landscape, but scheme- and provider-dependence creates concentration, comparability, and independence risks, so no single output should be treated as complete continuing assurance.

Verification is a criteria-based third-party gate under the CBI Taxonomy, but Chinese external review was encouraged rather than mandatory [44][168]; Green bond rating/scoring is data-driven and more granular than a binary label [140][42], while unclear project definitions weaken standardization and comparability [101]. SPOs were the most common review type and represented 38% of Chinese-issuer external reviews by volume in 2019 [155], and their methods vary [147], so Green Bond External Reviewers should be treated as complementary functions with standardized post-issuance checks rather than interchangeable assurance.

A market-oligopoly risk is plausible for Second-party opinion and Certification: SPOs are widely used while provider methodologies vary [38][147], and Certification operates through qualified, supervised bodies and defined schemes [175], which can narrow entry and amplify one provider’s judgment. Conflicts can arise because an SPO relies on recipient data [74] and certification rules must protect objectivity, independence, funding, and client relationships [175]; if those controls fail, green claims become less comparable and investor confidence suffers, whereas public review and follow-up suspension or revocation are mitigants [38][175][111].

Certification’s qualified-provider and continuing-compliance structure [114][175] and Verification’s approved-verifier, lifecycle controls [61][135][99] can reduce greenwashing, but scheme-specific access and variable engagement scope create concentration and coverage risks. Conflict arises if issuer–verifier relationships compromise independence; ICMA guidance is voluntary and external review is useful but not sufficient [47], so weak review can fail to expose ineligible projects or allocation and reporting problems, making independent evidence-based procedures and follow-up essential [61][175].

Verification spans eligibility, allocation, tracking, and post-issuance reporting [61][135][99], but Chinese bond types have inconsistent reporting and verification requirements, producing varying transparency [144]; rating/scoring is data-driven [140], and credit-rating agencies and green evaluation and certification agencies perform complementary, non-substitutable roles [139]. Accounting firms add audit-based verification capacity [112], yet unclear project definitions raise uncertainty and costs [101], so provider concentration or combined commercial roles can transmit conflicts and misclassification; standardized data and independent oversight are the implication.

At the 2022 cutoff [167], accounting firms could act as green-bond Verification providers [113], while reported credit-rating market shares were 80 per cent in 2017 and roughly 85 per cent during 2008–2014 [90]; expertise and economic-power entry barriers [90] and the issuer-pays model [142] create oligopoly, rating-shopping, and conflict mechanisms. If an audit or verification provider is commercially dependent on an issuer, or a rating agency favors the fee payer, inflated or less reliable assessments can misallocate green capital and weaken investor protection; public involvement and reputable CRA information provide counterweights [106][172][182].

Credit-rating agencies’ expertise and economic-power barriers [90], issuer-pays model, limited numbers, and rating shopping [142] make oligopoly and conflict risks mutually reinforcing; they can favor issuers and inflate ratings, transmitting unreliable risk signals, although reputation and public oversight improve stringency [172][106]. Consulting firms supply Verification, Certification, and SPO capacity [65][98][131], but nonuniform SPO methods [65] and advisory relationships can fragment comparability or create conflicts, so role separation and common disclosure are needed.

Consulting firms are possible independent auditing, SPO, and Verification providers [131][98][65], while certification bodies can operate under unified standards and third-party processes [131][143]; this expands capacity, but incomplete standards and certification systems can enable greenwashing [131]. Through the cutoff, concentration among recognized gatekeepers is therefore an oligopoly risk and issuer-linked consulting or certification work is a conflict risk; independent providers and common methods are the needed mitigants [127][100].

The ICMA Guidelines distinguish four separate GBER types: Second-party opinion (an independent, usually pre-issuance assessment of a bond or framework); Verification (independent testing against designated process or environmental criteria); Certification (conformity testing against a recognised external standard or label); and Green bond rating/scoring (methodology-based evaluation of a bond, framework or use of proceeds, distinct from a credit rating) [40][55].

Under a common ordinal rubric—high means more uniform, cross-issuer-comparable or information-rich output and low the reverse—with values ordered as standardization/comparability/information, Second-party opinion is low/low/high because provider methods and issuer impact assumptions vary [147][152]; Certification is high-but-scheme-specific/high within a scheme/high [54]; Verification is high/relatively high/high where standardized criteria and reports apply, subject to assurance level and scope [146]; and Green bond rating/scoring is low/low/high because provider scales differ despite quantified detail [140][42].

The ICMA Guidelines and Green Bond Principles establish the taxonomy—Second Party Opinion, Verification, Certification, and Green/Social/Sustainability Bond Scoring/Rating [40][46]. Some GBP research calls SPO “Consultant Review,” shortens Scoring/Rating to “Rating,” or uses “assurance” broadly; the substantive outputs remain an alignment opinion, criteria verification, standard/label certification and methodology-based scoring, while the Climate Bonds Taxonomy requires third-party verification and Climate Bonds Standard version 3.0 is an external basis, so the labels should not be treated as interchangeable [62][70][44][55].

Apply one rubric to all four: standardization means uniformity of criteria, procedure and output; comparability means ease of cross-issuer/provider comparison; information quantity means breadth and granularity of framework, project, proceeds, reporting and impact evidence; the basis is the review output rather than creditworthiness. High/Medium/Low mean strong/intermediate/weak, with scheme-specific and scope-dependent results as caveats; the resulting values are Second-party opinion low/low/high, Certification high-but-scheme-specific/high within scheme/high, Verification high/relatively high/high, and Green bond rating/scoring low/low/high [40][147][54][146][140][42].

China-focused research should treat Second-party opinions as transaction/framework-level pre-issuance alignment and use-of-proceeds reviews, including a 5-point Sustainability Quality Score; Verification tests project eligibility, proceeds controls, post-issuance compliance and reporting, with 12 institutions reported in the first half of 2019; and Certification tests conformity with an external standard plus follow-up, so these functions address credibility, monitoring and investor information [123][58][176][177][170][175]. Green bond rating/scoring and the provider map—Accounting firms, Credit rating agencies, Consulting firms and Certification bodies—indicate market capacity: accounting firms and regulated rating agencies are provider categories, consulting firms supply sustainability-review expertise, traditional agencies may offer green ratings without evidence that a rating proves green performance, and certification bodies face qualification, objectivity and follow-up requirements [169][170][123][111][130][175].

## 2. China GBER Market Landscape

### Role and market status at the end of 2022

**Review activity had substantial reach:** In 2022, external review covered 81% of volume and 68% of deal count within CBI-aligned Chinese issuance. SPOs were the most common method in both onshore and offshore markets. Separately, five onshore deals received Climate Bonds Certification: two from Agricultural Bank of China and three from China Development Bank, totaling USD7.0 billion, or RMB47.2 billion. [2]

**Reviewed transactions were larger on average:** The difference between volume and deal coverage suggests that external review was more prevalent in larger transactions. An estimate using the rounded coverage figures gives an average reviewed deal approximately twice the size of an unreviewed deal: `(81/68) ÷ (19/32) ≈ 2.0`. This describes the issuance mix; it does not establish that transaction size itself caused issuers to purchase reviews.

**The relevant market was sizeable:** Chinese issuers sold USD155 billion of labelled green bonds domestically and offshore in 2022. Of that amount, USD85.4 billion met CBI’s definition. Applying the reported 81% coverage to the latter gives an estimated USD69.2 billion of externally reviewed CBI-aligned issuance: `USD85.4 billion × 81%`. This is reviewed financing volume, not the revenues earned by reviewers. [2]

**GBERs translate claims into evidence:** Their core role is to assess whether projects qualify as green, whether selection procedures are credible, whether proceeds can be tracked, and whether reporting adequately informs investors. The China Green Bond Principles recommend an independent written assessment explaining alignment with all four core components and encourage continuing evaluation of project progress and environmental benefits. [12]

**Credibility depends on reducing information gaps:** Issuers know more than investors about their project portfolios, accounting systems and operational performance. A competent reviewer can investigate these matters once and communicate the results to many investors, reducing duplicated due diligence. This function is especially important for cross-border investors navigating different languages, taxonomies and disclosure practices. IIGF accordingly describes assessment and certification bodies as green bond market gatekeepers. [13]

**Recognition and use remained distinct:** The September 2022 registration of 18 institutions strengthened the recognized provider pool. Actual market coverage nevertheless depended on issuer decisions, product-specific requirements and the availability of public review information. Registration thus improved the institutional structure without making every green bond pass through the same independent review process.

### Regulatory bodies and the bonds they supervise

**Four channels govern the requested categories:** PBOC supervises green financial bonds; NDRC supervised green enterprise bonds at the cutoff; CSRC and the exchanges oversee green corporate bonds; and NAFMII administers green debt financing instruments under PBOC guidance. The distinction between “enterprise bonds” and “corporate bonds” is a Chinese regulatory distinction, despite the terms’ similar ordinary English meanings. [14]

Likewise, CGBSC registration is a reviewer-governance mechanism, not a substitute for product issuance or listing supervision. CPI’s regulatory mapping and CBI–CCDC’s bond classification distinguish these functions. [15][16]

**Instrument classification matters more than ownership:** An SOE can issue through different financing channels, and enterprise bonds should not be treated as synonymous with all SOE borrowing. For green-review obligations, the decisive questions are the instrument category, applicable rulebook, issuance date and commitments in the offering documents.

**Historical segmentation explains regulatory differences:** Separate authorities developed green requirements within their existing bond markets. PBOC’s foundational financial-bond announcement dates from 22 December 2015; NDRC’s issuance guidance was dated 31 December 2015 and published in January 2016. CSRC and NAFMII followed with their respective 2017 guidance. Different proceeds thresholds, account controls and reporting frequencies emerged from those separate channels. [17][18]

### Regulatory requirements across the four bond categories

**The end-2022 comparison requires dates:** The China Green Bond Principles, released on 29 July 2022, set a 100% green-use-of-proceeds approach. ICMA’s September analysis records PBOC and CSRC support and the exchanges’ implementation of 100% for new green corporate issuance. For enterprise bonds, the appropriate comparison retains the cited NDRC legacy regime while separately identifying what conformity with the China Principles requires. [14]

| Bond category | Green proceeds allocation | Special-account management | Fund-use disclosure | External green review |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Green Financial Bonds | 100% | Specialized account; traceable tracking | Quarterly; annual use report and special audit by 30 April | Independent evaluation/certification encouraged |

**The table combines operative rule layers:** The allocation comparison follows CPI and ICMA; account and disclosure provisions follow the PBOC, CSRC and NAFMII materials. These layers should be read together rather than treating the 2022 Principles as having automatically rewritten every earlier product rule. [11][19][20]

**Financial bonds combine frequent reporting controls:** PBOC’s regime requires quarterly disclosure of proceeds use and an annual prior-year use report accompanied by a special audit report by 30 April. NAFMII’s January 2022 monitoring notice reaffirmed these obligations and reported that some issuers’ disclosures were nonstandard despite generally timely reporting. Thus, a comparatively developed reporting regime still required attention to content quality. [21]

**Large financial-bond projects receive scrutiny:** PBOC’s 2018 continuing-supervision provisions require individual project disclosure for projects in the top 10% by allocation, projects receiving at least RMB50 million, or projects accounting for at least 1% of outstanding green financial-bond scale. Other projects can be summarized by category. These thresholds aim to make major allocations visible without requiring identical presentation for every small loan. [22]

**Mandatory audit differs from optional greenness review:** The annual special audit of financial-bond proceeds is a required control. It should not be conflated with a universal requirement to obtain an SPO or an independent pre-issuance assessment of the bond’s environmental credentials. “External review encouraged” therefore does not mean that every externally performed reporting or audit function was optional.

**Enterprise bonds retain the key distinction:** Under the cited NDRC regime, up to 50% of proceeds could repay bank loans or supplement working capital, implying a minimum 50% allocation to the designated green purposes. CPI’s regulator-specific comparison lists no additional green-specific special-account, reporting or verification requirement for this category. Zhang’s ADBI comparison and the 2022 legal analysis similarly distinguish NDRC’s limited green-specific reporting provisions from the more elaborate financial-bond regime. [17][23]

**Principles conformity imposes a higher benchmark:** A green enterprise bond represented as conforming to the China Green Bond Principles must meet their 100% green-use requirement, account-or-ledger tracking and annual disclosure provisions. That commitment should be assessed separately from the legacy regulatory minimum. A definitive NDRC adoption instrument, with an effective date and transition arrangements, is the condition that would justify treating the Principles’ requirements as an across-the-board replacement of the enterprise-bond baseline.

**Corporate bonds tightened allocation requirements:** The legacy comparison allowed up to 30% of proceeds for loan repayment or working capital outside the fully green allocation requirement. Under the CSRC-supported 2022 transition, new green corporate issuance moved to 100% green use. Existing bonds should still be assessed against the requirements and commitments governing their issuance rather than having their contractual history rewritten retrospectively. [14]

**Corporate-bond trustees provide continuing supervision:** CSRC guidance requires a dedicated proceeds account and diligent trustee oversight of proceeds use and account management. Disclosure addresses allocation, green-project progress and environmental benefits, including through the trustee’s annual management report. Independent professional assessment before issuance and during the bond’s life is encouraged, rather than expressed as an unconditional requirement in that guidance. [19]

**DFIs combine full allocation with supervision:** NAFMII’s rules require all green DFI proceeds to support green-project construction, operation, associated working capital or repayment of green loans. The dedicated-account model includes a supervision agreement. Its general proceeds-management regulation, effective 1 December 2022, also specifies separation of proceeds and look-through supervision by the regulatory bank where such accounts are established. [24][25]

**DFI reporting follows a defined schedule:** Prior-year proceeds use and project progress are disclosed by 30 April, and first-half information by 31 August. Changes in proceeds use require at least five working days’ advance notice. The 2022 special-product guide lists the assessment or certification report “if any,” reinforcing the distinction between mandatory proceeds controls and encouraged independent green review. [20][26]

**Green working capital remains eligible:** A 100% green-use rule does not prohibit every form of working-capital expenditure or refinancing. The China Principles expressly allow working capital associated with green projects and repayment of green-project interest-bearing debt. The relevant distinction is between demonstrably green-project-related financing and unrestricted general corporate use. [12]

**Traceability is broader than bank segregation:** The Principles allow a proceeds-supervision account or a dedicated ledger, require tracking of receipts, disbursements and recoveries, and require annual disclosure of allocation, project progress and expected or actual environmental benefits. They permit approved temporary investment in secure, liquid products for single periods of no more than 12 months and require records to be retained for at least two years after maturity. These provisions provide concrete matters for a reviewer to test. [12]

**The regulatory judgment for investors:** Identify the bond category, issuance date and stated alignment commitments before assessing reviewer obligations; the 2022 reforms substantially narrowed differences without eliminating the need for category-specific analysis.

## 3. Market Challenges and Risks

### Main challenges beyond the provider count

**Disclosure quality remained the central weakness:** The strongest market diagnosis is not that green bonds lacked all reporting, but that reporting often failed to connect a particular bond, its allocations and its environmental results. That weakens the information on which reviewers and investors depend. Historical document studies and the retrospective 2022 disclosure assessment demonstrate different parts of this problem. [11][6]

| Evidence period | Population and test | Result | Significance for GBERs |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPI review published in 2020 | 387 issuances; any public information | 38 lacked public information; 9.8%, calculated | Basic evidence access was uneven |
| Issuance before September 2018 | 270 issuances; both pre- and post-issuance documents on issuer websites | 32 met the test; 11.9%, calculated | Lifecycle documentation was difficult to follow |
| Through 31 December 2021 | 530 reports covering issuers with multiple outstanding green financial bonds | 444 lacked bond-by-bond differentiation; 83.8%, calculated | Allocation and impact attribution were blurred |

**The percentages have different meanings:** The first three calculated shares use the reported counts: `38/387`, `32/270` and `444/530`. The 2022 figures measure pre-issuance environmental-benefit disclosure and indicator completeness, rather than continuing independent review. Together they show why “a report exists” is a much weaker test than “the report supplies a complete, traceable evidence chain.” [11][23]

**High reporting rates provide an important counterexample:** CBI’s study of Chinese bonds issued before November 2017 found proceeds-use reporting for 85% of bonds by number and 93% by amount. This does not contradict CPI’s much lower rate for paired pre- and post-issuance documents on issuer websites: the studies use different populations, locations and tests. A bond can have an allocation report while still lacking a readily accessible pair of review documents or sufficiently granular environmental information. [9]

**Reporting aggregation can conceal allocation questions:** Banks often finance many eligible projects through several outstanding green bonds. Portfolio-level reporting can be efficient, but investors still need enough reconciliation to understand which funding supports which assets. If several bonds rely on the same broad portfolio description, it becomes harder to assess allocation completeness or guard against double counting of environmental benefits.

**Taxonomy alignment improved without becoming identical:** The jointly issued 2021 Green Bond Endorsed Project Catalogue removed clean-coal and related high-carbon fossil-energy activities, addressing a prominent historical disagreement. This means older descriptions of Chinese green bonds financing clean coal should not be presented as the unchanged end-2022 green-bond standard. At the same time, broader green-industry and green-credit classifications had not necessarily made identical adjustments. [14][30]

**International screening remained a separate exercise:** CBI updated its database methodology in July 2022, tightening criteria in areas including buildings and transport. The retrospective market report explains that the Chinese green-building certification threshold accepted for database inclusion moved from two stars to three. Consequently, a difference between domestic labelling and CBI inclusion can reflect a different environmental threshold, rather than misconduct by the issuer or reviewer. [6]

**Technical capacity affects review credibility:** Measurement, reporting and verification—MRV—requires more than identifying a project category. Reviewing emissions reductions may require operational data, a clearly stated baseline and defensible assumptions about the activity displaced. CPI identifies inconsistent reporting, uneven review quality and the technical demands of environmental monitoring as contributors to issuance time and cost. [11]

**Allocation and impact establish different facts:** Evidence that money financed an eligible asset does not, by itself, establish the amount of environmental benefit achieved. Expected benefits can differ from realized performance, and permitted refinancing does not itself prove that the bond caused additional environmental improvements. A credible reviewer should keep eligibility, allocation and impact conclusions distinguishable.

**Costs can reinforce unequal access:** Smaller or less experienced issuers may have fewer resources for project data, specialist staff and continuing reporting. China’s broader market literature also identifies under-utilization of green-bond financing by private enterprises. Improving review standards therefore involves a trade-off: stronger scrutiny should increase credibility without making access depend unnecessarily on issuer size or pre-existing institutional relationships. [34]

**The broader challenge judgment:** China’s principal remaining task was to make green eligibility, allocation and environmental results traceable across a bond’s life, rather than treating provider recognition or publication of an initial opinion as sufficient evidence of integrity.

### Market oligopoly risk: causes and potential harm

**Concentration creates a plausible risk channel:** The 2018–2019 rankings put approximately half the reported review activity in the hands of two firms, although the leading pair changed. That is meaningful evidence of concentration. The appropriate question is how such a structure could affect entry, pricing and review quality—not whether the concentration ratios alone prove a legal oligopoly.

**Reputation creates a strong entry advantage:** Investors cannot fully observe review quality before purchasing a bond. Issuers consequently have incentives to choose firms whose names are already recognized by arrangers and investors. Established accounting firms and specialist brands can win mandates partly because their opinions are easier for the financing chain to accept.

**Reputation also has a legitimate function:** Reliance on an experienced provider can reduce investors’ assessment costs and give the provider an incentive to protect its name. The concern arises when recognition becomes difficult for capable entrants to obtain, or when investors use brand acceptance as a substitute for reading the actual report. A reputational advantage is not automatically anticompetitive; its effect depends on whether market participants continue to compare substantive quality.

**Large issuer relationships reinforce scale:** Deloitte’s two large Industrial Bank mandates in 2018 and CICERO’s ICBC-related volume in 2019 show the importance of anchor clients. Repeat frameworks and recurring issuance can give a reviewer a substantial volume share without requiring a large number of new client wins. These relationships can be efficient, but they can also make market entry difficult for providers outside established issuance networks.

**Technical investment favors established organizations:** Sector expertise, assurance systems, environmental data and compliance processes entail costs before a firm wins its next engagement. Larger organizations can spread those costs across more clients and services. Smaller entrants must therefore offer sufficient differentiation to overcome both investors’ preference for established names and the economics of maintaining specialist capacity.

**Registration can improve quality and entry barriers:** CGBSC’s evaluation considers staffing, qualifications, report quality, internal controls and prior work. These are sensible competence safeguards, particularly given the concerns about inexperienced entrants. They can nevertheless favor incumbents if past scale becomes disproportionately important or if maintaining eligibility imposes costs unrelated to actual review quality. [27]

**Bundling can reinforce incumbent advantages:** A large provider may offer accounting, credit assessment, sustainability consulting and green review within a broader commercial relationship. Bundling can reduce coordination costs for an issuer. It can also make it harder for a specialist entrant to compete for the green-review component on its merits. The historical Chinese market discussion specifically identifies business bundling and low-price contracting as problematic competitive practices. [8]

**Market segmentation can produce local dominance:** The aggregate national ranking combines accounting assurance, SPOs, ratings and certification verification, as well as onshore and offshore business. A provider with a modest overall share could still be difficult to replace within a particular product or sector. Conversely, a large aggregate share may reflect a few major transactions rather than broad power across every service.

**Pricing risks run in two directions:** Dominant firms with few credible substitutes may be able to charge more or offer less responsive service. At the same time, concentrated networks can coexist with aggressive price cutting for particular mandates. A low headline review fee may attract an issuer while leaving insufficient resources for site work, technical challenge or post-issuance follow-up. The problem is therefore weak quality-adjusted competition, not simply high prices.

**Methodological complacency can reduce challenge:** If a provider expects to retain business primarily because its name is accepted, it may have less incentive to improve analytical methods or challenge familiar issuer practices. Several dominant firms may also rely on similar assumptions and templates, reducing the diversity of scrutiny applied to a sector. These are mechanisms through which concentration could weaken environmental assessment, rather than observed losses inferred from the share tables.

**Errors become consequential at greater scale:** A weak eligibility interpretation used by a reviewer with substantial market coverage can affect many bonds. If investors later reconsider that interpretation, the reputational consequences can extend beyond one issuer to a category or label. This is a credibility transmission channel; it should not be equated automatically with the systemic default mechanisms associated with conventional credit-rating downgrades.

**Domestic gains challenge a static-oligopoly account:** Lianhe Equator’s growth, CICERO’s advance and Deloitte’s reduced share show that market positions could change. The 2022 recognized pool also contained multiple institutional types. These facts weigh against portraying China’s market as an unchanging accounting-firm duopoly.

**More entry alone is insufficient:** Competition improves outcomes when investors and issuers reward rigorous methods and credible continuing work. If issuers mainly seek a favorable opinion at the lowest price, additional entry can instead intensify competition in permissiveness. Ba’s market discussion links inexperienced entry and low-price contracting with substantive weaknesses in some reports. [8]

**The oligopoly-risk judgment:** Concentration was a material concern because of reputational barriers, technical scale and established client relationships; the concern would diminish with sustained entry, declining comparable concentration and independent evidence that firms compete on review depth, methodological quality and continuing scrutiny.

### Conflicts of interest: incentives, safeguards and consequences

**Issuer payment creates the basic tension:** The entity whose green claims are being assessed commonly engages the reviewer. Investors receive much of the informational benefit, but the issuer usually controls the immediate commercial relationship. Berensmann’s 2017 analysis identifies issuer payment as a central conflict mechanism for SPO providers. [5]

**A conflict is not proven misconduct:** Payment by the issuer creates an incentive that needs management; it does not establish that a particular opinion is biased. Issuer-funded publication also makes a review accessible to a broad investor audience. The useful assessment is therefore whether the commercial relationship can influence analytical decisions and whether organizational controls prevent that influence.

**Repeat mandates can magnify dependence:** A reviewer may anticipate future work from an issuer, financial group or arranger. A demanding conclusion could delay issuance, require an asset to be removed, or make the bond less attractive to investors. Commercial staff may therefore prefer accommodating interpretations even where an analyst’s professional responsibility points toward a more critical assessment.

**Large clients make the trade-off sharper:** The 2019 CICERO–ICBC volumes illustrate how one issuer can represent a substantial portion of a provider’s reported activity in a particular market. This is evidence of client concentration, not evidence that the resulting SPO was compromised. It nevertheless identifies the kind of dependency that a reviewer’s internal governance should assess.

**Opinion shopping exploits methodological differences:** Where providers use different definitions, thresholds and disclosure expectations, an issuer can seek the most accommodating combination. This may involve selecting among legitimate analytical approaches, or it may become a means of avoiding scrutiny. The absence of a common SPO methodology makes it especially important that investors understand why a reviewer reached its conclusion.

**Advisory overlap creates self-review risk:** A provider that helps design a framework may later be asked to assess that framework. Commercial familiarity can make it harder to challenge assumptions or acknowledge weaknesses in prior advice. ICMA’s 2022 guidelines directly address this issue: the SPO institution should be independent of the framework adviser, or appropriate procedures such as information barriers should protect independence, with concerns disclosed to investors. [3]

**Broader service relationships add incentives:** Green review may sit alongside consulting, assurance, credit assessment or data services. A small green-review engagement can be commercially important because of those other relationships. The Chinese market discussion’s evidence of bundling is therefore relevant to independence even though bundling can also produce legitimate efficiencies.

**Low fees can weaken work indirectly:** A reviewer may preserve a formally independent conclusion while accepting an engagement budget that supports insufficient investigation. Limited staff time can reduce document testing, site work and examination of impact assumptions. This is a quality-risk mechanism distinct from deliberately issuing a misleading opinion.

**Ownership requires transaction-specific assessment:** Common control, investments or relationships with the reviewed issuer can create further conflicts. State affiliation alone, however, is not a sufficient basis for finding compromised independence; neither does private ownership eliminate issuer-payment incentives. The relevant questions concern the actual economic relationship and the ability of commercial interests to affect the engagement.

**An adjacent Chinese case is instructive:** Sheng’s 2019 analysis records that NAFMII and CSRC suspended Dagong Global’s domestic credit-rating business for one year in August 2018 following findings involving consulting services to rated companies, fees and governance deficiencies. This was a credit-rating case, not a green-bond-review finding. It supplies a concrete Chinese illustration of why advisory relationships and assessment functions require separation, without attributing that conduct to the separately registered Dagong Low Carbon green-review institution. [28]

**Institutional payment models can differ:** Sheng also describes China Bond Rating as having been established with an investor-pays model for credit ratings. That is a useful counterexample to assuming every institution has the same commercial structure. The green-review engagement itself still needs to be understood on its own terms; a parent institution’s credit-rating business model should not automatically be assigned to every environmental-review service. [28]

### Independence safeguards and their practical reach

**China had meaningful conduct controls:** The PBOC–CSRC interim conduct framework introduced common requirements for assessment and certification, followed by committee rules addressing professional capacity and registration. The 2022 recognized pool and annual self-check obligations represented institutional progress beyond the market’s earlier, less coordinated arrangements. [35][1]

**Professional principles apply across provider types:** ICMA’s June 2022 guidelines call for integrity, objectivity, competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behavior. Reports should identify scope, credentials, methodology, conclusions and independence or conflict policies. Accounting firms and regulated rating agencies may also operate under existing professional or regulatory requirements, while other providers have different institutional starting points. [3]

**NAFMII specifies substantive assessment work:** Its green DFI assessment template calls for independence, objectivity and fairness, and a commitment to the truth and accuracy of report statements and responsibility for the reasonableness of conclusions. It describes document review, interviews and sample on-site investigation, with at least one suitably qualified professional signing the report. These provisions establish an evidential and accountability basis beyond a generic independence declaration. [24]

**Safeguards address different parts of risk:** The table distinguishes established controls from the practical questions investors should use to judge their effectiveness. The final column is an analytical decision test, not an additional statement of Chinese law.

| Conflict channel | Safeguard in the cited framework | Residual incentive | Practical decision test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Framework advice and review | ICMA independence or information barriers | Reluctance to criticize prior advice | Are advisory roles and analytical separation disclosed? |
| Commercial dependence | Integrity and objectivity requirements | Pressure to retain mandates | Can analysts challenge clients without sales intervention? |
| Inadequate professional capacity | Registration and competence assessment | Fee pressure may reduce work | Do scope and procedures match project complexity? |
| Weak report accountability | NAFMII responsibility statements and professional sign-off | Formal attestations may exceed practical testing | What evidence supports the conclusion? |
| Continuing allocation changes | Encouraged post-issuance review and disclosure | Scrutiny may fade after issuance | Are subsequent allocation and impact claims independently tested? |

**Self-checks improve governance but differ from inspection:** Annual self-review can identify inconsistent practices and document responsibility within the institution. Its effectiveness nevertheless depends on candor, internal authority and external consequences. A requirement to submit a self-check is therefore a useful control, but should not be treated as equivalent to an independent assessment of every engagement’s quality.

**Timing determines the exposure left open:** A reviewer may be independent when assessing a framework, yet no longer be engaged when proceeds are allocated or projects encounter problems. Conversely, a well-defined continuing verification engagement can test behavior after the commercial success of issuance has already occurred. The 2022 Principles’ encouragement of periodic follow-up recognizes the importance of this stage. [12]

**The control judgment for practitioners:** Independence should be assessed through engagement terms, service separation, analytical authority, evidence and follow-up—not inferred solely from a provider’s brand or the presence of a standard declaration.

### Potential harm to investors and market credibility

**Weak reviews can admit weak claims:** An accommodating reviewer may accept broad project descriptions, unclear working-capital links or insufficiently supported environmental assumptions. Investors may then interpret a favorable opinion as stronger evidence than the engagement actually supplies. This is how a conflict can move from a private commercial relationship into market-wide information quality.

**Greenwashing can take several forms:** The most direct is proceeds being diverted from the promised eligible purposes. Other forms involve overstating benefits, obscuring adverse effects or implying a more demanding standard than the report tested. Chinese legal analysis published in 2022 describes proceeds-direction changes, environmental penalties affecting supported projects and inappropriate alternative uses of funds as reasons for stronger continuing disclosure and scrutiny. [23]

**Permitted flexibility needs careful interpretation:** Temporary investment of idle funds is not automatically diversion, because the China Principles allow approved cash management within defined conditions. Likewise, green-project refinancing and related working capital can be eligible uses. The reviewer’s task is to test compliance with those conditions rather than equating every non-construction expenditure with misuse. [12]

**Non-comparability raises investor costs:** When similarly favorable opinions reflect different scrutiny, investors must reconstruct the analysis themselves. That reduces the value of external review as a shared information service. It can also disadvantage a rigorously assessed issuer if a weaker competitor obtains an equally marketable conclusion through a less demanding process.

**Confidence can weaken before default occurs:** A bond can remain financially sound while becoming unsuitable for an investor’s environmental mandate. Loss of confidence in its green credentials can therefore affect purchase decisions or willingness to hold it even when its credit rating is unchanged. The environmental-review function and credit-risk function must remain analytically separate.

**Survey responses show disclosure sensitivity:** CBI’s China investor survey received 42 responses from 89 invited institutions, comprising 26 domestic and 16 international respondents. Of the 42, 26 said they would not buy without knowing where proceeds were invested, and 32 said they would sell following insufficient post-issuance disclosure. Calculated from those counts, the shares are 61.9% and 76.2%, respectively. These are stated preferences, not observed selling or an estimated price impact. [26]

**Concentration and conflicts can reinforce one another:** A dominant provider may be difficult to replace even if investors question its methods. A commercially dependent provider may also avoid a critical conclusion to preserve a major client relationship. Yet vigorous competition can create its own permissiveness problem when issuers reward favorable opinions. The common issue is whether market rewards and oversight support accurate, challenging assessment.

**The conflict-risk judgment:** Issuer-funded review can deliver valuable public information, but its credibility depends on separating advice from assessment, protecting analytical decisions and sustaining post-issuance scrutiny; effective conflict disclosures, independent quality checks and demonstrated enforcement would materially strengthen that judgment.

**Measurement and historical scope:** Status and regulatory judgments refer to 31 December 2022. The 2022 issuance results are retrospective, using CBI’s 20 January 2023 database snapshot without importing later product changes. Provider shares combine review types, while registration establishes recognized practice eligibility and named transactions separately demonstrate service use. Chinese legal names control identification; English names are descriptive. Enterprise-bond entries distinguish the legacy regime from China-Principles conformity, and derived volumes, concentration ratios and respondent percentages are identified as calculations.

**The overall end-2022 assessment:** China had built a substantial external-review market and a recognizable provider-governance structure. Its next credibility gain depended less on adding labels than on ensuring that recognized reviewers delivered independent, technically grounded and continuing evidence linking each bond’s promises to its actual financing and environmental results.
