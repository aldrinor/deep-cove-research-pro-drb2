# MindBridge and the Transformation of Auditing: An Early-2024 Report

**MindBridge is an early, recognized AI-audit specialist.** Founded in Ottawa in 2015, MindBridge Analytics Inc. develops financial-analysis software that combines business rules, statistical methods, and machine learning to identify unusual transactions and prioritize financial risk. Its flagship cloud platform, MindBridge AI, analyzes entire supplied datasets rather than limiting its initial screening to a sample. By **31 March 2024**, its most consequential audit-industry relationship was a confirmed strategic alliance with KPMG, integrating MindBridge analytics into KPMG Clara, the network’s digital-audit platform. [1][2]

**Its strongest contribution is better-directed audit work.** MindBridge can broaden transaction screening, explain risk indicators, and help auditors concentrate investigation on higher-risk activity. Customer examples demonstrate plausible efficiency gains, including an illustrative reduction from 384 sample items to 252. The important distinction is that examining every supplied transaction means comprehensive analytical coverage—not that every error is detected or every audit assertion is proven. The platform changes how auditors find and investigate risk while leaving professional judgment and the final audit conclusion with people. [3]

**The report follows five questions.** It establishes the company’s identity and development, explains how the product turns financial data into auditor actions, evaluates its benefits and distinctive features, assesses its competitive position and major relationships, and examines what this technology means for audit practice, skills, economics, and oversight.

**The business expanded beyond external audit.** By early 2024, MindBridge was selling its capabilities directly to enterprise finance teams as well as accounting firms. ## 2. MindBridge AI Core Product Details

### What the product actually is

**MindBridge AI is an analytical platform.** It is cloud-delivered software for financial-risk discovery and audit analytics. Users supply financial or operational records; the platform applies multiple analytical methods, assigns risk scores, and presents results through dashboards, detailed transaction views, reports, and software interfaces. Its principal output is a structured view of potentially significant risk—not an independently issued audit opinion. [14]

**The company and product are distinct.** MindBridge Analytics Inc. is the organization developing and selling the technology; MindBridge AI is its flagship platform and product brand. Earlier material, including ICAEW’s February 2020 evaluation, calls the product “Ai Auditor.” The labels identify the same company’s evolving financial-analysis offering, while dated documents establish the capabilities relevant to each period. [6][14]

**Its users extend across assurance and finance.** External auditors use it for ledger exploration, journal-entry testing, and risk assessment. Internal auditors and risk professionals use it to examine processes and controls. Finance teams use it for financial-data analysis, detective controls, and audit readiness. The product materials also identify accountants, business advisers, and enterprise users, including large companies and CPA firms. [6][13]

**It fits across the audit lifecycle.** MindBridge’s methodology places the platform in planning, fieldwork, and completion. During planning, it helps locate unusual activity and assess risk concentrations. During fieldwork, it helps select entries for investigation and retrieve relevant transaction details. During completion, it supports documentation, reporting, and communication about findings. [15]

**It functions as an analytics layer.** ICAEW’s 2020 evaluation explicitly described Ai Auditor as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, audit-workflow and working-paper software. Later API developments and KPMG Clara integration made its outputs more closely connected to broader audit systems. The useful architectural distinction remains between the analytical engine and the surrounding system that manages the whole engagement. [6][2]

### Primary functions and supported datasets

**The main functions form a connected workflow.** Ingestion brings data into the platform; analysis generates indicators; scoring and visualization prioritize attention; audit-plan tools and exports connect those findings to investigation and documentation. MindBridge’s 2022 journal-entry guide describes this progression explicitly. [16]

| Primary function | What MindBridge does | Audit purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Data ingestion | Imports and prepares financial records through uploads and APIs | Establish the analysis population |
| Ledger and transaction analysis | Applies analytics across supplied records | Understand activity across the period |
| Anomaly detection | Surfaces unusual amounts, flows, patterns, and exceptions | Identify investigation candidates |
| Risk prioritization | Scores entries and aggregates risk | Focus planning and testing |
| Visualization | Provides dashboards and transaction drill-down | Explore concentrations and explanations |
| Workflow support | Supports selections, sampling, and audit-plan tasks | Translate findings into procedures |
| Reporting and documentation | Exports selections, scores, explanations, and reports | Support working papers and review |

**General-ledger analysis is the central audit use.** The general ledger records the accounting entries underlying financial statements. MindBridge evaluates entries individually and as parts of transactions, allowing auditors to investigate amounts, debit-credit flows, timing, and other characteristics. Its journal-entry guide describes both whole-ledger screening and filtered analyses directed at risks such as management override. [16]

**The platform extends into specialized analytics.** MindBridge’s 2024 product materials identify five principal financial datasets: general ledger, vendor invoices, revenue, payroll, and company cards. Earlier ICAEW documentation also records accounts-payable and accounts-receivable analytics. These applications share the underlying risk-discovery approach while addressing different business processes and data structures. [13][6]

**Different engagement types use different configurations.** Product documentation distinguishes general-ledger analysis, Transaction Risk Analytics, and review-engagement analysis. General-ledger analysis can be full-period, interim, or periodic; Transaction Risk Analytics supports large structured financial and operational datasets. This flexibility matters because an external audit, an internal control review, and an enterprise payroll investigation do not have identical objectives. [17]

### The technologies behind the platform

**MindBridge’s AI is principally anomaly-focused.** Artificial intelligence is the broad category of computer methods used to recognize patterns and support decisions. Machine learning is a subset that derives patterns from data rather than relying exclusively on explicit instructions. MindBridge combines machine-learning techniques with conventional rules and statistics, so “AI” describes a collection of analytical methods rather than one universal algorithm. [8]

| Technology | Plain-language explanation | Application in MindBridge |
|---|---|---|
| Business rules | Explicit checks for specified characteristics | Manual entries, reporting-period timing, transaction structure |
| Statistical analysis | Comparison with expected numerical patterns | Benford analysis, frequency and amount indicators |
| Unsupervised machine learning | Pattern discovery without predefined outcome labels | Outlier Anomaly and Unusual Amounts |
| Ensemble AI | Combination of several analytical indicators | Overall entry and transaction risk scores |
| Hierarchical aggregation | Grouping transaction results into larger views | Risk by account, user, department, or division |

**Unsupervised learning addresses scarce error labels.** A supervised model learns from examples already classified, such as fraudulent and legitimate transactions. An unsupervised model instead examines the structure of the data and identifies observations that differ from surrounding patterns. MindBridge’s technical paper places anomaly detection at the core of its approach. This is particularly relevant when an organization has extensive transaction records but relatively few reliably labelled examples of error or fraud. [8]

**Anomalies can be global or contextual.** A transaction might be exceptionally large compared with the entire ledger, or unusual only when compared with similar entries in the same account or posted by the same user. MindBridge’s technical explanation distinguishes these global and local comparisons. Contextual comparison helps explain why a moderately sized transaction can deserve attention even when it falls below a simple whole-ledger amount threshold. [8]

**Rules and statistics supply complementary signals.** A manual posting near year-end presents a different kind of information from an unusual debit-credit relationship or amount distribution. Named indicators in MindBridge’s materials include Rare Flow, Outlier Anomaly, Manual Entry, Unusual Amounts, transaction complexity, and two-digit Benford analysis. Combining these signals allows an auditor to consider several dimensions of unusualness simultaneously. [15][3]

**The model category differs from generative AI.** MindBridge’s undated regulatory explanation states that its models do not employ generative AI, large language models, or GPT models. Its dated 2024 technical paper likewise distinguishes anomaly detection from language-model applications. The core product examined here analyzes financial patterns and produces risk indicators; it is not primarily a chatbot that generates an audit answer from a prompt. [18][8]

### Control points, ensemble scoring, and explainability

**Control points quantify individual risk indicators.** A control point is MindBridge’s term for an analytical check or indicator applied to the data. It may reflect a business rule, statistical method, or machine-learning technique. The system combines their results into broader risk scores rather than treating every triggered check as an equally important finding. [16]

**The documented scoring approach uses weights.** MindBridge’s 2022 guide describes its Ensemble AI score as a weighted average of control-point results. A simplified mathematical expression of that description is:

\[
R_t=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_jc_{tj}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_j}.
\]

Here, \(R_t\) represents an entry’s combined score, \(c_{tj}\) a particular control-point result, and \(w_j\) its weight. The expression explains the combination mechanism; it is not a complete specification of every model or configuration in the platform. [16]

**The control-point count depends on context.** The dated documents describe the following general-ledger configurations:

| Document period | Reported count | Relevant description |
|---|---:|---|
| 2021 methodology | 28 tests | Rules, statistical methods, and machine-learning techniques |
| 2022 journal-entry guide | 32 tests | Simultaneous general-ledger analysis |
| 2024 technical paper | 32 control points | General-ledger transaction-risk example |

The figures belong to their respective documents and configurations. The 2024 general-ledger example supports using 32 when describing that example, rather than presenting 28 as a fixed platform-wide count. [15][16][8]

**Explainability connects scoring to audit reasoning.** MindBridge displays the control-point indicators contributing to a flag, allowing the auditor to understand whether unusual amounts, rare flows, manual entry, or other characteristics drove the result. Its enterprise brochure also describes plain-English definitions for flagged transactions. Such explanations help a reviewer formulate an investigation; they do not require a language-generating model. [15][13]

**Assertion mapping makes results more audit-specific.** Audit assertions are the claims implicit in financial reporting—for example, that recorded transactions occurred, amounts are accurate, and records are complete. MindBridge’s methodology maps control points to assertions and provides account-level assertion scores. Firms can adjust relevant control points and weights and document their reasoning in the Audit Plan report. This translates a general anomaly signal into the language used to design audit procedures. [15]

### From source data to auditor action

**Preparation determines the meaning of coverage.** MindBridge’s implementation description starts with a use case and a data strategy. Records are extracted from enterprise resource planning systems, transformed into a suitable structure, and loaded into the platform—an extract, transform, load process, usually abbreviated ETL. An application programming interface, or API, allows software systems to automate requests and transfer results. [8]

**Completeness checks precede reliance on scores.** MindBridge’s journal-entry workflow instructs the engagement team to assess whether dashboards and financial statements look reasonable and whether data-quality problems exist. Its completeness report adds ledger activity to opening balances and compares the expected result with supplied closing balances, account by account. This helps detect missing or inconsistent activity before the auditor interprets risk scores. [16]

**Balanced totals are only one safeguard.** A reconciliation is valuable, but offsetting omitted entries could leave a net balance unchanged. The analytical implication is that auditors still need to understand extraction boundaries, transaction identifiers, transformations, and the relationship between source systems and uploaded records. “Every supplied row was scored” and “the supplied rows faithfully represent the relevant business activity” are different propositions.

**Risk scores then direct investigation.** Consider a hypothetical manual year-end entry with an unusual account relationship and amount. MindBridge could surface several relevant indicators and assign it a higher combined risk score. The auditor would inspect the explanation, understand the business purpose, select appropriate supporting evidence, and decide whether the entry reflects a legitimate adjustment, an error, or a control problem. The software supplies the investigation candidate; the conclusion requires accounting and audit reasoning.

**Documentation preserves the analytical trail.** Selected entries can become audit-plan tasks or be exported with transaction details and associated risk information. Q1 2024 release notes specifically state that general-ledger audit-plan tasks created through the API appear on the Audit Plan page and in exports. This connection matters because an analytical finding becomes useful audit evidence only when the procedure, follow-up, and conclusion can be reviewed. [16][19]

**The product is explainable decision support.** Its central mechanism is to turn broad financial-data coverage into a prioritized, inspectable set of questions that auditors can investigate and document.

### Efficiency: fewer routine steps and more targeted testing

**The time-saving mechanism is concrete.** Automated ingestion, completeness reporting, risk scoring, filtering, and exports reduce work that otherwise involves assembling spreadsheets, writing repeated checks, and requesting transaction details individually. Once a full ledger is accessible, an audit team can answer additional questions from the existing dataset rather than repeatedly returning to the client. This combination reduces both analytical effort and coordination delays. [16]

**Doeren Mayhew illustrates the coordination benefit.** In a 2020 vendor-published case, the firm used Ai Auditor on the U.S. division of a global automotive-parts manufacturer. The audit involved foreign currencies, third-party inventory warehouses, and substantial related-party balances. Obtaining the entire ledger early helped auditors investigate year-end inventory and accounts-payable adjustments directly, reducing the open-items list and client back-and-forth. [21]

**The benefit was broader than automatic detection.** That engagement’s efficiency arose partly because auditors had the right information earlier. Some of the improvement therefore came from data access and workflow redesign, not solely from machine learning. This distinction is practically important: a firm that buys analytical software but continues to obtain fragmented records late in the engagement may capture much less value.

**The 75% figure is a vendor claim.** MindBridge’s corporate brochure states that organizations realize “up to 75%” time savings through more focused decision-making. Arithmetically, a 75% reduction would leave 25% of the original time for the measured activity. The statement should be read as an upper-end reported outcome for relevant work, rather than as a promise that an entire financial-statement audit becomes three-quarters shorter. [14]

### Cherry Bekaert: a worked savings example

**The most useful quantified example concerns sampling.** Cherry Bekaert adopted MindBridge firm-wide in 2020 and developed a methodology connecting data analytics with its audit-risk model. In an illustrative client example, the case study compares a 384-item sample with a 252-item sample. The reduction is **132 items, or 34.4%**, calculated as 132 divided by 384; the source rounds this to 34%. [3]

| Illustrative sampling measure | Original approach | Revised approach | Reduction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Items tested | 384 items | 252 items | 132 items |
| Time at 10 minutes per item | 64 hours | 42 hours | 22 hours |
| Value at US$200 per hour | US$12,800 | US$8,400 | US$4,400 |

**The savings calculation is transparent.** The estimated gross saving is **132 avoided items × 10 minutes ÷ 60 × US$200 per hour = US$33.33 (est.)**. This is the value of avoided sampling time under the source’s assumptions. It is not automatically a reduction in the client’s bill or the audit firm’s cash expenditure, because billing rates, staff costs, and engagement pricing differ.

**The example also establishes a break-even threshold.** For the direct financial benefit in this illustration to remain positive, the additional costs allocated to data preparation, platform use, analysis, and review would need to be below US$4,400. Reusable integrations could improve later-engagement economics, whereas substantial one-off data cleaning could consume the estimated benefit. The case itself explicitly balances the sampling saving against the cost of loading data and performing MindBridge procedures. [3]

**Methodology changes explain the smaller sample.** Cherry Bekaert did not simply replace random selections with a software score. It reviewed its audit-risk model, identified higher-risk transactions separately, evaluated controls where appropriate, defined the assurance obtained from other procedures, and revised sample-size calculations. The resulting efficiency therefore reflects a combination of technology and changes in how evidence was evaluated. [3]

**The risk model connects the reasoning.** In its simplest form, audit risk equals the risk of material misstatement multiplied by detection risk. When appropriately designed procedures address significant items and provide other persuasive evidence, the remaining population may require less assurance from standalone substantive sampling. The judgment concerns the evidence actually obtained, not merely the existence of an anomaly score.

**A hypothetical calculation illustrates that interaction.** The case’s worked example assumes a 5% target audit risk and four other risk factors of 50% each: inherent risk, control risk, analytical-procedures risk, and other-substantive-procedures risk. The resulting allowable sampling risk of incorrect acceptance is:

\[
S=0.05/(0.50\times0.50\times0.50\times0.50)=0.80.
\]

The 80% figure applies to that conditional sampling component, not to the overall probability of an incorrect audit opinion. The example’s risk assignments are methodological judgments; a MindBridge score is not itself a direct measurement of any of those probabilities. [3]

### Accuracy, coverage, and independent evaluation

**Coverage and accuracy answer different questions.** Full-population analysis asks whether all supplied transactions passed through the analytical checks. Detection accuracy asks whether the resulting flags correctly identify errors or other matters relevant to the audit. A system can score every row and still generate legitimate unusual items, overlook normal-looking errors, or prioritize an anomaly that is immaterial to the financial statements.

**Anomaly detection identifies unusualness, not wrongdoing.** A rare transaction may be a legitimate acquisition adjustment, a new product launch, or an unusual but properly authorized financing arrangement. Conversely, a recurring improper transaction might resemble normal activity. The benefit is a better-informed investigation queue; the auditor must still determine accounting treatment, supporting evidence, materiality, and whether a control failed.

**Accuracy depends on the decision threshold.** Reviewing more flagged items can capture additional problems but also requires more investigation of legitimate transactions. A quantified superiority judgment would turn on labelled, same-population comparisons showing both the proportion of flags that represent genuine problems and the proportion of genuine problems detected, together with the associated review effort. These measures distinguish useful detection from simply producing a longer exception list.

**The Bank of England provides independent support.** Its initial eight-week FinTech Accelerator proof of concept used approximately 100,000 data points covering seven years of desensitized historical credit-union regulatory data. An adapted version of Ai Auditor combined statistical methods, user-defined rules, and machine learning to score observations and visualize potentially anomalous activity. The Bank found that it usefully detected anomalies and offered an intuitive interface. [22]

**The second trial sharpened the practical lesson.** The Bank applied tailored versions to operational credit-union data and transaction data supporting SONIA, the sterling overnight benchmark. It found effective dashboards, heat maps, trend charts, and useful anomaly flags, especially those identifying unusual activity within counterparties. The strongest uses were retrospective checks, and different techniques performed differently across the datasets. [20]

**Use-case fit mattered more than generic AI.** The Bank’s experience supports combining detectors and tailoring the presentation to the analytical task. It also distinguishes an operationally useful anomaly tool from an automatic conclusion about fraud or financial-statement correctness. The first trial did not encompass repeated operational testing of the learning capability, while the second identified further potential from longer use and optimization. [22][20]

**Algorithm audits address another assurance question.** Holistic AI describes a Level 7 “glass-box” or “white-box” audit with access to MindBridge’s algorithms. More than 60 contributing algorithms were individually assessed for correctness against documentation, resilience, and documentation quality, with attention to privacy, explainability, and robustness. **Competitive superiority requires a common measurement basis.** A stronger relative judgment concerning Thomson Reuters, Oracle, IBM, or other audit-firm platforms would depend on comparable product versions assessed for population coverage, anomaly-detection outcomes, explanation quality, workflow integration, deployment effort, and adoption share. Historical first-mover comparisons additionally depend on the specific capability and first commercial-use date. Broad corporate size or category inclusion does not answer those product-level questions.

**MindBridge’s commercial advantage is focused packaging.** An audit firm can obtain specialized financial indicators and an established investigation interface without developing its own anomaly system. The alternative is not merely another vendor: large firms may build internal tools, combine general analytics products, or integrate purchased capabilities. MindBridge’s value rises when its domain-specific package reduces the cost and difficulty of creating a defensible audit application.

### KPMG: the principal audit-industry alliance

**KPMG is the central confirmed relationship.** On **24 April 2023**, KPMG and MindBridge announced a strategic alliance following several years of collaboration. KPMG’s own announcement states that MindBridge’s statistical, machine-learning, and rules-based analytics would be embedded in KPMG Clara. Selected member firms had piloted the technology, with rollout underway across the global network. [2]

**The purpose was granular, explainable risk analysis.** KPMG described evaluating general-ledger and subledger populations to identify unexpected or higher-risk transactions for further analysis and challenge. MindBridge supplied the specialist analytics; KPMG supplied the audit methodology, client relationships, and broader digital-audit environment. This is a technical and commercial strategic alliance, rather than a disclosed equity investment or an explicitly classified reseller agreement. [31]

**The relationship materially increased distribution potential.** KPMG’s 2023 announcement described a network operating in 143 countries and territories. MindBridge’s then-CEO said deployment through Clara would reach tens of thousands of audit clients. A February 2024 customer story described close to 90,000 audit professionals working in Clara. Those are network and platform reach indicators, not a count of completed MindBridge deployments or separately paying MindBridge users. [2][32]

**Integration also reduced adoption friction.** Auditors are more likely to use analytical findings when they appear within their established engagement environment. KPMG also linked the alliance with no-code capabilities and professional upskilling. The implication is that distribution, workflow integration, and training can be as consequential as improvements in the underlying algorithms. [2]

## 5. Industry Impact Analysis

### Planning shifts toward population-level risk assessment

**The most immediate change is better-informed planning.** MindBridge-like platforms allow auditors to examine the distribution of risk indicators before choosing detailed procedures. Instead of relying only on interviews, high-level account movements, and initial selections, teams can inspect unusual entries and aggregate patterns across accounts, users, business units, and periods. This can make planning more specific to the entity’s actual activity. [8]

**Aggregation changes where attention is directed.** An individual unusual posting may appear insignificant, but a concentration of similar entries within one division or user group can suggest a process-level question. Conversely, a high-risk aggregate may be driven by a small number of legitimate, exceptional transactions. Drill-down connects the broad pattern with its underlying records, allowing the auditor to distinguish those explanations.

**Financial significance still shapes the response.** Anomalous activity and material misstatement are not equivalent. The auditor must relate patterns to the financial statements, the assertions being tested, and the entity’s business. A large ordinary transaction can be more consequential than a small statistically unusual one. Population-level analytics improves the starting information; it does not remove the need to decide what matters.

**The resulting planning model is iterative.** Initial analysis can reveal that a population contains different transaction types requiring separate treatment. Auditors may refine filters, inspect explanations, and reconsider the scope of detailed work. MindBridge’s journal-entry guide explicitly discusses iterative scoping and selection, including recalibration when poorly defined parameters produce excessive outliers. [16]

### Testing becomes risk-directed without eliminating sampling

**Full-population screening changes selection economics.** Once the platform has assessed the supplied ledger, auditors can select the highest-risk items, investigate specific patterns, or sample from defined risk groups. This differs from choosing a sample first and learning about only those entries. The platform helps characterize the population before the more expensive work of obtaining and inspecting supporting evidence. [16]

**Detailed testing still serves a separate purpose.** A ledger-based anomaly procedure can identify an unusual sale but cannot, by its score alone, establish that goods were delivered or that a contract supports recognition. The follow-up procedure must answer the relevant audit question. Consequently, “all entries analyzed” should be understood as the reach of the analytical procedure, not as a statement that all possible substantive procedures have been performed on every entry.

**Risk-stratified sampling combines different objectives.** Intelligent Sampler’s documented allocation—high-risk items first, then 60% medium-risk and 40% low-risk within the remaining sample—places more attention on suspicious activity while retaining lower-risk selections. The 60/40 split applies to the remainder after high-risk selection, not to the entire ledger or necessarily the entire final sample. [16]

**Low-risk selections retain an important role.** They can help auditors assess whether apparently normal activity behaves as expected and whether reliance on the analytical classification is reasonable. They also discourage the mistaken inference that an unflagged population is automatically correct. The scope of those procedures depends on the audit objective, the evidence supplied by other work, and the firm’s methodology.

**Analytics changes evidence allocation rather than responsibility.** Cherry Bekaert’s example demonstrates how sample reductions can follow from a revised assessment of residual risk and other evidence. The relevant transition is from uniform or habit-based effort toward an explicitly reasoned allocation of testing. Its success depends on maintaining persuasive evidence while removing work that contributes relatively little additional assurance.

### Full-population analysis and continuous auditing are different

**Coverage and frequency are separate dimensions.** A platform can analyze every transaction in an annual dataset without operating continuously. Equally, frequent monitoring can cover only a narrow subset of activity. MindBridge’s periodic-analysis capabilities provide a practical intermediate step between a one-off year-end analysis and real-time oversight. [17][26]

| Operating model | Population reach | Timing | Early-2024 interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sample-based periodic review | Selected records | Defined review dates | Established audit approach |
| Full-population batch analysis | All supplied in-scope records | After a dataset is prepared | Documented MindBridge capability |
| Repeated periodic analysis | Successive supplied populations | Interim or recurring cycles | Supported analysis mode |
| Real-time continuous analysis | Connected transaction streams | As activity occurs | Potential described by KPMG |

**KPMG framed continuous auditing as a potential.** Its April 2023 announcement discussed the possibility of analyzing transactions in real time and identifying issues as they occur. That language describes a direction of development, rather than a claim that all KPMG external audits had become continuous. The proven operational change at the cutoff was broader, explainable analysis integrated into digital audit work. [31]

**Internal monitoring offers a natural application.** Management can use repeat analysis as a detective control, while internal audit can independently evaluate the process and investigate exceptions. External auditors may consider relevant results within their own evidence assessment. These activities have different responsibilities and reporting purposes, even when they use related data and analytical methods.

**Continuous data does not create continuous opinions.** Faster identification of an unusual transaction can improve the opportunity for investigation or remediation. An external audit conclusion still concerns a defined reporting subject and depends on a complete body of evidence. Automating the analytical feed therefore changes the timing of work more directly than it changes the nature of the auditor’s responsibility.

### Independent studies indicate meaningful but uneven effects

**Broader studies support the proposed mechanisms.** MindBridge-specific customer examples become more informative when placed alongside research on audit technology generally. Two particularly relevant studies examine different settings: AI investment at U.S. public accounting firms, and technology-based techniques in internal-audit functions. Their findings help explain possible industry effects without treating them as MindBridge product measurements.

**External-audit AI is associated with quality gains.** Fedyk and colleagues’ *Is Artificial Intelligence Improving the Audit Process?*, published in the 2022 volume of Review of Accounting Studies, analyzed more than 310,000 employee profiles across 36 large U.S. public accounting firms during 2010–2019. It supplemented the analysis with interviews involving 17 partners from the eight largest firms. AI investment was measured through changes in the share of employees with AI-related skills. [30]

| External-audit outcome | Reported association | Measurement basis |
|---|---:|---|
| Restatement likelihood | 5.0% lower | One-standard-deviation increase in recent AI investment |
| Audit fees, one year later | 0.9% lower | Prior three-year increase in AI-worker share |
| Audit fees, two years later | 1.5% lower | Same AI-investment measure |
| Audit fees, three years later | 2.1% lower | Same AI-investment measure |

**Restatements provide an outcome-based quality proxy.** A restatement corrects previously issued financial information, making it relevant to audit quality. The study’s results are consistent with AI helping auditors identify risk and allocate effort more effectively. A restatement measure is nevertheless different from a detector’s transaction-level accuracy, and the reported association concerns audit-firm investment rather than a specific software purchase. [30]

**Efficiency gains also appeared gradual.** The increasing fee association over one, two, and three years is consistent with the interviews’ description of delayed benefits from implementation and training. Buying or developing a tool may precede its effective use across engagements. This helps explain why first-year adoption costs and later productivity improvements can coexist.

**Internal-audit evidence shows both output and cost.** Masli and colleagues’ 2023 study used a survey of 268 internal auditors, a separate survey of 505 chief audit executives, and interviews with 11 chief audit executives. The latter survey covered organizations headquartered in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. Technology-based audit techniques included a broader set of tools than AI alone. [34]

| Internal-audit measure | Association with one-standard-deviation higher technology use | Operational meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Audits completed | 18.5% more | Greater organizational coverage |
| Significant risk factors found | 10.8% more | More identified matters |
| Recommendations produced | 12.3% more | More reported improvements |
| Audit days per engagement | 13.5% fewer | Faster engagement completion |
| Internal-audit function size | 15.5% larger | Greater staffing commitment |

**Faster engagements did not mean cheaper functions.** Masli and colleagues found fewer days spent on planning and fieldwork, but no significant association with report-preparation days. Their separate cost analysis associated greater technology use with larger internal-audit functions and higher budgets. The findings therefore support improved task efficiency while showing why total expenditure can still rise. [34]

**The economic choice concerns redeployment.** Time saved can support more audits, broader coverage, or deeper investigation. It can also support lower fees or smaller budgets. These choices produce different effects on audit quality and staffing. The technology creates capacity; organizational decisions determine whether that capacity strengthens assurance, reduces expenditure, or does some combination of both.

### Auditors’ roles, skills, and employment change

**Routine screening gives way to interpretation.** KPMG’s 2024 customer account describes a strategy of standardizing workflows, centralizing execution, removing routine work, and shifting auditors toward judgment-based and complex concerns. MindBridge contributes by filtering large populations into more focused areas for attention. The remaining work includes explaining anomalies, evaluating evidence, understanding business context, and communicating conclusions. [32]

**No-code access does not remove expertise.** Auditors need enough data literacy to understand extraction boundaries, missing values, account mappings, transaction grouping, and transformations. They need analytical literacy to distinguish a statistical outlier from an accounting problem. They also need the ability to explain why a procedure is appropriate and what assurance its result supplies. Those skills complement traditional accounting knowledge rather than replace it.

**The employment evidence is not one-directional.** Fedyk and colleagues associate AI investment with subsequent reductions in accounting employees, concentrated at junior levels. Masli and colleagues associate broader technology use with larger internal-audit functions. The settings, technologies, and organizational objectives differ, making these results compatible with several forms of transformation rather than a single replacement story.

| U.S. public-accounting employment measure | Three years later | Four years later |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total accounting employees | 3.6% lower | 7.1% lower |
| Junior accounting employees | 5.7% lower | 11.8% lower |

These are the study’s associations following a one-standard-deviation increase in AI-worker share over the previous three years; it found no significant displacement at middle or senior levels. [30]

**Junior training becomes an important design problem.** If technology removes work traditionally assigned to new auditors, firms must preserve opportunities to learn transaction flows, evidence evaluation, and professional skepticism. Otherwise, faster production could weaken the development of future reviewers. A constructive response is to involve junior auditors in understanding data quality, investigating exceptions, and explaining analytical results rather than confining them to accepting software classifications.

**Specialists and engagement teams must cooperate.** Fedyk and colleagues found that AI development was concentrated in centralized teams. This resembles the KPMG–MindBridge model: a small number of technical and methodology specialists develop or govern capabilities used by many engagement teams. Centralization can spread expertise, but engagement auditors still need to understand the tool sufficiently to apply it appropriately to their client. [30]

### Oversight becomes part of audit competence

**Professional skepticism extends to analytical systems.** An auditor should question not only management’s explanation of a transaction but also why the system classified it as unusual or normal. Relevant questions concern data integrity, model purpose, configuration, explanations, and whether the procedure addresses the intended assertion. Explainable control points make this scrutiny more practical.

**Customization creates both flexibility and responsibility.** Changing weights can better align the analysis with a particular engagement, but it can also alter which entries receive attention. MindBridge’s methodology therefore connects configuration changes with documented reasoning. The governance issue is whether the adjustment reflects legitimate understanding of the entity or merely makes an inconvenient exception population smaller. [15]

**Cherry Bekaert provides an implementation example.** Its customer case describes developing firm-wide policies, reviewing MindBridge’s security assurance, independently calculating rules-based and statistical selection criteria, examining algorithm documentation, and defining circumstances in which use was appropriate. The firm also established training materials and change champions. This illustrates the professional work required to convert available analytics into an accepted audit procedure. [3]

**Auditing AI is a related but different task.** A scholarly review of continuous auditing *of AI systems* counted 22 framework studies and 13 tool studies. Its subject was checking AI against relevant norms and standards, not using a platform such as MindBridge to examine accounting ledgers. The distinction matters: an AI tool can assist a financial audit while itself becoming an object of technical and governance scrutiny. [35]

**Privacy remains fundamental to adoption.** Financial datasets can contain sensitive commercial, employee, and counterparty information. Secure ingestion, access controls, vendor assessment, and appropriate data handling are therefore part of the audit application, not peripheral IT concerns. The Bank of England’s tailored trial, for example, used a ring-fenced environment within its own infrastructure to maintain confidentiality; that trial arrangement should be understood as a specific deployment, rather than a general description of every commercial installation. [20]

### Adoption was accelerating, but remained uneven

**Commercial deployment coexisted with practical friction.** KPMG’s global initiative shows that specialist AI analytics had moved into major audit infrastructure by early 2024. Smaller-firm experiences show why that transition was uneven. A February 2024 Journal of Accountancy article describes GW CPA LLP encountering difficulties with a client’s job-costing receivables, batch transactions, and subaccounts. Grimbleby Coleman similarly found that more clients than anticipated were difficult fits because usable data could not easily be extracted from their systems. [36]

**Data preparation can dominate the business case.** These examples explain why a technically capable detector may deliver little immediate benefit on a poorly structured engagement. Standardizing extraction and defining required records can improve repeatability. Conversely, if each client requires extensive manual transformation, the integration workload can absorb the efficiency gained during analysis.

**Sector surveys show interest beyond anomaly detection.** Gartner’s 11 March 2024 release reported a survey of 112 chief audit executives conducted in July and August 2023. It found that 41% were using or planned to use generative AI in 2024, comprising 12% already using it and 29% intending adoption over the next year. Another 20% planned adoption beyond one year. [37]

| Gartner respondent status | Share of surveyed chief audit executives | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Already using generative AI | 12% | Reported use at survey time |
| Intended adoption within the next year | 29% | Near-term intention |
| Intended adoption after more than one year | 20% | Longer-term intention |

**Intentions are different from completed deployment.** The 41% headline combines observed use and planned use; it is not a 41% installed-use rate. Gartner also describes applications such as drafting, summarizing, and finding themes in text. Those uses show the profession’s expanding technology agenda, while remaining distinct from MindBridge’s core non-generative financial anomaly analysis. [37]

**Client expectations were also changing.** KPMG’s 2023 survey of more than 200 financial executives at companies with revenue above US$1 billion found that 65% reported AI use in their financial-reporting functions, while 49% had piloted or deployed generative AI. Respondents expected auditors to use AI for stronger challenge and data analysis. These are client-side expectations and self-reported practices, rather than measurements of MindBridge adoption. [33]

**Adoption depends on organizational readiness.** Masli and colleagues identify implementation expense, auditor competence, data quality, operating costs, and data access among the principal barriers. The implication is that buying the analytical capability is only one component of transformation. Sustainable benefit depends on suitable data, capable staff, governed procedures, and a deliberate choice about how saved time is used. [34]

**The evidence has distinct measurement bases.** Dated company and KPMG announcements establish commercial facts; customer accounts illustrate selected outcomes; regulator trials assess specific use cases; academic percentages describe wider-sector associations. Undated terminology and year-only 2024 illustrations establish neither launch dates nor Q1 completion. Entries, rows, users, customers, control points, and algorithms are different measures. The sampling savings above are calculated gross estimates, while the independent studies retain their original populations and investment measures.

### Overall judgment

**MindBridge demonstrates a substantial workflow transformation.** Its importance lies in making broad, multi-method financial analysis accessible within real audit and finance processes. The transition is from spending much of the engagement finding potentially relevant records toward using explainable analytical results to decide which records and processes deserve deeper investigation.

**The lasting change is better-directed judgment.** As of early 2024, MindBridge was a credible example of FinTech transforming an established profession through automation, full-population analysis, and specialist infrastructure. The strongest outcome is not an autonomous auditor, but a different allocation of human expertise: less routine searching, more investigation and interpretation, and greater responsibility for understanding both the financial evidence and the technology used to analyze it.

**From sampled audit work to AI-assisted risk discovery**

**MindBridge AI** is described retrospectively by a page published in **February 2026 and updated in July 2026** as claiming to analyze **100% of transactions in a dataset** with machine-learning models, surface risk patterns, anomalies, and outliers, and help auditors focus substantive procedures on stronger risk signals [230]. Examples include unusual posting times, atypical account combinations, and entries reversed shortly after period-end. Because this is post-cutoff, secondary evidence, the claim cannot by itself establish that the capability or its effects were independently verified by 31 March 2024.

**Coverage, efficiency, and quality claims**

Later evidence describes the potential shift from periodic review to continuous monitoring: a page created **7 April 2026 and updated 11 September 2026** says AI platforms can analyze **100% of transactions and control evidence**, score risks, generate reports rapidly, and flag problems in real time [229]. The same page reports, without identifying the study population, denominator, baseline, geography, or observation period, **68% higher efficiency**, **up to 70% higher accuracy**, and **30–45% lower audit time** for AI-based audits [229]. These are reported claims, not validated MindBridge outcomes through the requested cutoff.

**Changes to auditor work and safeguards**

The supported task shift is from repetitive evidence collection, checking, and routine analytics toward configuring procedures, reviewing flagged items, investigating exceptions, evaluating complex transactions, assessing root causes, advising the business, and documenting conclusions [181][229]. A separate broader example describes AI pre-populating control-testing workpapers so associates review and confirm extracted evidence while senior staff concentrate on control design, exceptions, and conclusions; this illustrates AI-enabled audit work generally, not a MindBridge-specific result [230].

**Workflow evidence**

A July 29, 2024 KPMG retrospective reports a 2023 multi-year global alliance embedding MindBridge AI/ML in KPMG Clara Transaction Scoring: claimed analysis of 100% of general-ledger and subledger populations, audit evidence and outliers, but no conventional baseline or before/after measure of time, quality, staffing, adoption or skills [187]. Because publication postdates 31 March 2024, this is retrospective evidence. Its other Clara examples—risk review of meeting minutes, substantive-test design and documentation improvement—and its human-in-loop and 90,000-professional claims are KPMG statements, not independent MindBridge effects [187].

Auditors shift toward data interpretation, flag investigation, limitation checks and risk responses while retaining skepticism, judgment and accountability [185][293]. Firms may recruit data scientists and value change agility, technical, creative, collaborative and communication skills; training, mentoring and tool testing remain necessary [185]. The 2024 playbook reports 92% of potential entrants and 87% of current/past audit professionals prioritising employer technology investment/use (method and denominator absent), and warns non-adoption could reduce professional appeal over 5–10 years [185].

**Workflow shift and coverage**

By 31 March 2024, evidence supports workflow change, not a quantified universal gain. Conventional analysis was time/tool constrained and sample-based; advanced analytics process large populations and high-risk areas. **Auditor and industry effects**

AI can optimize team allocation; analyze external, unstructured, image and transaction data; surface anomalies, fraud indicators and missing disclosures; reduce routine analytics; improve client communication; and, through drones, inspect difficult sites and count objects with AI. A user cited 30 million SAP-BSEG records/year, 80% of prior sifting and “100%” visibility—not industry estimates [70]. MindBridge uses rules/statistics/ML; 40+ control points score transactions [70]. Human review/data-quality/governance matter; later guidance flags privacy/bias/training [70][258].

Pre-cutoff: WEF (2020) described MindBridge analyzing **100% of financial datasets** with ensemble ML/AI, risk-weighted analysis and visual explanations [42]; Equifax Canada (5 April 2022) jointly developed synthetic-ID-fraud detection for credit-scoring clients using anomalous patterns and actionable results [75]; Cherry Bekaert used ML ledger anomaly/risk scoring in its audit methodology, reportedly removing a legacy approach [218]; a 2022 report described tracing money-flow deviations before outside-auditor review [214]. This is bounded company/use-case evidence, not industry causation. A later compilation reports a KPMG alliance intended to add AI to KPMG Clara and improve risk identification, but shows no measured pre-cutoff effect [169]. Later terms call outputs automated estimates and adjuncts, not replacements; users must verify findings [172].
