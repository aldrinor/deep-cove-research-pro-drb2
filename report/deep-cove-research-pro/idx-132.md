# Melasma Treatment Through Early 2024: Topicals, Procedures, Lasers, and Lasting Results

**The best-supported starting point is photoprotection plus topical treatment**, often a time-limited combination of hydroquinone, tretinoin, and a low-potency corticosteroid. Chemical peels, microneedling, and selected injectable treatments can add benefit. IPL and lasers can accelerate lightening, but none reliably cures melasma; post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation—darkening caused by inflammation—and recurrence are central concerns, particularly in darker skin. Through early 2024, the evidence supports choosing a device for a particular patient and protocol, not declaring one universally “best laser.” [1][2]

The following table gives the main decisions first. MASI, the Melasma Area and Severity Index, and its modified form, mMASI, measure pigmentation severity; lower scores are better. A percentage reduction in a score is different from the percentage of patients achieving clearance.

|---|---|---|---|
| Hydroquinone-based triple cream | 35% cleared versus 5.1% with hydroquinone at 8 weeks | Irritation; steroid effects with prolonged use | Best-established induction option |
| Non-hydroquinone topicals | Azelaic acid: pooled MASI difference −1.23 points versus hydroquinone | Tolerability varies by ingredient | Useful alternatives and maintenance agents |
| Superficial chemical peels | Glycolic acid favored over TCA by 1.89 MASI points in a meta-analysis | PIH, especially after excessive injury | Selected epidermal-disease adjunct |
| Microneedling | One treatment: 9% mMASI reduction at 1 week | Pain and transient inflammation | Mainly a drug-delivery adjunct |
| Platelet-rich plasma | Small study: mMASI 7.52 to 3.04 at 12 weeks | Injection reactions; variable preparations | Selected adjunct, not first-line replacement |
| IPL | IPL plus hydroquinone: 39.8% melanin-index improvement versus 11.6% | PIH, crusting, rebound | More attractive in lighter phototypes |

The topical, procedural, and device results above come from the cited topical review [3], azelaic-acid meta-analysis [4], peel meta-analysis [5], treatment network analysis [6], PRP study [7], laser review [2], and picosecond randomized trial [8].

**The report resolves four practical questions:** which creams suppress pigment with acceptable irritation; when non-laser procedures add something worthwhile; how the principal devices differ in physics, depth, safety, and durability; and how to combine these options into a sensible treatment strategy. Evidence published online through March 2024 is included.

## 1. Topical Treatments

**Topicals remain the treatment foundation.** Hydroquinone and triple-combination cream have the most established first-line role. Alternatives such as azelaic acid, topical tranexamic acid, cysteamine, kojic acid, and niacinamide are particularly useful when tolerability, maintenance, or avoidance of hydroquinone determines the choice. A systematic review encompassing 174 randomized or controlled clinical trials placed topical treatment before chemical peels and lasers. [1]

**Different ingredients interrupt different steps.** Melanocytes manufacture melanin inside pigment-containing structures called melanosomes, which are transferred to neighboring keratinocytes, the main epidermal cells. Treatment can reduce pigment synthesis, reduce transfer, accelerate removal through epidermal turnover, or diminish inflammatory signals that stimulate melanocytes. These complementary actions explain why a well-chosen combination can outperform a single ingredient. [3]

### First-line ingredients at a glance

| Ingredient or regimen | Principal mechanism | Alone or combined? | Common or important adverse effects | Representative efficacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydroquinone, usually 2%–4% | Inhibits tyrosinase and melanin production | Both; central to triple cream | Burning, erythema, scaling; ochronosis with inappropriate prolonged use | Variable positive response in 60%–90% after 5–7 weeks |
| Tretinoin, commonly 0.025%–0.1% in studies | Increases turnover; reduces pigment transfer; improves penetration | Usually combined; monotherapy is slower | Dryness, peeling, burning, retinoid dermatitis | 68% improved versus 5% with vehicle at 40 weeks |
| Low-potency corticosteroid | Suppresses inflammatory melanogenic signaling | Primarily combined | Atrophy, telangiectasia, acne, perioral dermatitis | Minimal efficacy alone |
| Fixed triple-combination cream | Hydroquinone plus tretinoin plus fluocinolone | Combination by definition | Irritation; steroid complications with prolonged exposure | 35% clearance versus 5.1% with hydroquinone at 8 weeks |

The ingredient profiles and triple-cream comparison are summarized in the 2022 topical review [3]; the 40-week tretinoin result is also discussed in the March 2024 treatment update [9].

### Photoprotection: an active treatment component

**Visible-light protection improves treatment response.** In an eight-week randomized trial summarized in a sunscreen review, women using 4% hydroquinone achieved a 75% MASI reduction with sunscreen covering ultraviolet and visible light, versus 60% with UV-only sunscreen. Because both groups used hydroquinone, this comparison demonstrates the added value of sunscreen spectrum, not the effect of sunscreen alone. [10]

**Tinted protection has a specific purpose.** Iron oxides in tinted sunscreens help attenuate visible light, including shorter visible wavelengths capable of stimulating pigmentation. Broad-spectrum UVA/UVB protection, adequate application and reapplication, shade, and protective clothing remain important alongside that visible-light coverage. The clinical aim is to reduce ongoing pigment stimulation while other treatments remove or suppress existing pigment. [1][11]

**Maintenance begins with trigger control.** Melasma commonly returns after successful lightening because susceptibility, light exposure, hormonal influences, and other stimulating factors persist. This makes photoprotection part of treatment throughout induction and maintenance, rather than a precaution used only around a procedure. [2]

### Hydroquinone

**Efficacy: the best-established single depigmenter.** Hydroquinone remains a reference treatment against which many alternatives are tested. González-Molina and colleagues reported variable positive responses in 60%–90% of treated patients after five to seven weeks; an older placebo-controlled comparison summarized in the March 2024 update reported complete clinical responses in 38% with 4% hydroquinone versus 8% with placebo. These are different response definitions, but both support genuine depigmenting activity. [3][9]

**Mechanism: suppress new melanin production.** Hydroquinone interferes with tyrosinase, a copper-dependent enzyme essential to melanin synthesis. It also alters melanosome formation and degradation and suppresses melanocyte metabolic activity. The practical result is less newly manufactured pigment; existing epidermal pigment then diminishes as the skin turns over. [3]

**Safety: irritation sets the practical limit.** Common reactions are burning, redness, itching, and scaling from irritant contact dermatitis. Chang and colleagues’ pooled analysis reported irritation in 18.3% of hydroquinone-monotherapy participants, while individual regimens produced substantially higher rates. Inflammation matters clinically because excessive irritation can itself worsen pigmentation. [12][3]

**Ochronosis is a distinct warning sign.** Exogenous ochronosis is a paradoxical, often difficult-to-treat blue-gray or blue-black discoloration associated with hydroquinone exposure, particularly inappropriate prolonged use or high concentrations. Hypopigmentation, paradoxical hyperpigmentation, and allergic contact dermatitis are additional concerns. These risks support supervised courses and reassessment rather than indefinite escalation when improvement plateaus. [3]

**Usual use: alone or combined.** Hydroquinone at approximately 2%–4% can be used alone, but it is commonly combined with tretinoin and a low-potency corticosteroid. Monotherapy remains reasonable when the other components are unsuitable; combination treatment generally offers stronger short-term efficacy. [1]

**Regulation is separate from toxicity.** In the United States, the 2020 regulatory change removed the previous over-the-counter hydroquinone marketing pathway; this should not be generalized to every country. Animal carcinogenicity concerns also should not be presented as proof that medically supervised topical hydroquinone causes human cancer: the topical review distinguished high-dose animal experiments from human topical exposure. [3]

### Retinoic acid, especially tretinoin

**Efficacy: useful but slow alone.** Tretinoin can improve melasma as monotherapy, but its time course is less attractive than that of optimized combination treatment. In the 40-week comparison summarized in the March 2024 update, 68% improved with 0.1% tretinoin versus 5% with vehicle, with clear benefit delayed until approximately week 24. Erythema or desquamation occurred in 88% of the tretinoin group. [9]

**Mechanism: accelerate pigment-containing cell turnover.** Tretinoin, or all-trans-retinoic acid, increases keratinocyte turnover, disperses epidermal pigment, reduces melanosome transfer, and increases penetration of accompanying topical agents. It is also described as influencing tyrosinase activity. These actions make it particularly valuable beside hydroquinone: one component suppresses new pigment while the other helps remove existing pigment and improve delivery. [3]

**Safety: dermatitis can undermine benefit.** Dryness, peeling, redness, burning, and stinging are the usual adverse effects. A separate vehicle-controlled study in Black patients reported retinoid dermatitis in 67% of tretinoin-treated participants. In pigment-prone skin, a regimen that produces persistent dermatitis may lose its practical advantage even when it has biological depigmenting activity. [13]

**Usual use: predominantly in combinations.** Tretinoin is commonly incorporated into triple-combination cream or paired with hydroquinone and sunscreen. Trials summarized in the topical review reported more than 50% improvement after six weeks with tretinoin 0.05%–0.1%, hydroquinone, and sunscreen. Adapalene offers a relevant tolerability comparison: a small 14-week study reported similar MASI reductions with tretinoin and adapalene, approximately 37%–41%, with better tolerability generally favoring adapalene. [3]

**The decision turns on tolerability.** Tretinoin monotherapy is most defensible when a slower response is acceptable and the skin tolerates it. When irritation is prominent, reducing exposure or changing the regimen is more rational than assuming stronger peeling will produce better pigment control.

### Topical corticosteroids

**Efficacy: not a useful standalone strategy.** Corticosteroid monotherapy has minimal depigmenting efficacy relative to its potential adverse effects. Its established role is as a carefully selected component of a combination, particularly fluocinolone acetonide in fixed triple-combination cream. [3]

**Mechanism: suppress inflammatory pigment signals.** Corticosteroids reduce prostaglandins and cytokine signaling associated with melanogenesis, including endothelin-1 and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor. They also reduce the irritation caused by hydroquinone and tretinoin. This helps a combination remain effective and tolerable without making the corticosteroid the principal pigment-removing ingredient. [3]

**Safety: prolonged facial exposure is consequential.** Skin thinning, visible small vessels, acneiform eruptions, rosacea-like redness, and perioral dermatitis are the main concerns. The fact that a steroid initially reduces redness does not make unrestricted long-term use safe; repeated suppression can conceal an increasingly unsuitable regimen. [3]

**Usual use: limited combination treatment.** The intended approach is a low-potency steroid within a defined regimen, not prolonged use of a potent “fairness” or bleaching cream. If atrophy, telangiectasia, or steroid-related dermatitis develops, the treatment strategy needs to change rather than simply adding another depigmenting ingredient. [1]

### Triple-combination cream

**Efficacy: complementary ingredients outperform simpler regimens.** The commonly studied fixed formulation contains hydroquinone 4%, tretinoin 0.05%, and fluocinolone acetonide 0.01%. In a 119-patient eight-week trial, clearance occurred in 35% with triple cream versus 5.1% with 4% hydroquinone alone. Multicenter evidence also reported complete or near-complete clearing in 77% with triple cream versus 47% with dual combinations. [3][9]

**Mechanism: combine suppression, turnover, and tolerance.** Hydroquinone inhibits melanogenesis; tretinoin improves turnover and penetration; fluocinolone reduces inflammatory signaling and treatment irritation. The advantage is not simply having three ingredients. Each addresses a different part of the process, allowing more effective pigment control at established concentrations. [3]

**Safety: effective does not mean indefinite.** Redness, scaling, dryness, burning, and itching remain common. The corticosteroid can reduce irritation without eliminating it, and continued exposure carries steroid-related risks. A pooled topical analysis reported irritation in 50.9% across hydroquinone-containing combinations, although that broad category encompasses different formulations and schedules. [12]

**Usual use: induction followed by maintenance.** Mahajan and colleagues recommended nightly fixed triple cream for an induction course of up to eight weeks. Their review described carefully supervised twice-weekly maintenance, commonly for up to six months, as one option; non-hydroquinone maintenance is another. Continuous daily triple cream should not be treated as the default lifelong regimen. [1]

**The preference changes with adverse effects.** Triple cream is particularly attractive when relatively strong initial lightening is needed and all components are appropriate. Hydroquinone intolerance, retinoid dermatitis, steroid complications, pregnancy-related considerations, or a preference for a simpler maintenance regimen can shift the balance toward alternatives.

### Azelaic acid

**Efficacy: a credible standalone alternative.** A 2023 meta-analysis of six randomized studies, involving 673 patients, favored azelaic acid over hydroquinone for MASI by a mean difference of −1.23 points, with a 95% confidence interval of −2.05 to −0.40. However, objective response categories, pigmentation reduction, and adverse events did not differ significantly. The resulting judgment is that 20% azelaic acid is an effective alternative, not that every azelaic-acid formulation consistently outperforms hydroquinone. [4]

**Mechanism: reduce melanogenesis and oxidative stimulation.** Azelaic acid inhibits tyrosinase and reactive-oxygen pathways involved in pigment production. Common adverse effects are localized stinging, burning, itching, and erythema; pooled irritation was 18.7% in Chang and colleagues’ analysis. It can be used alone, added to another regimen, or selected for maintenance, and is commonly considered when pregnancy or breastfeeding changes the choice of topical therapy. [12][11]

### Topical tranexamic acid

**Efficacy: comparable results in selected comparisons.** In a randomized double-blind study of 60 women, topical tranexamic acid 5% and hydroquinone 2% both significantly reduced MASI over 12 weeks, without a significant between-group difference. Satisfaction and tolerability favored tranexamic acid. That comparison supports an alternative to low-concentration hydroquinone, rather than equivalence to every hydroquinone or triple-cream regimen. [14]

**Mechanism: interrupt light-triggered signaling.** Tranexamic acid inhibits the plasminogen/plasmin pathway in keratinocytes, reducing downstream arachidonic-acid and prostaglandin signaling that stimulates melanocytes. Effects on vascular and endothelin-associated pathways may also contribute. It is therefore different from a direct pigment-destroying procedure: its intended role is to reduce signals encouraging pigment production. [11]

**Safety and use: usually tolerability-driven.** Topical irritation was 0.8% in one pooled analysis, although other studies recorded broader adverse-event categories more frequently. Topical tranexamic acid can be used alone, but it is frequently an adjunct to other ingredients, microneedling, or laser treatment. Its local tolerability should not be confused with the separate systemic safety considerations of oral tranexamic acid. [12][14]

### Cysteamine

**Efficacy: an effective hydroquinone-free option.** Randomized placebo-controlled studies support 5% cysteamine, and a 50-patient trial found comparable clinical improvement between short-contact cysteamine and a modified Kligman formulation over 16 weeks. Chang and colleagues reported a pooled standardized mean difference of −1.6, with a 95% confidence interval of −2.0 to −1.2, for severity improvement. [3][12]

**Mechanism: act at several synthesis steps.** Cysteamine inhibits tyrosinase and peroxidase, scavenges dopaquinone, chelates metal ions, and influences intracellular antioxidant pathways. Burning, dryness, redness, itching, and odor can affect adherence; pooled irritation was 42.2%, despite favorable tolerability in some short-contact trials. It can serve as primary treatment, an adjunct, or maintenance, with the formulation and contact time materially affecting acceptability. [12][3]

### Kojic acid

**Efficacy: useful, often in combinations.** Kojic acid improves melasma, but some direct comparisons favored 4% hydroquinone over lower-concentration kojic-acid formulations. The topical meta-analysis reported a pooled standardized mean difference of −0.9, with a 95% confidence interval of −1.3 to −0.5. This supports activity without establishing it as a stronger substitute for triple cream. [12][3]

**Mechanism and use: inhibit tyrosinase activity.** Kojic acid chelates metal ions and inhibits tyrosinase, reducing melanin production. It is used alone or combined with other lightening agents, commonly at approximately 1%–4%. Irritant or contact dermatitis, itching, localized swelling, and discomfort are the main concerns; pooled irritation was 5.3% in Chang and colleagues’ analysis. [11][12]

### Niacinamide

**Efficacy: modest evidence with favorable tolerability.** A 27-patient split-face study summarized in the 2023 clinical update reported a 62% MASI reduction with 4% niacinamide versus 77% with 4% hydroquinone after eight weeks. Erythema, itching, and burning were milder with niacinamide. This makes it an attractive supportive or maintenance ingredient, especially when avoiding irritation is a priority. [11]

**Mechanism and use: reduce pigment transfer.** Niacinamide reduces transfer of melanosomes from melanocytes to keratinocytes, thereby lowering visible epidermal pigment accumulation; anti-inflammatory effects may also help. It can be used alone, usually around 4%–5%, or combined with other lightening ingredients. Its role is complementary rather than a direct equivalent of pigment removal by a laser. [3][11]

### Vitamin C

**Efficacy: supportive rather than foundational.** In a small 16-week split-face trial, 5% ascorbic acid produced good-to-excellent patient-reported results in 62.5%, with colorimetric improvement not significantly different from hydroquinone. Hydroquinone acted earlier, and the broader evidence base for vitamin C is less established than for hydroquinone or triple cream. [3]

**Mechanism and use: antioxidant pigment control.** Vitamin C interacts with copper at the tyrosinase active site and suppresses oxidation of melanin intermediates. It can be used alone or as an adjunct; irritation, stinging, erythema, and scaling become more relevant with stronger or penetration-enhanced formulations. In the small 5% trial, irritation occurred in 1 of 16 participants, compared with 11 of 16 on the hydroquinone-treated side. [3]

### Choosing induction and maintenance

**Potency and sustainability are different decisions.** A strong induction regimen may be appropriate for active, distressing melasma, while a simpler non-hydroquinone regimen may be preferable for continued control. Conversely, an alternative cream that is theoretically gentler has little practical value if its odor, stinging, or complexity prevents regular use.

**Treatment should adapt to the skin.** The concentrations and schedules above describe studied approaches, not a self-treatment prescription. Persistent dermatitis, paradoxical darkening, skin thinning, or new pale spots warrant reassessment; during pregnancy or breastfeeding, selection should be reviewed rather than automatically continuing a hydroquinone-retinoid combination. [3][11]

**Section takeaway: optimize a tolerable topical regimen before interpreting melasma as “laser-resistant” or committing to procedural escalation.**

## 2. Aesthetic Procedure Treatments (Non-Laser)

**Non-laser procedures are principally adjuncts.** Superficial peels remove epidermal pigment; microneedling enhances delivery and stimulates repair; intradermal tranexamic acid delivers an active drug; and platelet-rich plasma supplies a variable mixture of platelet-derived signaling factors. These are distinct interventions, and their benefits should be judged against optimized topical treatment rather than against an untreated baseline alone.

| Procedure | Mechanism and principal target | Representative efficacy | Main risks | Usual role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial chemical peel | Controlled epidermal separation and renewal | Glycolic acid favored over TCA in pooled MASI comparison | Burning, peeling, PIH; deeper injury can scar | Second-line epidermal adjunct |
| Conventional microneedling | Microchannels plus repair signaling | Combination results favorable in some trials, neutral in others | Pain, erythema, burning; possible PIH | Mainly drug-delivery adjunct |
| Intradermal TXA | Local plasmin-pathway inhibition | TXA plus hydroquinone exceeded hydroquinone at 16 weeks | Injection pain, bruising, local reactions | Selected medication-delivery procedure |
| Platelet-rich plasma | Platelet growth factors and repair-associated signaling | Pooled severity improvement; approximately 5% average recurrence at 3 months | Pain, swelling, bruising, discoloration | Selected biologic adjunct |
| Microneedling radiofrequency | Needle-delivered electrical heating and remodeling | Some combinations improved mMASI more than laser alone | Pain and thermal injury or dyspigmentation | Selected energy-based adjunct |

The procedural evidence is summarized in the peel consensus [15], microneedling review [16], TXA review [14], PRP meta-analysis [17], and laser-combination review [18].

### Chemical peels

**Efficacy: glycolic acid has the strongest comparative support.** A 2023 systematic review included 24 studies and 1,075 participants, with treatment assessments over eight to 36 weeks, and judged glycolic acid the safest and most effective peel among those evaluated. A separate meta-analysis of 13 comparative studies involving 478 dark-skinned patients favored glycolic acid over trichloroacetic acid, or TCA, by 1.89 MASI points. [19][5]

**Mechanism: remove pigment-bearing epidermal cells.** Peels create controlled loss of cohesion between epidermal cells, followed by regeneration. This removes melanin-containing keratinocytes and accelerates turnover. Superficial peels primarily address the epidermal component; attempting to reach dermal pigment by increasing peel depth also increases the amount of injury and the risk of a counterproductive pigment response. [15]

| Peel | Commonly studied approach | Comparative finding | Main practical concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glycolic acid | Serial superficial sessions; often 20%–70% | Best-supported peel; added benefit over topicals is inconsistent | Burning, erythema, PIH |
| TCA | Conservative concentrations, often 10%–35% | Some studies favorable; glycolic acid favored in pooled comparison | More burning, cracking, dyschromia |
| Salicylic acid | Superficial 20%–30% protocols | Adding peels to 4% hydroquinone did not improve efficacy in one RCT | Irritation, peeling, PIH |
| Jessner or modified Jessner | Alone or combined with another peel | Some combination studies improved response | Additive irritation and excessive depth |
| Lactic acid | Serial superficial protocols | Small studies showed improvement | Irritation; concentration-dependent injury |
| Retinoic-acid peel | Studied separately from daily tretinoin | Small comparison found results similar to glycolic acid | Retinoid irritation and desquamation |

These named protocols and comparisons are described in the 2023 clinical update [11] and peel meta-analysis [5].

**Added benefit is not automatic.** Several split-face studies found no significant advantage from adding glycolic-acid peels to hydroquinone-based topical treatment. Other studies found improved MASI when peels were added to triple cream or azelaic acid, but with more burning, erythema, or PIH. This suggests that the value of a peel depends on the baseline regimen and whether accelerated exfoliation adds a meaningful benefit without excessive inflammation. [9]

**Safety: depth matters more than enthusiasm.** Immediate stinging, redness, scaling, and temporary discomfort are expected possibilities. PIH is particularly important in Fitzpatrick III–VI; medium or deep injury adds risks of persistent dyschromia and scarring. Priming with an appropriate topical regimen, conservative selection, and maintenance are therefore central to the benefit-risk balance. [15]

**Durability: exfoliation does not suppress every trigger.** Peels can lighten epidermal pigment but do not permanently normalize melanocyte stimulation. Some small peel studies reported sustained improvement, including a lactic-acid versus Jessner comparison without relapse at six months, while consensus recommendations still emphasize topical maintenance to control recurrence. [11][15]

**The resulting judgment favors superficial treatment.** Serial conservative peels are reasonable for selected predominantly epidermal disease after topical treatment has been established. Mixed or dermal pigmentation, prior procedure-induced darkening, or repeated inflammation weakens the case for further peeling rather than supporting a deeper peel.

### Conventional microneedling

**Efficacy: strongest as a delivery adjunct.** A systematic review of eight studies concluded that most evidence concerned microneedling combined with topical treatments, with limited support for monotherapy. A single dermaroller treatment reduced mMASI by 9% after one week in a pilot study, but the more clinically relevant question is whether adding microneedling improves an established drug regimen. [16][6]

**Mechanism: open channels and initiate repair.** Needles create controlled micro-injuries that allow topical agents to enter more readily through the skin barrier. Repair-associated signaling, collagen production, increased epidermal turnover, and possible remodeling of the basement membrane may contribute. Protocols using approximately 1.5-mm needles have been studied, but the appropriate depth depends on the device, location, and treatment purpose. [14]

**Comparative results depend on the companion treatment.** In a 42-patient study, six sessions with topical 10% tranexamic acid improved MASI more than microneedling alone at week 12. Conversely, another 50-participant study found no significant benefit from adding microneedling with 4% tranexamic acid to nightly 4% hydroquinone over eight weeks. These results support selective use for delivery, not the assumption that needles improve every topical regimen. [14]

**A direct comparison challenges laser necessity.** In a 60-patient randomized trial, monthly microneedling plus topical 3% tranexamic acid and monthly Q-switched Nd:YAG plus the same topical both improved melasma after five sessions and two-month follow-up. Mean mMASI changed from 4.60 to 1.88 with microneedling and from 5.12 to 2.33 with laser, without a significant difference in efficacy or satisfaction. Microneedling produced more downtime in that study. [20]

**Safety: transient reactions are common.** Pain, erythema, burning, and itching generally settle over one to two days. The network review recorded adverse events in 45 of 86 microneedling-monotherapy participants, or 52.3%, while describing PIH risk as very low. That combination is plausible: frequent short-lived redness or discomfort is not equivalent to frequent persistent pigment damage. [6][14]

**Durability follows the complete regimen.** Microchannels are temporary, and the procedure does not remove hormonal or light-related stimulation. Its practical value is greatest when improved delivery produces additional pigment control that outweighs discomfort and inflammation; persistent irritation or no incremental response should prompt reconsideration.

### Intradermal tranexamic acid and mesotherapy

**Efficacy: selected drug delivery can help.** In an assessor- and analyst-blinded split-face study of 55 patients, four intradermal tranexamic-acid treatments combined with nightly 4% hydroquinone produced better therapeutic outcomes and satisfaction than hydroquinone alone at week 16. Other comparisons found intradermal tranexamic acid comparable to hydroquinone rather than consistently superior. [14]

**Mechanism: deliver an active drug locally.** “Mesotherapy” describes a delivery method, not a single melasma treatment. For tranexamic acid, the biological target remains plasmin-associated melanogenic signaling; injections change where and how the drug is delivered. Results from tranexamic-acid injections should therefore not be generalized to unspecified mixtures of vitamins, peptides, or other substances. [11]

**Safety and durability require regimen-specific judgment.** Pain and local injection reactions can limit acceptability, and repeated visits may be necessary. A local route should not be assumed to make every concentration or formulation equivalent, or to establish permanent control. Intradermal tranexamic acid is best considered a selected adjunct when its demonstrated incremental benefit justifies the injection burden. [1]

### Platelet-rich plasma

**Efficacy: promising, with mainly short follow-up.** A meta-analysis of ten studies involving 395 adults reported a pooled mMASI improvement effect of 1.18, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.89–1.47. The authors described average recurrence of approximately 5% at three months. A separate systematic review assigned PRP a moderate recommendation while noting that preparation and treatment methods differed substantially. [17][21]

**A small prospective study illustrates magnitude.** In 20 women with Fitzpatrick IV–V mixed melasma, mean mMASI fell from 7.52 to 3.04 by week 12. That is a calculated 59.6% reduction: `(7.52 − 3.04) ÷ 7.52 × 100`. No relapse was observed during the reported three-month follow-up with strict sun protection. [7]

**Mechanism: modulate repair-associated signaling.** PRP is an autologous blood preparation enriched in platelets, which release factors including platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor-beta, and epidermal growth factor. Proposed benefits include modulation of melanogenesis, repair, and collagen or extracellular-matrix remodeling. These mechanisms make PRP biologically different from a peel, although clinical improvement does not prove which pathway caused it. [22]

**Safety: usually local and transient.** Pain, erythema, edema, bruising, congestion, and occasional hyperpigmentation or discoloration have been reported. No serious adverse events were reported in the ten-study meta-analysis. PRP can be injected alone or combined with microneedling, but added components have not uniformly improved outcomes. [17][21]

**The resulting judgment remains adjunctive.** PRP is reasonable to discuss when a patient accepts injections and understands the treatment variability. Its favorable three-month results should not displace the better-established role of photoprotection and topicals.

### Microneedling radiofrequency

**Radiofrequency is not ordinary microneedling.** Microneedling radiofrequency adds electrical energy that heats tissue around the needles, aiming to remodel the dermal environment. It is non-laser, but it is an energy-based procedure; results from conventional needling cannot be assumed to apply to it. [23]

**Some combinations improve response and tolerability.** A retrospective 114-patient comparison reported a larger mMASI reduction with low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG plus fractional microneedling radiofrequency than with laser alone—2.9 versus 1.8 points—and fewer pigmentary complications, but more procedural pain. This supports a possible role in selected combination protocols rather than a standalone first-line recommendation. [18]

**Section takeaway: non-laser procedures are most useful when they add a specific benefit—superficial pigment removal, improved delivery, or selected remodeling—without provoking more inflammation than the skin can tolerate.**

Chemical peels are a substantive non-laser, second-line/adjunct option, chiefly for the epidermal component: controlled exfoliation removes epidermal melanin and halts melanosome transfer [71]. Evidence includes 10 RCTs, 3 prospective comparative studies, and 478 patients, making them a studied option but not a first-line replacement [107].

Chemical peels can lighten epidermal or refractory melasma, but outcomes vary: reported MASI reductions were 62.36%, 60.98%, and 44.71% with glycolic, salicylic–mandelic, and phytic-combination peels [72]. Deeper injury increases post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH), dyschromia, and scarring, especially in darker skin, while temporary pigment removal without melanogenesis suppression favors relapse; use supervised peels with priming, sunscreen, and topical maintenance, noting 26.6% recurrence after one four-session glycolic course [49][71][80].

Microneedling has a substantive non-laser role as a minimally invasive adjunct: controlled microinjuries promote melanin clearance and topical-drug delivery [93][100]. Across 16 trials, one study reported 44.41% MASI improvement after the third follow-up, but heterogeneity was high (I²=95%); local irritation, erythema, and PIH risk favor clinician-selected use over routine monotherapy [70][74][99][48].

With I²=95%, microneedling should generally follow adequate priming as an adjunct to clinician-selected topical treatment, particularly when transdermal delivery or the dermal component is targeted, rather than as established standalone treatment [99][100][60][108]. Irritation or procedure-related worsening remains possible in reactive skin, and one report observed recurrence in 2 patients (20%) within 3 months after stopping; maintenance and follow-up are therefore important [44][73].

Microneedling is best selected after priming to improve topical delivery or dermal remodeling; darker or reactive skin favors shallower depth and longer intervals, while needles ≤1 mm temporarily disrupt the epidermis [98][48]. ## 3. Laser and Phototherapy

**Device choice is a benefit-risk decision.** IPL, Q-switched lasers, fractional non-ablative lasers, fractional ablative lasers, and picosecond systems are commercially used categories. Their differences in wavelength, pulse duration, target, and delivery pattern determine how they interact with skin; the category name alone does not predict efficacy, safety, or recurrence. [2][23]

### How the main technologies differ

| Technology | Dominant physical effect | Principal target | Main treatment location |
|---|---|---|---|
| IPL | Photothermal | Melanin; filters may also target hemoglobin | Mainly epidermal pigment; deeper reach varies |
| Q-switched pigment lasers | Mixed photothermal and photomechanical/photoacoustic | Melanosomes and melanin | Wavelength-dependent epidermal and dermal pigment |
| Non-ablative fractional 1440–1550 nm | Photothermal coagulation | Water-containing tissue | Junctional region through dermis |
| Non-ablative fractional 1927 nm | Superficial photothermal injury | Water-containing tissue | Epidermis and superficial junctional region |
| Fractional CO₂ or Er:YAG | Photothermal ablation and coagulation | Water | Epidermis into dermis, depending on settings |
| Picosecond pigment lasers | Predominantly photomechanical/photoacoustic | Pigment particles; optical breakdown with selected delivery | Wavelength- and beam-dependent epidermal/dermal targets |

The physical distinctions are described in the laser review [2], Q-switched systematic review [18], and picosecond trial [8].

**Photothermal means light converted into heat.** Selective photothermolysis uses wavelength-dependent absorption and an appropriate pulse duration to concentrate heating in a target. IPL uses this principle to heat pigment-containing structures; fractional resurfacing uses it primarily to heat or ablate water-containing tissue. [23]

**Photoacoustic describes pressure-driven pigment disruption.** Very short pulses can generate rapid expansion and pressure waves that fragment pigment. “Photomechanical” is the broader description of mechanical tissue effects, with photoacoustic effects representing an important component. These mechanisms are not completely independent of heating: rapid energy absorption initiates the process, and surrounding tissue injury can still occur. [24][25]

**Fractional describes delivery, not one mechanism.** A fractional device treats microscopic zones separated by relatively untreated skin. A non-ablative fractional laser coagulates tissue without creating open ablative channels; an ablative fractional laser creates microscopic columns of tissue removal. A fractionated picosecond beam may instead create focal optical breakdown, so it should not be assumed to behave like fractional CO₂. [2][18]

**Depth is not a simple wavelength ladder.** Increasing wavelength can allow deeper penetration among pigment-targeting wavelengths, but water absorption changes the relationship in the infrared. Thus, 1927 nm is generally more superficial than 1550 nm because it is absorbed more strongly by water. Treatment energy, spot size, density, and pulse characteristics also influence the actual tissue effect. [2]

**Melasma biology explains the double edge.** Pigment may be epidermal, dermal, or mixed, while affected skin can also show basement-membrane disruption, vascular changes, inflammation, and photoaging-associated dermal abnormalities. A procedure may clear pigment or remodel tissue, but excessive injury can stimulate the same inflammatory pathways that worsen pigmentation. Clinical assessment of depth helps, although Wood’s-lamp classification does not perfectly match histology. [9][2]

### Intense pulsed light

**Efficacy: useful improvement, especially with topicals.** In Wang and colleagues’ randomized study, four IPL sessions over 16 weeks plus 4% hydroquinone produced a 39.8% reduction in relative melanin index, compared with 11.6% with hydroquinone alone. Relative melanin index measures the pigment difference between affected and surrounding skin; it is not a clearance percentage. [2]

**Combination treatment strengthens the response.** Goldman and colleagues randomized 56 patients to IPL plus triple cream or IPL plus placebo cream. At week 10, 57% in the triple-cream group were clear or almost clear, versus 23% with placebo cream. Another IPL/triple-cream study reported MASI reductions of 49.4% at six months and 44.9% at 12 months, demonstrating that meaningful improvement can persist within a combined regimen. [2]

**Mechanism: broad-spectrum thermal pigment targeting.** IPL is not a laser. It uses filtered, noncoherent broad-spectrum light, commonly approximately 500–1200 nm, delivered in millisecond pulses. Absorption by melanin produces epidermal coagulation and pigment-containing microcrusts that are shed; selected longer wavelengths can reach deeper structures, and particular filters can also interact with hemoglobin. Its dominant melasma-treatment mechanism is photothermal, not picosecond-like photoacoustic fragmentation. [26]

**Target: mainly epidermal pigment, with variable depth.** This makes IPL particularly plausible for an epidermal component and, in selected settings, coexisting vascular changes. It does not mean every IPL platform reliably treats dermal melasma: filters, pulse structure, fluence, and cooling differ between systems, and epidermal melasma has generally responded better than mixed disease. [2]

**Safety: normal epidermal pigment also absorbs light.** Common effects include erythema, edema, temporary darkening, and microcrusting; recovery can extend to one or two weeks. PIH and rebound melasma are the more consequential outcomes. In the Wang study, two IPL-treated patients developed PIH. Darker or recently tanned skin presents more competing epidermal melanin, narrowing the therapeutic margin. [2]

**Recurrence: initial lightening may partly reverse.** The 2017 review reported recurrent pigmentation within 24 weeks in 24.2% of participants who initially improved with IPL. Delayed partial repigmentation and rebound have also been described. Maintenance is therefore part of the treatment proposition, not evidence that the initial treatment permanently corrected melasma. [2][23]

**Judgment: favor selected lighter phototypes.** Reviews generally favor Fitzpatrick I–III, sometimes carefully selected light type IV, particularly after inadequate topical response. Fitzpatrick phototype describes burning and tanning response and helps estimate pigmentary risk; prior PIH and actual skin condition remain important alongside that classification. A history of rebound, substantial epidermal pigmentation, or low tolerance for crusting makes IPL less attractive. [27][23]

**IPL takeaway: it can accelerate improvement, but broad thermal targeting is precisely why darker skin and rebound-prone melasma require caution.**

### Q-switched lasers

**Efficacy: separate traditional treatment from toning.** Conventional pigment-laser settings and low-fluence “laser toning” are not interchangeable. Older high-fluence Q-switched ruby and alexandrite approaches often produced disappointing melasma results or worsening. Low-fluence 1064-nm Nd:YAG has a substantially larger melasma evidence base and can produce strong short-term improvement. [2]

**Mechanism: nanosecond pulses produce mixed effects.** Q-switching creates brief, high-intensity pulses, generally in the nanosecond range. Melanin absorbs energy, producing selective heating and rapid expansion with mechanical or acoustic disruption. Low-fluence toning is variously described as “subcellular selective photothermolysis” or predominantly photoacoustic pigment fragmentation; the intended endpoint is melanosome disruption with less injury to pigment-containing cells. [18][24]

**Cell preservation is an aim, not a guarantee.** Histologic and ultrastructural studies support changes in mature melanosomes and melanocyte dendrites without necessarily reducing melanocyte numbers. Nevertheless, aggressive or cumulative treatment can produce persistent pigment dysfunction, and “low fluence” should not be interpreted as “no meaningful injury.” [18]

The wavelength-specific evidence comes from the 2024 randomized comparison [28], older ruby study [29], and Q-switched systematic review [18].

**Short-term response can be substantial.** In Wattanakrai and colleagues’ 22-patient split-face trial, five weekly low-fluence 1064-nm treatments plus 2% hydroquinone improved mMASI by 75.9%, versus 24% with hydroquinone alone. Relative lightness improved by 92.5% versus 19.7%. Those two measurements describe different aspects of improvement and should not be combined into a single success rate. [2]

**Other protocols produce more modest gains.** A 22-woman study of photoacoustic twin-pulse treatment reported approximately 20% MASI reduction after five treatments, despite higher patient-reported improvement. A low-fluence alexandrite comparison summarized in the 2023 energy review reported mMASI improvements of 44% at two weeks and 19% at 24 weeks, versus 36% and 27% with Nd:YAG. This illustrates the difference between early response, later response, and patient perception. [24][23]

**Wavelength alone does not decide superiority.** The 52-patient randomized study published in 2024 found greater MASI improvement with 532 nm than 1064 nm after eight treatments and two-month follow-up, without a significant difference in recurrence or pain. Earlier protocols reviewed in 2022 raised greater pigmentary concerns with 532-nm treatment. The reasonable interpretation is protocol- and population-dependent performance, not a universal reversal in favor of the shorter wavelength. [28][18]

**Safety: pale spots are especially important.** Immediate pain, erythema, and edema are usually less consequential than mottled or confetti-like hypopigmentation, punctate leukoderma, PIH, and rebound hyperpigmentation. In one retrospective series, mottled hypopigmentation occurred in 21 of 177 patients—11.9%—within ten low-fluence sessions. Recovery can take years, making this more serious than a few days of redness. [18]

**Cumulative exposure changes the risk.** Higher fluence, short intervals, and excessive session numbers have been associated with mottled hypopigmentation. The systematic review recommends discontinuing treatment promptly when this develops. Short-lived redness after one session does not predict whether repeated exposure will remain acceptable. [18]

**Recurrence can erase much of the advantage.** Several frequently cited low-fluence Nd:YAG cohorts reported recurrence of 64%–100% at three months. Importantly, the 100% recurrence in Wattanakrai’s trial included partial return of pigmentation; it did not mean every treated side became worse than baseline or lost all comparative benefit. [2]

**The lower-recurrence counterexample matters.** It prevents the 64%–100% range from being misread as an inevitable outcome for every toning protocol. At the same time, favorable results in twelve followed patients cannot neutralize the clinically important recurrence and hypopigmentation signals across the larger literature.

**Judgment: selected refractory disease, not routine escalation.** Low-fluence Nd:YAG can be reasonable after inadequate medical treatment or topical intolerance, particularly when a patient values faster improvement and accepts repeated visits. Conservative treatment, an appropriate maintenance regimen, and active monitoring for pale spots are essential. A new pattern of hypopigmentation or rebound should weaken the case for further sessions, not trigger automatic energy escalation. [30]

**Q-switched takeaway: strong short-term lightening is credible, but cumulative pigment injury and recurrence often determine whether that lightening was worthwhile.**

### Fractional non-ablative lasers

**Efficacy: useful alternatives with protocol-sensitive safety.** Non-ablative fractional lasers can improve melasma without removing a continuous skin surface. The principal wavelengths discussed in the melasma literature include 1440, 1540, 1550, and 1927 nm, but 1550 and 1927 nm have the clearest representative clinical evidence in the sources considered here. [2]

**Mechanism: create microscopic thermal treatment zones.** These water-targeting lasers generate columns of coagulated tissue below the ablative threshold, leaving surrounding tissue and the surface barrier sufficiently preserved to support healing. Remodeling and transepidermal elimination of pigment-containing debris are proposed mechanisms. They are photothermal treatments, not pigment-specific photoacoustic lasers. [2]

**Target depth differs materially by wavelength.** The 1440–1550-nm group can treat from the dermal-epidermal junction into the dermis, with 1550-nm settings reaching approximately 1.3–1.5 mm. By contrast, 1927 nm has much greater water absorption and an approximate maximum treatment depth around 200 micrometers, making it more closely aligned with superficial and junctional pigment. These depths describe device-tissue interaction, not a guarantee that every pigment deposit at that depth will respond. [2]

**The 1550-nm results are mixed.** An early pilot found 75%–100% clearance in six of ten women after four to six sessions. In another 25-patient study, 60% were judged improved after treatment, falling to 52% at six months; those figures are proportions of patients improving, not mean percentage MASI reductions. [2]

**Direct topical comparisons are more informative.** Kroon and colleagues’ randomized pilot found similar improvement with four 1550-nm treatments and triple topical therapy, with recurrence in 50% of each group at six months. Wind and colleagues’ split-face study instead favored topical therapy and reported PIH in 31% on the laser-treated side. The laser settings differed, supporting the importance of treatment intensity rather than a simple “fractional is safe” classification. [2]

**The 1927-nm trajectory shows benefit fading.** In a 14-patient pilot, three to four sessions produced MASI improvements of 51%, 33%, and 34% at one, three, and six months after treatment. Erythema, edema, and microcrusting lasted approximately three to seven days, with no PIH or scarring observed in that pilot. [2]

**Longer follow-up supports maintenance, not cure.** In a 20-woman retrospective 1927-nm series, 60% achieved more than 50% clearance at one month, and mean MASI remained 53.8% below baseline at six to twelve months. Patients used 4% hydroquinone maintenance. At an average of 10.2 months, 33.3% reported at least partial recurrence and 13% full recurrence; these categories should not be added because “at least partial” can include full recurrence. [2]

**Safety: non-ablative does not mean non-inflammatory.** Pain, erythema, edema, and microcrusting are common short-term possibilities, and PIH can be clinically significant. A general Fraxel device review described approximately 5% overall adverse effects across its broader clinical literature, but that is a different population and measurement basis from the 31% PIH finding in a melasma-specific trial. [31][2]

**Judgment: select depth and density carefully.** Superficial 1927-nm treatment is a plausible option for an epidermal or junctional component, while 1540–1550-nm approaches offer deeper remodeling. Neither depth nor remodeling proves durable superiority over topical treatment. Prior PIH, unacceptable downtime, or worsening after early sessions should shift the decision toward less inflammatory management.

**Non-ablative fractional takeaway: these devices offer controlled remodeling and pigment clearance, but settings and maintenance matter more than the reassuring word “non-ablative.”**

### Fractional ablative lasers: CO₂ and Er:YAG

**Efficacy: adjunctive benefit is better supported than superiority.** A 2024 meta-analysis of fourteen randomized trials and 527 patients found that ablative fractional laser plus medication improved MASI more than medication alone, by a reported mean difference of 1.54 points, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.16–2.92. Physician global assessment also favored combination treatment, whereas patient self-assessment did not differ significantly. Ablative fractional monotherapy was not significantly superior to other lasers. [32]

**Mechanism: ablate water-containing tissue in columns.** CO₂ at 10,600 nm and Er:YAG at 2940 nm primarily target water. Fractional delivery creates microscopic ablative channels and surrounding thermal zones, removing epidermal pigment and stimulating repair. Depending on settings, columns can extend into the papillary or reticular dermis; the effect is photothermal ablation, not selective photoacoustic fragmentation of melanosomes. [2][25]

**CO₂ and Er:YAG differ thermally.** Er:YAG’s strong water absorption permits relatively precise superficial ablation with less residual thermal injury, while CO₂ produces more coagulation. This physical distinction does not make Er:YAG automatically safer for melasma: pigment-producing inflammation can occur after either treatment. [26]

**Reported response magnitude can look attractive.** The 2021 network review summarized approximately 48% MASI reduction with a fractional CO₂ protocol and 26% with an Er:YAG resurfacing protocol after two treatments one month apart. These are protocol-specific ablative results; older Er:YAG resurfacing evidence should not be treated as interchangeable with every modern fractional Er:YAG setting. [6]

**A direct trial shows the trade-off.** In a 37-patient split-face comparison, adding fractional CO₂ to 4% hydroquinone accelerated early improvement in darkness, but did not significantly improve final clinical response, physician assessment, or satisfaction compared with hydroquinone alone. Only 5.4% achieved more than 75% improvement on the combination side at final follow-up. Earlier lightening is therefore not the same outcome as a better eventual result. [33]

**Safety: greater injury creates greater consequences.** Erythema, burning, edema, crusting, and wound-care requirements are expected concerns. PIH, prolonged dyschromia, herpes reactivation, acne, infection, and scarring are more consequential possibilities. Fractionation reduces the injured surface compared with full-field resurfacing, but it does not eliminate inflammatory pigment responses. [32][2]

**Older resurfacing supplies a warning.** In a ten-woman refractory-melasma series, all developed PIH three to six weeks after Er:YAG resurfacing despite marked immediate improvement. That is not a 100% PIH estimate for modern fractional treatment; it demonstrates why impressive immediate pigment removal can be a misleading endpoint in this disease. [26]

**Recurrence evidence supports a cautious interpretation.** The fractional CO₂/hydroquinone trial observed no recurrence among its 37 analyzed patients at three months. Conversely, other ablative series described initial improvement followed by PIH or pigment return. In an older twenty-patient Er:YAG study, only 15% retained more than 50% improvement at four months. [33][2]

**Response persistence is a useful proxy.** From that Er:YAG result, a calculated 85% did not show greater than 50% improvement at four months: `100% − 15% = 85%`. This measures failure to maintain a substantial endpoint response, combining weak responders and patients whose benefit faded; it is not an 85% recurrence rate and is not a fractional-CO₂ forecast.

**Judgment: reserve intensive resurfacing for selected cases.** Ablative fractional treatment is most defensible when refractory disease, a specific drug-delivery objective, and willingness to accept recovery justify the additional injury. The preference weakens sharply when the main objective is low-risk cosmetic maintenance, especially in someone with prior PIH.

**Ablative fractional takeaway: these devices can add efficacy, but the incremental benefit must justify greater inflammation, recovery, and pigmentary risk.**

### Picosecond lasers

**Efficacy: 1064 nm has encouraging direct evidence.** In a randomized assessor-blinded trial, 60 women with Fitzpatrick III–IV melasma were assigned to nonfractional 1064-nm picosecond Nd:YAG, 755-nm picosecond alexandrite, or 2% hydroquinone; 59 were analyzed. At week 24, mean MASI improvement was 35.9%, 25.5%, and 24.0%, respectively. The 1064-nm group performed significantly better than both comparators, while 755 nm and hydroquinone were comparable. [8]

| Trial arm | Treatment course | MASI improvement at week 24 | Recurrence by study endpoint | PIH |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1064-nm picosecond Nd:YAG | Three sessions, 4 weeks apart | 35.9% | 1/20, or 5.0% | 1/20, or 5.0% |
| 755-nm picosecond alexandrite | Three sessions, 4 weeks apart | 25.5% | 2/20, or 10.0% | 1/20, or 5.0% |
| Hydroquinone 2% | Twice daily for 12 weeks | 24.0% | 1/19, or 5.3% | 1/19, or 5.3% |

All values in this table come from the same [8].

**Mechanism: predominantly mechanical pigment fragmentation.** Dermatologic picosecond systems commonly deliver pulses of approximately 300–900 picoseconds, compared with nanosecond pulses in conventional Q-switched systems. The shorter energy delivery favors photomechanical or photoacoustic pigment fragmentation with less thermal spread. Selected fractional optics can create laser-induced optical breakdown—focal plasma formation and microscopic vacuoles—rather than conventional thermal resurfacing columns. [8][34]

**Target: wavelength and optics both matter.** The 1064-nm wavelength is less strongly absorbed by epidermal melanin and penetrates more deeply than 755 nm. In the three-arm trial, wavelength and pulse duration differed together—450 picoseconds for the Nd:YAG system and 750 picoseconds for alexandrite—so the better result cannot be attributed solely to wavelength. The authors presented the physical explanation as a hypothesis, not a proven causal separation. [8]

**Pooled evidence favors a restrained conclusion.** A six-RCT meta-analysis found significant overall MASI/mMASI improvement but substantial heterogeneity, with I² of 70%. The 1064-nm subgroup showed significant improvement, whereas 755 nm did not significantly outperform topical hypopigmenting agents in its subgroup comparison. Thus, “picosecond” is not a sufficient description of the treatment being evaluated. [35]

**Direct nanosecond comparisons are less decisive.** In Lee and Kim’s prospective split-face study, five treatments produced week-16 mMASI changes from 7.88 to 4.87 with picosecond treatment and from 7.85 to 5.76 with photoacoustic twin-pulse Q-switched treatment. The between-treatment difference was not significant. Both sides worsened between week eight and week sixteen, illustrating that reduced thermal spread does not eliminate pigment return. [36]

**Safety: less heat does not mean no dyspigmentation.** Transient erythema, pain, PIH, and hypopigmentation remain relevant. In the three-arm trial, one patient in each laser group developed PIH, which resolved over the reported one-to-six-month period. A refractory-melasma study of 755-nm picosecond alexandrite reported macular hypopigmentation and dissatisfaction despite improvement, providing an important counterexample to blanket safety claims. [8][13]

**Recurrence: the clock matters.** The 5%–10% recurrence figures in the three-arm trial were measured at week 24 from treatment initiation—approximately four months after the last laser session, not six months after completion. Recurrence did not differ significantly between the three groups. These results are encouraging, but they do not establish that picosecond lasers prevent relapse better than optimized medical treatment. [8]

**Commercial options extend beyond studied comparisons.** Platforms may offer 532, 730, 755, 785, or 1064-nm options, depending on the system. The principal melasma comparisons discussed here concern 1064 and 755 nm; a platform’s wavelength menu should not be read as evidence that each wavelength has the same clinical performance or durability. [37][35]

**Judgment: promising for selected patients.** A 1064-nm picosecond option is reasonable to discuss when a patient accepts procedural treatment and values reduced thermal spread. The conclusion would become stronger with reproducible, longer off-treatment superiority in both pigment improvement and complication rates; the early-2024 evidence supports consideration, not universal preference.

**Picosecond takeaway: the technology improves the physical approach to pigment fragmentation, but clinical superiority still depends on the particular wavelength, optics, protocol, and patient.**

### Vascular-targeted lasers as selective adjuncts

**Vascular targeting answers a different question.** Pulsed-dye lasers, usually around 585–595 nm, primarily target hemoglobin in superficial dermal vessels through vascular photothermolysis. The rationale is to reduce vascular-associated signals contributing to melanogenesis, rather than directly removing all epidermal or dermal melanin. [23]

**Results favor selection over routine use.** In a seventeen-patient split-face study, adding pulsed-dye laser to Q-switched Nd:YAG did not significantly improve the overall group response, but the subgroup with visibly widened capillaries benefited more. PIH or rebound occurred in two patients on the combination side, both with darker phototypes. Another small retrospective series combining pulsed-dye and 1927-nm treatment found greater than 50% improvement in six of eleven patients, without reported rebound during three-to-36-week follow-up. [18][23]

**Safety includes vascular and pigmentary reactions.** Purpura can persist for approximately seven to fourteen days with vessel-rupturing settings; erythema, pain, and PIH are additional concerns. A vascular component on examination may justify discussion of this approach, but visible vessels alone do not establish that adding another laser will improve the patient’s overall outcome. [25]

### Commercial availability and regulatory status

**Market availability is not universal authorization.** IPL, Q-switched pigment lasers, fractional resurfacing, and picosecond systems were all commercially used by early 2024. Authorization is specific to a device, indication, jurisdiction, and date; it should not be generalized to every system sharing a wavelength or category. U.S. 510(k) marketing clearance also differs from drug approval and does not itself establish comparative efficacy or recurrence performance.

**Section takeaway: ask what the exact device and protocol have demonstrated in melasma—not merely whether the platform is marketed, newly introduced, or regulator-cleared.**

## 4. Comprehensive Comparison and Conclusion

### Which treatment offers the best overall balance?

**Topical-first treatment wins the starting decision.** Its advantage is not that it always produces the largest early MASI reduction. It directly suppresses ongoing pigment production, avoids procedural injury and recovery, and has an established maintenance role. Device treatment becomes more attractive when topical response is inadequate, topical intolerance persists, or a patient accepts additional risk for faster improvement. [1][27]

| Option | Main efficacy advantage | Main disadvantage | Reported durability anchor | Overall position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Triple-combination topical | Strong controlled short-term clearance evidence | Irritation and cumulative steroid exposure | Relapse can follow withdrawal | Preferred induction when appropriate |
| IPL | Accelerated lightening with topical treatment | Broad epidermal heating, crusting, PIH | 24.2% recurrence among responders at 24 weeks in one study | Selected lighter-phototype adjunct |
| Low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG | Sometimes large early pigment reductions | Mottled hypopigmentation and rebound | Several cohorts: 64%–100% recurrence at 3 months | Selected refractory treatment |
| Non-ablative fractional laser | Remodeling plus pigment elimination | PIH despite intact surface | 1550-nm trial: 50% recurrence at 6 months | Alternative escalation, carefully parameterized |
| Ablative fractional laser | Can enhance medication response and delivery | More injury, recovery, dyspigmentation | CO₂/HQ trial: 0/37 recurrence at 3 months | Intensive selected adjunct |
| Picosecond laser | Reduced thermal spread; encouraging 1064-nm results | Pigmentary injury still possible | Trial: 5%–10% recurrence by week 24 | Promising protocol-specific option |

The comparison draws on the topical systematic review [3], laser review [2], CO₂ trial [33], and picosecond randomized trial [8].

**Non-laser adjuncts occupy a useful middle ground.** Superficial peels can accelerate epidermal pigment removal, while microneedling can improve delivery without relying on melanin absorption. PRP or intradermal tranexamic acid may suit selected preferences and treatment objectives. Their value is incremental: improvement from baseline is encouraging, but superiority over a well-tolerated topical regimen is the more useful clinical test. [9][16][21]

**Recurrence and injury are different failures.** Recurrence means pigmentation returns after improvement. PIH means treatment-associated inflammation adds pigmentation; hypopigmentation means treatment leaves lighter areas, sometimes persistent. A procedure can have little immediate downtime yet a serious delayed pigment complication, or substantial transient redness with acceptable eventual results. These outcomes should not be collapsed into one “side-effect rate.”

### What do comparative rankings actually show?

**Short-term network rankings favor several devices.** Liu and colleagues’ 2021 network meta-analysis included 59 randomized trials, 2,812 participants, and fourteen active treatment categories. Its SUCRA scores placed Q-switched Nd:YAG, IPL, ablative fractional laser, and triple cream near the top. SUCRA summarizes relative ranking within that evidence network; it is not the percentage of patients cleared or the probability that an individual will benefit. [6]

| Position among 14 active categories | Treatment | SUCRA score | Practical reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Q-switched Nd:YAG | 85.0% | Strong short-term efficacy signal |
| 2 | IPL | 79.6% | Favorable ranking from only three studies |
| 3 | Ablative fractional laser | 78.6% | Efficacy must be balanced against injury |
| 4 | Triple-combination cream | 75.8% | Strong result without procedural exposure |
| 12 | Picosecond laser | 32.8% | Early, sparse and conflicting evidence |
| 14 | Non-ablative fractional laser | 28.1% | Highly protocol-dependent category |

These are selected entries from the published [41], retaining their original positions.

**The ranking answers a narrower question.** The network selected the assessment time with the greatest MASI change, and its mean final assessment was approximately thirteen weeks. That makes it informative about observed treatment response, but less suited to deciding which intervention provides the best year-long control after treatment stops. The later picosecond evidence also helps explain why an early network position should not be treated as a permanent technology ranking. [6]

**Safety rankings are particularly sensitive to reporting.** For example, the network recorded no adverse events among 47 IPL-monotherapy participants, yet other IPL studies clearly documented PIH. It also recorded broad adverse-event frequencies of 20% for ablative fractional treatment and 52.3% for microneedling. These numbers do not establish that ablative treatment is inherently safer: studies differed in whether they counted brief redness, pain, pigment changes, or other reactions. [6]

**The resulting judgment is evidence-aware selection.** A high efficacy ranking can justify discussing a treatment; it does not override recurrence, persistent hypopigmentation, patient priorities, or a safer effective alternative.

### Is combination therapy better than monotherapy?

**Usually, but the combination must be specified.** In the network review, 27 of 31 direct comparisons favored combination therapy, while four found no significant difference. The calculated proportion is 87.1%, conventionally reported as 87%: `27 ÷ 31 × 100`. This is a count of favorable study comparisons, not an 87% patient response rate. [41]

**Complementary mechanisms provide the strongest rationale.** Photoprotection reduces stimulation, topicals suppress new pigment, and a selected procedure accelerates pigment removal or improves drug delivery. This is more compelling than simply combining two devices that both injure pigment-containing tissue. Triple cream is the clearest example of complementary treatment supported by direct controlled comparisons. [3][2]

| Tested combination | Comparative finding | Decision implication |
|---|---|---|
| Triple cream versus hydroquinone | Higher clearance with triple cream | Established complementary combination |
| IPL plus triple cream versus IPL plus placebo | 57% versus 23% clear/almost clear at week 10 | Ongoing pigment suppression adds value |
| IPL plus Q-switched Nd:YAG versus Nd:YAG alone | 60% versus 40% mMASI improvement; recurrence 33% versus 11% | Better early response can coexist with worse durability |
| Fractional CO₂ plus hydroquinone versus hydroquinone | Faster early darkening reduction, similar final clinical response | Added injury may not improve the final result |
| Q-switched Nd:YAG plus fractional CO₂ versus Nd:YAG | Similar efficacy in one comparison, less mottled hypopigmentation | A combination may offer a safety benefit rather than more clearance |

The comparisons are reported in the topical review [3], laser review [2], fractional CO₂ trial [33], and Q-switched systematic review [18].

**The exceptions change clinical advice.** The small IPL-plus-Nd:YAG comparison is particularly instructive: its stronger initial mMASI improvement did not translate into lower recurrence. More treatment can produce additional benefit, additional inflammation, or both; the study does not establish the mechanism behind its higher recurrence, but it clearly challenges the assumption that adding energy necessarily improves durability.

**Sequencing can be more important than stacking.** Establishing photoprotection and a tolerated pigment-suppressing regimen before a procedure can reduce the biological stimulus for rapid repigmentation. After treatment, the skin’s recovery and tolerability should determine when irritating agents resume. Maintenance should preserve improvement without replacing procedural injury with chronic topical dermatitis. [2][1]

### Situation-specific treatment choices

**The preferred pathway depends on the patient.** The following decisions are conditional treatment judgments, not individual response forecasts.

| Clinical situation or priority | Preferred approach | Reason | What changes the choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| New or topical-responsive melasma | Photoprotection plus appropriate topical induction | Best-established starting balance | Intolerance or inadequate response |
| Hydroquinone intolerance or maintenance | Azelaic acid or another tolerated non-HQ topical | Continued pigment control with different tolerability | Persistent irritation or poor adherence |
| Predominantly epidermal, residual pigment | Consider superficial peel as adjunct | Matches superficial target | Prior PIH or little added response |
| Need to improve topical delivery | Consider conventional microneedling | Delivery-focused mechanism | No incremental benefit or excessive inflammation |
| Lighter phototype seeking faster lightening | Discuss IPL plus maintenance | Reasonable efficacy in selected patients | Rebound history or low downtime tolerance |
| Mixed or dermal, treatment-refractory disease | Specialist-selected 1064-nm or fractional approach | Deeper targeting or remodeling may help | Pigment injury, low risk tolerance |
| Darker phototype or previous procedure-induced PIH | Favor tolerable medical therapy; conservative escalation | Narrower pigmentary safety margin | A compelling, demonstrated procedural benefit |
| Prominent visible vascular component | Consider selective vascular adjunct | Targets a different disease component | No added benefit or pigment worsening |

These decisions follow the treatment hierarchy [1], peel consensus [15], laser consensus [27], and protocol-specific device evidence [18].

**Darker skin requires precision, not automatic exclusion.** Conservative parameters, appropriate cooling, careful assessment of previous PIH, and test treatment can improve selection. A test spot is helpful but does not guarantee safety after repeated whole-area exposure, particularly for cumulative hypopigmentation. The relevant expertise is experience with melasma and the patient’s skin type, not simply experience operating the device. [30][18]

**Deeper pigmentation does not mandate aggressive treatment.** Mixed or dermal pigment can be less responsive to superficial treatment, but increasing treatment depth also exposes more tissue to injury. A patient who prefers partial improvement with low risk may reasonably continue medical management rather than pursue an anatomically deeper procedure. Depth matching helps choose among procedures; it is not an obligation to perform one.

**Oral tranexamic acid is a separate decision.** Although outside the principal topical and procedural categories, it is an important physician-selected adjunct for some refractory cases. A trial summarized in the March 2024 update reported a 49% mMASI reduction with 500 mg daily for three months versus 18% with sunscreen alone. Its off-label use requires assessment of thromboembolic risk and other contraindications; gastrointestinal and menstrual adverse effects are also relevant. It should not be treated as an interchangeable substitute for a topical serum or cosmetic supplement. [9][1]

**Patient priorities should determine acceptable trade-offs.** Faster lightening, minimal downtime, low risk of pale spots, fewer visits, and sustained control are different objectives. A protocol that produces the largest early score reduction may be the wrong choice for someone unwilling to accept prolonged pigment irregularity or repeated maintenance procedures.

**How to interpret the evidence.** This report uses evidence available online through March 2024. MASI/mMASI changes, melanin-index changes, standardized effects, and clearance proportions measure different outcomes; recurrence retains each study’s denominator, maintenance regimen, and follow-up clock. Many procedural studies are small and predominantly involve women with phototypes II–V, while adverse-event definitions differ. Values labeled “calculated” are arithmetic from reported inputs, including the PRP score reduction and Er:YAG response-persistence proxy; they are not individualized predictions. [6][1]

**The final judgment is straightforward:** begin with consistent photoprotection and an effective, tolerable topical regimen; add a procedure only for a defined additional benefit. Among devices, prioritize the exact protocol’s pigmentary safety and durability over novelty or early clearance. Combination therapy is often beneficial, but the best combination is the least burdensome one that controls melasma without creating a new pigment problem.

**Comparison basis and diagnostic scope**

Melasma is an acquired, usually facial, symmetric hypermelanosis involving centrofacial, malar or mandibular areas; it is more prevalent in women and darker phototypes and is associated with ultraviolet exposure, pregnancy, hormonal therapy and family history [128][116]. Epidermal, dermal and mixed disease should not be treated as one phenotype: epidermal lesions are often more responsive, while dermal lesions tend to be bluish, blurred and less responsive [112][113]. A 210-person Asian reflectance-confocal cohort classified 71.5% of cases as epidermal and 28.5% as mixed [115]. In 89 Iranian women, Wood’s lamp classified 88.8%, 1.1% and 10.1% as epidermal, dermal and mixed, whereas dermoscopy classified 50.6%, 28.1% and 21.3%; agreement was only 59.6% (κ=.232) [114]. Pigmented contact dermatitis from photosensitizing hair dye, including para-phenylenediamine, can mimic melasma and should be excluded [119]. Treatment selection should consequently be tailored to skin type, severity, phenotype and patient goals [121].

**Population and generalizability**

The evidence mainly represents women with facial melasma and Fitzpatrick phototypes III–V. A Brazilian multicenter study of 953 adults reported 97.5% women, phototypes II/III/IV in 12.8%/36.3%/39.7%, and extra-facial disease in 14.2% of postmenopausal versus 3.5% of non-menopausal women [132]. In a Tunisian cohort of 197 patients, including 188 women, phototypes III/IV/V were 14%/45%/41% and centrofacial/malar/mandibular patterns were 76%/23%/1%; sun exposure was a trigger in 51% and aggravator in 84%, pregnancy an aggravator in 51% of women who had been pregnant, and oral-contraceptive use was reported by 38% of exposed women [118]. Among 119 northern-Indian cases, 83.2% were women, phototypes IV/III/V were 66.4%/21.8%/11.8%, and 73.1% had centrofacial disease; higher phototype was associated with higher MASI (p<.001) [119]. A 302-woman Brazilian cohort reported phototypes III/IV in 34.4%/38.4%, family history in 56.3%, pregnancy as a trigger in 36.4%, contraceptive-pill exposure in 16.2%, intense sun exposure in 27.2% and zygomatic involvement in 83.8% [131]. A 312-patient study from Siliguri reported a mean age of 33.45 years, a female-to-male ratio of approximately 4:1 and a 55.44% centrofacial pattern [120]. An undated commercial summary quotes 1.5%–33% population prevalence and 15%–50% prevalence during pregnancy; because its design is not established, these figures are contextual rather than pooled epidemiology [112].

**Photoprotection and durability**

Broad-spectrum and visible-light photoprotection should accompany active treatment and maintenance; it is not an active-treatment comparator. Light, heat and ultraviolet radiation stimulate melanocytes, unprotected ultraviolet exposure appears to be the strongest environmental trigger, visible light is relevant, and the contribution of infrared radiation remains uncertain [113][119]. Practical advice includes tinted or iron-oxide-containing broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30–50, reapplication every two hours during sun exposure, hats and shade [113]. An observational Indian report suggests physical sunscreens containing iron oxide and zinc oxide for some indoor workers, but gives no application quantity, adherence or reapplication protocol [119]. The medically reviewed Cleveland Clinic article supporting this advice was updated in 2020 and provides no formal certainty grade or comparative trial [113].

**What direct comparisons support**

- **Tretinoin and triple combination:** In 28 Black patients completing a vehicle-controlled trial, daily whole-face **0.1% tretinoin** for 10 months improved MASI by **32% versus 10% with vehicle at 40 weeks**, reduced epidermal pigment, and caused mild retinoid dermatitis in 67% of tretinoin-treated patients [134]. The Cochrane review found triple-combination cream more effective for lightening than hydroquinone alone and several dual combinations, although many underlying studies were small or poor quality [133]. Hydroquinone is described as a standard-of-care option in many countries and useful for controlling relapses, but its side-effect profile limits prolonged use and no universal recurrence rate is established [128].

- **Intradermal procedures and IPL:** In a 40-case Egyptian split-face study, three intradermal TXA or PRP sessions four weeks apart produced 3-month mMASI reductions of **45.67% and 53.66%**, respectively (p=.005). This evidence concerns intradermal—not oral or topical—TXA. Additional monthly observation was intended to detect recurrence, but no numeric recurrence rate was reported; the abstract and adverse-event table also differed regarding side-effect comparisons [47]. In an Iraqi trial of 20 versus 21 patients, four IPL sessions at four-week intervals improved MASI, especially in epidermal and mixed disease; epidermal combination-group MASI fell from **25.74±6.6 to 3.94±2.6**. Dermal IPL-alone MASI changed from **20.40±4.49 to 18.88±4.1** at eight weeks (p=.271) [126]. IPL risks were mainly mild erythema and dryness; transient PIH occurred in 3/20 patients (15%) in the combination group, and no scarring was recorded [126]. The combination appeared more effective in this small trial, but modified Kligman’s formula and sunscreen were co-interventions, so IPL itself was not proven superior to monotherapy.

**Photoprotection and maintenance**

**Oral tranexamic acid**

In 58 Egyptian women, topical tranexamic acid and vitamin C each reduced MASI over 12 weeks with no between-side difference; 30 dropouts left 45 evaluable participants per side. Three-month follow-up looked for relapse but gave no numeric rate; burning and erythema subsided quickly, with adverse-event frequencies not reported. [136]

**Photoprotection and combination evidence**

**Broad-spectrum plus visible-light photoprotection is foundational adjunctive care, not depigmenting monotherapy.** UV and visible light activate melanogenesis; UVA penetrates deeper, and visible light has stronger pigment effects in Fitzpatrick III–VI skin [143]. An undated report of 68 adults found MASI improvement of **77.8% with UV-visible-light protection versus 61.9% with UV-only sunscreen** [143]. A Mexican registry trial (2012–13; 68 female adults, phototype ≥III) compared 290–400 versus 290–800 nm sunscreen plus 4% hydroquinone for 12 weeks, using 2 mg/cm² every 3 hours from 08:00–17:00; MASI (0–24), colorimetry, histology and physician global assessment were planned, but no efficacy result or post-treatment relapse was reported [142]. Visible-light coverage is therefore promising, not a universal effect estimate.

**Maintenance and contextual adjuncts**

**TXA:** Topical 2–5% formulations (~12 weeks), with penetration-variable efficacy, may be used alone or combined for mild–moderate/vascular melasma, hydroquinone intolerance or maintenance [144]. Oral TXA blocks plasminogen–keratinocyte binding→less UV-plasmin/prostaglandin signaling and melanogenesis; 500–750 mg/day reportedly gives ~10–40% reduction, but relapse is high within 2 months of stopping [28,84]. After topical failure it may be adjunctive, but superiority is unproven; small, uncontrolled, variable-dose trials and risks (headache/GI effects, hypomenorrhea, rash/alopecia, ALT elevation, thromboembolism) require screening [28,84]. A 75–80% global benchmark lacks a denominator [144].

Refractory mixed/dermal melasma: an RCT (62; 31/arm) found IPL+bleaching/sunscreen MASI −49.4% at 6 months and −44.9% at 12 (17.6→8.9→9.7; IGA p=.002); monotherapy/recurrence NR, combination safe [123]. **Durability and comparability**

**Evidence-weighted comparison**

| Modality | Best-supported result | Main limitation or deciding condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydroquinone, tretinoin, and triple-combination cream (TCC)** | Hydroquinone (HQ) 4–5% remains a comparator; with HQ 4%, UV-visible sunscreen produced a **77.8% ± 11% MASI reduction at 8 weeks**, versus **61.9% ± 16%** with UV-only sunscreen. Tretinoin 0.1% improved melasma in 68% versus 5% with vehicle at 40 weeks. Daily Tri-Luma in 228 patients produced near-clearance at 12 months. [72] | HQ commonly irritates and carries a rare ochronosis risk, particularly after more than 6 months in skin of colour. TCC caused 129 transient application-site reactions and 6 telangiectasias in the 228-patient study; prolonged or recurrent use was associated with telangiectasia, atrophy, hypertrichosis, and acneiform eruptions. |
| **Azelaic acid, kojic acid, cysteamine, and topical tranexamic acid (TXA)** | In 29 patients with mild melasma, azelaic acid 20% twice daily reduced MASI from **7.6 ± 3.5 to 3.8 ± 2.8 at 2 months**, versus **7.2 ± 3.2 to 6.2 ± 3.6** with HQ, with less erythema and pruritus. Kojic acid has tyrosinase-inhibitory and antioxidant actions, but the reported clinical result involved TXA, kojic acid, and niacinamide rather than kojic-acid monotherapy. Cysteamine 5% was comparable with HQ plus ascorbic acid for mMASI and quality of life; adverse-event comparison was NR. Topical TXA 3% was comparable with HQ plus dexamethasone, whereas 5% TXA did not outperform vehicle in 21 women. [72] | Formulation, penetration, route, and co-ingredients vary substantially; these agents should not be treated as interchangeable or assigned a universal recurrence rate. |
| **Photoprotection and oral TXA** | Broad-spectrum sunscreen used three times daily reduced mean MASI from **12.38 ± 14.7 to 9.15 ± 4.7 at 12 weeks** in 100 patients. Iron-oxide visible-light protection limited the 6-month median MASI increase to 0.45 versus 2.43 without visible-light protection. Oral TXA 250 mg twice daily produced MASI-50 in 100% versus 53% with monthly intralesional TXA at 3 months; a 42-patient observational cohort reported 73.8% improvement over 12.7 months. [72] | Photoprotection is foundational adjunctive and maintenance care, not proof of sunscreen monotherapy superiority; a defined photoprotection-specific relapse rate was not reported. Oral TXA requires systemic screening because headache, gastrointestinal symptoms, and menstrual changes occur, and safety beyond roughly 12–16 weeks is incompletely established. |
| **Chemical peels, microneedling, and PRP** | In a 30-patient, 12-week comparison, glycolic- and trichloroacetic-acid peels improved MASI more than lactic acid, while good/excellent tolerance was 90% for lactic acid versus 30% for glycolic acid and 20% for TCA. Microneedling evidence remains low-level: an eight-study review found frequent topical co-intervention confounding. PRP reduced MASI from **15.5 ± 8.4 to 9.5 ± 7.2** after three applications in 23 patients; a split-face study found mMASI reduction of **53.66% ± 11.27%** versus **45.65% ± 8.10%** with intradermal TXA. [72] | Deeper peels may improve efficacy but increase pigmentary risk. Adding TCA to glycolic acid did not significantly improve MASI and produced more adverse effects. Microneedling plus topical TXA was not significantly different from QS Nd:YAG plus topical TXA in a 56-patient trial, but caused more pain and erythema. |
| **IPL and other light** | IPL efficacy is mixed: PIH occurred in 14.3% in one split-face trial; in 17 patients treated with IPL plus HQ, the relative melanin index rose from 39.8 at week 16 to 50.1 at week 36, and two developed PIH. Among 32 patients with phototypes IV–V, 12 improved, 10 did not, 4 deteriorated, and 6 stopped because of PIH. Red or infrared LED evidence is low; blue light may worsen pigmentation. [72] | Thermal stimulation can aggravate melasma, especially in darker skin; lower fluence is advised when IPL is considered. |
| **Low-fluence 1064-nm QS Nd:YAG** | It targets dermal melanin with selective photothermolysis and limited epidermal injury. Fluence of 1.5 J/cm² over four passes and 3 J/cm² over two passes produced similar responses, with less pain at the lower fluence. Efficacy was comparable with TCC, Jessner peeling, and low-fluence 755-nm QS laser, and superior to glycolic-acid peeling in the cited comparisons. [72] | Relapse is frequent, and higher energy increases hypopigmentation, PIH, and rebound hyperpigmentation. It is not first-line treatment and should be reserved for carefully selected, refractory disease under experienced supervision. |
| **Fractional and picosecond lasers** | Fractional photothermolysis can reduce MASI and melanin indices but causes erythema, dryness, and PIH, particularly above 5% coverage; one study reported transient PIH in 28% of phototypes III–IV. The 1927-nm thulium laser was reported to provide sustained improvement with better tolerance than lower-wavelength fractional devices. Picosecond treatment produced mMASI improvement from **10.8 to 2.7 at 6 weeks and 3.6 at 12 weeks** after nine 1064-nm sessions; efficacy was similar to QS Nd:YAG, with acneiform eruption 0% versus 31.3%. [72] | Recurrence after picosecond treatment remains high. Picosecond laser plus topical TXA improved hemi-MASI more than laser alone but caused less redness and sensitivity when used alone; adding HQ produced no additional mMASI reduction and increased PIH risk. |

TCC (HQ 4%/tretinoin 0.05%/fluocinolone 0.01%) beat HQ 4% alone in 120 facial-melasma patients over 8 weeks (>75% improvement, 73% vs 49%) [206]. In a 17-patient split-face pilot, TCC+PDL versus TCC yielded MASI 2.79 vs 4.35 at treatment end and 4.15 vs 6.06 after one summer (P=.02); tolerance fell and PIH occurred in 3 phototype-IV patients, while relapse prevention remained unproven [185].

QS 1064-nm laser improved mMASI 75.9% after five weekly sessions in 22 Asians; hypopigmentation, rebound and recurrence in all made benefit temporary [207]. Combined QS+microdermabrasion achieved >75% clearance in 81% and ≥6-month remission, without a monotherapy control [203]. Fractional 1927-nm thulium is water-absorbing thermal treatment: MASI fell 29% in 68 Korean women after three sessions; recurrence was 13/60 (21.7%) at 4.1 months, with transient erythema, edema, dryness and PIH [204]. Corticosteroids have lower-level efficacy evidence and misuse harms; glycolic/TCA peels produced 79%/73% MASI reductions at 12 weeks, but 2025 safety data are ineligible [185,255,76]. Cysteamine’s review was ongoing and dated 3 August 2024; no eligible conclusion exists [208].

Cutoff: 31 March 2024; 1 Apr 2024 IPL, 6 Jul 2024 thulium, 21 Nov 2025 TXA correction and Aug 2026 market reports are excluded [215][213][209][214]. A 532-nm fractional Nd:YAG partial-unilateral-lentiginosis case and a 2020 blue-LED infant-bilirubin study are indirect, not melasma evidence [218][216].

Pre-cutoff IPL trial (n=17): four IPL+hydroquinone sessions reduced relative melanin index 39.8% versus 11.6% with hydroquinone; 2 had PIH and 24.2% of improvers had recurrent pigmentation within 24 weeks—combination, not monotherapy [270]. Low-dose 1064-nm QS Nd:YAG (20 patients; five weekly treatments) was called effective but lacked comparator, safety, recurrence or durability data; a 2016 case report described darkening and punctate leukoderma [332][210].

ICNIRP reports increasing laser/IPL/LED cosmetic availability, but regulation varies and this does not establish universal approval or a melasma indication; safety evidence is largely uncontrolled. Pain, erythema, edema and pigment change predominate; burns, scarring and eye injury are rarer, with dark skin, high sun exposure, photosensitizers and pregnancy increasing concern [212]. Post-cutoff Q-switched (5–30-ns/photoacoustic) and FDA IPL (20 May 2024; no clinical testing) records are unusable [219][220]; indirect tretinoin/corticosteroid sources give no direct melasma efficacy, recurrence or combination result [221][222][223].

RCTs best support causality; observational evidence is noncausal, and internal/external validity both matter [224]. Cochrane: TCC>HQ, RR1.58 (95%CI1.26–1.97); 20 studies/2,125 participants, but heterogeneity/short follow-up limit certainty [229]. KA: >50% clearance 60% vs47.5% (40 women); KA+GA vs HQ+GA (n39): equal51%, KA28%, HQ21%; dermatitis5/8 at1–12mo, safety assessed <1%; recurrence NR [303,246]. Azelaic acid inhibits epidermal melanogenesis; pregnancy/lactation safety reported; efficacy/combination NR [205]. Niacinamide 5% or 2%+2% TXA (4–8wk): insufficient undated patch evidence; vitamin C: 7 studies,1–20%, durability NR [306,314]. Cysteamine: 2 double-blind RCTs; comparable to TCC/TXA, better tolerated; odor/oxidation [309,311]. Microdermabrasion: upper-layer exfoliation; discoloration/≤24h redness; melasma outcomes NR [227]. PRP uncertain [230]. Oral TXA: 44 RCTs/3,164; 500mg/day,1–6mo; +topicals improved MASI at4/8/12wk; GI/menstrual effects; recurrence/screening NR [231]. Picosecond: pre-cutoff 62-article review; no melasma estimate/certainty [182].

Combination evidence is regimen-specific. In 50 women, clobetasol 0.05%→HQ 4% (14+46 d) gave 43.1% mMASI reduction at day 60 vs 44.8% HQ (P>0.1); lead-in not advisable [238]. Low-fluence 1,064-nm laser+drugs beat laser alone (MD 1.78); laser+laser did not (MD 0.05); laser+IPL beat IPL alone (MD 3.23) [330]. Recurrence: 16.7%/22 (10 weekly), 58.8%/34 at 1 y, 81%/20 Latin American after stopping; definitions/maintenance differed, hypopigmentation incidence 2%/147 [77]. IPL: noncoherent polychromatic photothermolysis; Korean split-face found no difference vs Q-switched Nd:YAG after 6 sessions (p=.44) [325]. 2023 FDA IPL records concern hair removal, not melasma efficacy [234][235]. Fractional water-targeting: ablative vaporizes epi/dermis; nonablative coagulates; reported ~12.5% prolonged erythema and >12% PIH in phototypes III–VI (not melasma) [233]. Microneedling+topicals beat topical alone: moderate effect at 8 wk, large at 12–16 wk (12 studies/459); adjunct evidence [217]. LED unestablished; PRP-specific result NR; microdermabrasion has no direct comparison [211][217][232]. Topical TXA 2% improved mMASI in 22/23 over 12 wk (mostly mild); relapse NR [315]. SPF70 tinted sunscreen was co-used ≥3×/day; no sunscreen-only comparator [238].

**Combination evidence, maintenance, and safety**

Direct evidence supports particular regimens, not combination therapy universally. In a 12-week Multan randomized trial, at least 50% MASI reduction was reported in 82.5% with hydroquinone plus tretinoin plus topical steroid versus 49.1% with hydroquinone alone (reported denominator: 57; [335]). In another 8-week multicentre trial of 120 people with moderate-to-severe facial melasma, more-than-75% improvement occurred in 73% with triple-combination cream versus 49% with hydroquinone; 35% versus 5% had lesions approximately matching surrounding skin, with similar safety and no drug-related discontinuations ([305]). A 20-woman study using hydroquinone 4% plus tretinoin 0.025% and sunscreen reported 80% (16/20) with at least one-grade severity improvement and 60% (12/20) with at least 51% improvement at week 12, but had no monotherapy comparator; three participants developed treatment-related dryness, erythema, peeling or stinging, one moderately ([241]).

Postcutoff cards [363,366,369] excluded. Microneedle-peel: n=5; 3–6 monthly sessions; change 100%, satisfaction 46→80%; uncontrolled, recurrence NR; infection/bleeding/PIH—sterile clinician [79]. QS-Nd:YAG: 1064 nm; n=50 Asian women; 15 weekly, 8 mm/2.8 J·cm⁻²/10 Hz; improvement 50–74%; mixed photothermal/photoacoustic; erythema/burning/edema 1–24 h; small add-on gains reported [243]. Glycolic 35% peel: mMASI 3.6→2.5 at 12 wk (P=.004); stinging 80%, burning 20%; recurrence NR, combination NS (P=.441) [245]. TCA-combination peel: mMASI 0.48 vs 0.53 at 75 d (P=.274), recurrence NR [244]. NAFL 1550 nm: safety-only; 71/286 effects (24.8%), PIH/melasma worsening; density risk [246].

Melasma recurrence or relapse generally means return or worsening after treatment completion: one operational definition is a >25% MASI increment at week 16, whereas resistant melasma may mean either recurrence after completion or ineffectiveness of one well-executed regimen [183][190].
