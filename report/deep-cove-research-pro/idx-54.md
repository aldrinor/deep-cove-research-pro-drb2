# AI Scaffolding for K–12 EFL Writing: Student Agency and Creativity, 2018–November 2024

**Writing and self-regulation show the clearest benefits:** AI-integrated tools can scaffold school students’ English writing through timely language feedback, support for composing decisions, and explicit strategy instruction. The strongest controlled evidence is Liu et al.’s elementary EFL trial, in which LLM-supported instruction improved writing performance, self-regulated learning strategy use, and motivation compared with traditional strategy instruction. Most gains persisted at a one-month follow-up. [1]

**Agency and creativity require different judgments:** Studies document students interpreting feedback, choosing revisions, and improving free-writing performance. These findings support carefully bounded claims about decision-making and opportunities for creative expression; they provide a less direct basis for claims about developing authorial identity, originality, genre breadth, or creative risk-taking. Teacher guidance, understandable feedback, and proficiency-sensitive support are central to this distinction. [2][3]

**Organization of the review:** Part 1 summarizes the studies’ technologies, participants, instructional designs, activities, and findings. Part 2 compares three scaffolding approaches, then examines the four requested dimensions of agency and four dimensions of creativity, distinguishing measured outcomes from interpretations of how support works.

**Designs answer different questions:** Liu et al.’s randomized trial offers the clearest comparison of two instructional approaches. Because both groups received SRL strategy instruction, its positive result concerns adding LLM support to an established teaching model—not simply comparing technology-rich teaching with an absence of strategy instruction. The trial therefore provides particularly relevant evidence for the agency question. [1]

**Process studies explain the decisions:** Krange et al.’s classroom interactions, the Korean Grammarly screencasts, and the German ProWritingAid change records illuminate what learners do between receiving feedback and producing a revised text. They show that feedback uptake involves interpretation and judgment. These studies are especially valuable for understanding agency, even though their findings answer a different question from a controlled comparison of average writing gains. [3][12][7]

**Experience outcomes need precise interpretation:** Woo et al. measured students’ experiences of learning to use ChatGPT, while Hsiao and Chang examined an autonomy-oriented course and its learning experiences. Their findings are consequential for instructional design, but satisfaction, an enjoyable presentation, or an autonomy-oriented course objective is not itself a demonstrated increase in autonomous writing. The same distinction applies to creativity: participation in free-writing is relevant evidence, but a writing-achievement result and an originality result describe different achievements. [4][6]

**Publication timing follows first appearance:** Online-first publication determines inclusion within the requested period. For example, Liu et al.’s article was published online on 12 August 2024, although it subsequently appeared in a 2026 journal issue. Its findings belong within the review’s November 2024 cutoff. [1]

For this record, the deciding condition is the distinction between implementation evidence and outcome evidence. Woo et al. provide direct evidence that ChatGPT was integrated into a secondary-school EFL writing activity and that prompt-engineering instruction was a consequential design issue. However, the available report does not directly measure autonomy in writing decisions, self-regulated learning behaviors, control over the writing process, voice or identity expression, AI-enhanced ideation, novel language combinations, genre development, or creative risk-taking. The workshop context, prior prompt-engineering instruction, unspecified tool version and duration, and absence of a control or writing-performance comparison also prevent attributing the reported motivation, cognitive-load, or satisfaction patterns to ChatGPT alone [15][16].

[23] The publication record displays **14 June 2024** and identifies the item as issue 18/2024, while the accessible report copy is labeled a **December 2023 preprint**; the intervention and data-collection dates are not reported. [22][23][24] The study therefore falls within the reported 2024 publication year, but the supplied records do not independently resolve the online-first/issue-date basis or document peer-review status. Its final eligibility should remain **pending verification**, rather than being presented as fully verified peer-reviewed evidence.

The study’s evidence is directly informative about motivation, cognitive load, satisfaction, and learner–AI interaction, but not about causal improvement in writing, agency, or creativity. The small voluntary sample, single-school setting, short workshop, and absence of a reported comparison group limit generalizability and causal interpretation. The machine-in-the-loop design foregrounds learner choice and control, but that design feature should not be mistaken for a measured change in autonomy, self-regulation, process control, voice, identity, or creativity. Vietnam’s **47-student** WAB study (2025-Apr) and Korea’s **45-student** generative-AI picture-book study (2024-12) are also post-cutoff [82][88].

The **78-university-learner** study sometimes associated with e001 is separate: it used 38 experimental and 40 control participants, an eight-week AWCF/Paragraph Punch intervention, and reported experimental scores of **10.6 to 15.6**, *t*(36) = **20.9**, *p* < .001. Its 750-word story-continuation task included creativity, but low-performing-group selection, prior knowledge, same-instructor teaching, and multiple interventions prevent attributing those results to e001 or ChatGPT [46]. Secondary mappings are discovery-only: one review reports 4 high-school and 2 middle-school studies among 21, while another reports only **14.49%** K-12 representation among 69 ChatGPT studies; neither verifies a primary-study row [73][27].

Wilson’s automated-writing reports are possible companion reports because they share authorship, automated feedback/scoring, and an elementary-school context, but the supplied evidence does not establish that they are one underlying school-level EFL AI-writing study [63]. The Galloway citation is a duplicate bibliographic listing rather than evidence for the target study [61]; undetected duplicate publication can double-count participants, so reports should be linked at the study level using authors, sites, dates, and sample size [59][56].

## Part 2a — Thematic Analysis: AI Scaffolding Approaches

**Three functions organize the evidence:** Cognitive support tools scaffold thinking about writing; creative support systems provide starting points and possibilities for expression; language enhancement tools help learners improve linguistic form and clarity. These are instructional functions rather than mutually exclusive product categories. A generative system can serve different functions depending on whether students request a planning question, an idea, or a correction.

**Tool choice should follow purpose:** The literature supports matching the scaffold to the writing difficulty while retaining a meaningful task for the learner. A tool’s ability to generate fluent text does not establish that generating a complete text is its most educationally useful role. The practical comparison is therefore between forms of assistance and the student decisions each form preserves.

| Instructional need | Scaffold category and examples | Evidence anchor | Student’s central responsibility | Outcome to monitor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Planning, monitoring, interpreting feedback | Cognitive support: CALLA-LLM, EAT | Liu et al.; Krange et al. | Set priorities and explain revisions | Strategy use and writing performance |
| Beginning and developing open-ended writing | Creative support: WRTN, ChatGPT | Jeong and Ahn; Woo et al. | Select, transform, and develop possibilities | Free-writing achievement and student-authored ideas |
| Revising language and clarity | Language enhancement: Grammarly, Write&Improve, ProWritingAid | School feedback studies | Judge suggestions against intended meaning | Accuracy, clarity, and justified feedback choices |

### Cognitive support tools

**The strongest link concerns regulation:** Cognitive scaffolding is most clearly supported when AI is embedded in instruction that explicitly develops how learners manage writing. Liu et al.’s CALLA-LLM model improved SRL strategy use alongside writing performance and motivation over five weeks. Because the comparison group also learned strategies through traditional CALLA, the result supports the instructional value of the LLM-supported model relative to that active alternative. [1]

**Cognitive support keeps thinking visible:** Planning, questioning, evaluating alternatives, and deciding how to respond to a problem are different from receiving a corrected sentence. Lee’s contextual L2 study makes this distinction concrete. The six adolescents using Essay Critiquing System 2.0 demonstrated questioning, reading, reasoning, planning, evaluative comments, and problem-solving; the analysis identified 48 composing strategies. Automated content feedback therefore operated within an active composing process rather than replacing every mental activity involved in writing. [8]

**Content decisions remain intellectually important:** Lee’s participants were most concerned with what to write, followed by how to express themselves. This matters because language-learning difficulties can make writing appear to be mainly a problem of correct English. The study instead shows adolescents attending to meaning and content while working with automated assistance. As contextual evidence, it helps explain why a useful scaffold should support the student’s intended message as well as linguistic execution. [8]

**Feedback interpretation is a learning task:** Krange et al.’s study identifies three connected demands: recognizing feedback, understanding its significance, and using it in writing. Fifty-six eighth-graders received automatic feedback on individual essays and discussed it with teachers and peers. Teachers helped students get started, identify errors, and share interpretations. The resulting instructional unit was therefore not simply “student plus software,” but an interaction among feedback, student reasoning, and classroom dialogue. [3]

**Human mediation makes support usable:** Across EAT and CALLA-LLM, teachers remain involved in making AI assistance educationally productive. The EAT findings connect usefulness to supported interpretation, while Liu et al. explicitly endorse a human-in-the-loop approach. Together, these studies support a strong design judgment: automated feedback should be integrated with opportunities to understand and act on it. They do not require interpreting every teacher intervention as a reduction in learner independence. [3][1]

**Assistance and substitution differ educationally:** A question that helps a student reconsider an argument leaves the student responsible for the argument; a ready-made replacement can remove that responsibility. This is an interpretation of the reviewed process evidence, not a separate experimental finding. Its practical implication is to prefer assistance that makes the next decision intelligible: what problem matters, what alternatives exist, and why a revision would improve communication.

**Cognitive demand requires careful calibration:** Woo et al.’s workshop provides an important counterpoint to the assumption that AI always reduces effort. Students reported high cognitive load during the writing task, especially while engineering prompts. A flexible conversational interface can therefore create an additional learning task: students must learn to request useful help while also composing in English. Cognitive scaffolding succeeds when this additional task remains manageable. [4]

**Judgment for cognitive scaffolding:** The best-supported approach combines AI assistance with explicit strategy teaching and feedback interpretation, leaving learners responsible for understanding the writing problem and selecting a response.

### Creative support systems

**Free-writing provides the clearest school example:** Jeong and Ahn’s WRTN study offers the most directly relevant school-level result for open-ended writing. Forty-one sixth-graders participated in eight lessons over four months, and the reported writing-achievement benefit was particularly evident in free-writing. The study also examined interest, motivation, confidence, anxiety, and difficulties associated with different writing levels. [2]

**Creative support expands possible directions:** Generative systems can be used to supply prompts, possible continuations, alternative wording, or questions about an emerging text. Their distinctive instructional potential is to provide material that a student can select, change, combine, or reject. The creative contribution of the scaffold lies in opening possibilities for the writer; the student’s creative contribution lies in deciding what those possibilities become.

**Open-ended activity is not originality:** Free-writing allows comparatively open expression, but it is an activity format rather than a genre or a validated creativity measure. WRTN’s positive result therefore supports the usefulness of generative assistance for completing open-ended English writing. It does not justify treating all improved free-writing as more original writing. Better performance could reflect stronger expression, greater confidence, better understanding of the task, or a combination of these processes. [2]

**Differentiation changes the creative opportunity:** Jeong and Ahn identified different AI-use difficulties across writing levels and matched them with different forms of support. This is more informative than a general recommendation to give everyone the same prompt template. Students’ access to creative possibilities depends partly on whether they can understand and manage the assistance offered.

| Writing level | Reported difficulty | Implemented support | Instructional significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher level | Time management | Prepare the AI tool before class | Preserve time for composing |
| Intermediate level | Understanding complex-grammar feedback | Adjust response difficulty | Make assistance understandable |
| Lower level | Selecting suitable vocabulary | Teacher guidance and modified AI commands | Support meaningful lexical choices |

The level-specific difficulties and responses are reported in Jeong and Ahn’s WRTN study [2].

**More possibilities also create work:** Woo et al.’s findings show why a generative system should not automatically be treated as an effortless brainstorming partner. High satisfaction coexisted with high cognitive load. For a developing EFL writer, evaluating several AI responses, formulating another request, and deciding what to retain may compete with developing the text itself. The observed load supports simplifying the interaction; it does not establish that students’ creativity declined. [4]

**Student transformation is the key distinction:** A creative-support design should allow a learner to depart from the system’s first suggestion. As a pedagogical implication, a teacher might ask students to explain how they changed an offered idea, combined alternatives, or pursued their own direction. These activities make the student’s contribution visible. They are proposed applications of the evidence about composing decisions and differentiated support, rather than outcomes already demonstrated by the WRTN intervention.

**Creative support can begin with language:** A student may have an imaginative idea but insufficient English to develop it. In that situation, assistance with vocabulary or feedback comprehension can support creative expression without supplying the idea itself. Jeong and Ahn’s lower-level learners’ vocabulary-selection difficulties illustrate why creativity support and language support cannot be separated completely. The instructional priority is to help the learner express an intention, not assume that difficulty expressing it means an absence of ideas. [2]

**Judgment for creative scaffolding:** Generative systems are promising supports for beginning and developing open-ended writing, especially when assistance is calibrated to proficiency and students retain responsibility for transforming ideas.

### Language enhancement tools

**Writing improvement is the recurring result:** Grammarly, Write&Improve, ProWritingAid, and ChatGPT were used to provide feedback on language or writing quality. These tools differ in interface and scope, but their shared instructional function is to help learners notice a gap between a draft and a communicative or linguistic goal. The relevant school studies report writing-performance gains, accuracy gains, or evidence of meaningful engagement with revision. [9][11][7]

**Controlled comparisons support language-focused assistance:** Nguyễn Phúc Sơn’s study divided 30 disadvantaged Vietnamese middle-school students into equal groups—30 ÷ 2 = 15 students per group—and reported a significant Write&Improve effect on writing assessed through four Cambridge-based criteria. Asyifa compared 31 Grammarly users with 31 controls and also reported a significant writing benefit. These findings support language-feedback tools as useful components of school writing instruction. [9][11]

**Significance and magnitude answer different questions:** The positive tests in these studies indicate a reported difference under their respective instructional conditions. They do not establish that one product is superior to another. The studies used different populations, tasks, and assessment arrangements, so a comparison between Write&Improve and Grammarly should concern their instructional functions and observed patterns rather than an invented ranking of effectiveness.

**ChatGPT produced a mixed outcome profile:** Alzahrani and Alotaibi’s eight-week high-school study reported improvements in task achievement, coherence/cohesion, lexical resource, and overall writing, alongside a small decline in grammatical range and accuracy. The criterion-level pattern is more informative than the overall positive conclusion alone. It shows that improvement in one aspect of writing should not be assumed to extend uniformly to every other aspect. [5]

| Writing criterion | Pretest mean | Posttest mean | Calculated change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Task achievement | 3.0 | 5.2 | +2.2 points |
| Coherence and cohesion | 3.2 | 5.0 | +1.8 points |
| Lexical resource | 3.0 | 4.4 | +1.4 points |
| Grammatical range and accuracy | 3.0 | 2.9 | −0.1 points |

These are the study’s reported criterion means; changes are calculated as posttest minus pretest. The authors separately reported overall writing scores of 12.3 and 17.4, a calculated increase of 5.1 points. [5]

**The grammar result resists simple explanations:** The decline from 3.0 to 2.9 is a useful counterexample to claims that ChatGPT assistance necessarily improves grammatical performance. However, the pre/post design does not isolate whether the change reflects AI use, instructional priorities, task differences, or other influences. The defensible implication is to monitor separate writing dimensions, particularly when a tool is being used for several purposes at once.

**Feedback acceptance is not the goal:** In the Korean Grammarly case, both students analyzed feedback and selectively incorporated it, with accompanying accuracy improvement. The German ProWritingAid pilot similarly documented cognitive engagement when learners rejected suggestions that did not fit their intended meaning. These results challenge the idea that successful feedback use should be measured simply by the proportion of suggestions accepted. [12][7]

**Comprehension shapes the benefit:** The Korean case linked differences in revision actions, accuracy improvement, and perceived feedback comprehensibility to proficiency and cognitive burden. This identifies a mechanism connecting language enhancement with agency: learners need enough understanding to judge a recommendation. A correction that can be clicked into place but not understood may improve the immediate text while contributing less clearly to an informed writing decision. [12]

**Teacher attention can remain higher-order:** Asyifa describes a division of instructional labor in which Grammarly supports grammar, style, clarity, self-assessment, and revision, while teachers attend to organization and argumentation. This is a useful pedagogical rationale, not a demonstrated measurement of teacher time saved. Its importance is that automated language feedback can be positioned as one source of support within broader writing instruction rather than treated as a complete account of writing quality. [11]

**Judgment for language enhancement:** Language tools offer the most consistent practical support for revision, but their educational value depends on students understanding suggestions and evaluating them against meaning—not maximizing compliance with automated feedback.

**Additional documented evidence**

AI scaffolding is coded here by documented function rather than product name. **Cognitive support** assists planning, organizing, monitoring, evaluating, problem solving, or revision; **creative support** assists ideation, elaboration, experimentation, genre development, or narrative cocreation; and **language enhancement** assists grammar, vocabulary, spelling, translation, fluency, cohesion, style, or language-focused revision. The categories may overlap, but tool availability, fluent output, or a generic writing task alone does not establish a category.

**Cognitive support tools**

**Cognitive-support evidence is strongest for Woo et al.’s 2024 ChatGPT study.** Published on 14 June 2024, the peer-reviewed report by Woo, Wang, Guo, and Susanto used ChatGPT with 21 Hong Kong secondary-school students in a workshop involving a 500-word English-language writing task. The mixed-methods design included a pre-workshop motivation questionnaire, think-aloud protocols during writing, and a post-workshop questionnaire on motivation, cognitive load, and satisfaction [15][41]. Students were introduced to ChatGPT and taught prompt-engineering skills before completing the task. Motivation did not differ significantly before and after the workshop, although the mean increased slightly; students reported high cognitive load, particularly during prompt engineering, but high overall satisfaction [15].

The study therefore supports a **cognitive-support-related instructional design**, but not an uncomplicated claim that ChatGPT reduced cognitive demands. Its documented mechanism was the combination of ChatGPT-assisted composition and explicit instruction in how to formulate prompts. The authors recommended iterative activity and instructional-material design and careful scaffolding of prompt-engineering instruction so that ChatGPT would support writing without overwhelming learners [15]. Thus, prompt engineering is evidence of a learner-facing cognitive and interactional scaffold, whereas the high cognitive-load finding is a material qualification rather than evidence of a beneficial cognitive effect.

The full instructional account describes possible ChatGPT writing uses including brainstorming, text generation, question answering, rewriting, summarizing, paraphrasing, translation, text analysis, and adding information [23]. It also describes workshop examples in which students were shown how to ask the system to adopt a role, act as a search engine, analyze or complete text, explain output, paraphrase, summarize, or translate. These passages document the tool’s available writing affordances and the instructional design, but they do not show that every function was implemented as an outcome-producing intervention in the 21-student study [23].

At the broader category level, cognitive-support functions include topic or outline development, information or corpus search, text modification, feedback and revision, responses to questions, sentence generation, and explanations. Prompt-engineering strategies can specify a system’s role, objective, context, constraints, and iterative refinement of output [30]. A separate secondary map describes ChatGPT-like systems as interlocutors, content providers, teaching assistants, or evaluators and associates natural-language processing with writing feedback on structure, coherence, and style [92]. These reviews are useful for functional coding and discovery, not as substitutes for primary empirical evidence.

Secondary records also name Argumate, automated writing-evaluation systems, Google Translate, and intelligent tutoring systems as examples of cognitive or process support [95]. However, the supplied records do not verify that these examples involve separable K–12 EFL writing samples meeting the present review criteria. They should therefore remain discovery leads rather than final examples in this section.

**Creative support systems**

**Creative-support classification requires more than text generation.** A creative-support system should document assistance with brainstorming, imaginative elaboration, alternative ideas or language, narrative shaping, genre experimentation, or other creative decisions in an actual writing activity. AI-generated fluency or a polished final product is not, by itself, evidence of student creativity.

Woo et al.’s 2024 study provides only **partial and indirect support** for a creative-support classification. Students completed a 500-word English writing task with ChatGPT, but the reported outcomes were motivation, cognitive load, satisfaction, and perceptions of the learning process—not ideation quality, originality, novel language combinations, genre development, or creative risk-taking [15]. The study’s title and supplied results therefore do not establish a direct creative-writing effect. Its discussion of brainstorming and text generation shows possible affordances, but those general capabilities should not be recoded as measured creative development in this study [23].

The secondary records contain several possible creative-writing leads. One review lists Woo, Wang, and Guo’s 2023 *Understanding Strategies for Creative Writing with NLG* as an EFL study concerning students’ idea generation; another lists Niloy et al.’s 2024 *Is ChatGPT a Menace for Creative Writing Ability? An Experiment* [30][31]. The supplied spans do not provide the latter studies’ separable K–12 samples, creativity definitions, instruments, comparisons, or findings. Consequently, neither record can be presented here as a verified eligible primary example without checking the original paper.

A school-writing review also describes Character.ai as supporting customizable characters, editing, regeneration, narrative shaping, and cocreation, and summarizes a 15-week chatbot experiment involving Chinese children learning English in which learners received comments on their writing and polished their productions [31]. Those descriptions suggest mechanisms for creative exploration and language practice, but they are secondary accounts and do not establish that the underlying studies meet every present eligibility criterion. Likewise, a review-level taxonomy identifies suggestions for topics, outlines, resources, and brainstorming through conversation as creative-support functions [30].

The distinction between generation and creativity is important. One review reports that AI-generated texts could show equal or higher historical understanding than student-produced texts while displaying less emotional commitment [31]. This counterexample indicates that informational quality or textual fluency should not be treated as equivalent to creative voice. The same review suggests that a permissive chatbot environment may make learners feel more reassured when taking risks in second-language writing, but this is a generalized interpretation rather than a direct finding from Woo et al.’s study [31].

**Language enhancement tools**

**Language enhancement is a plausible ChatGPT affordance but remains unverified as a study-specific function in Woo et al. (2024).** The primary report documents ChatGPT-supported English writing and prompt-engineering instruction, but the supplied evidence does not report a grammar, vocabulary, translation, fluency, cohesion, or form-feedback intervention or outcome [15]. The study should therefore not be coded as a confirmed language-enhancement intervention solely because ChatGPT was used for English writing.

The report’s instructional background does describe relevant language needs and potential uses: EFL learners may have difficulty retrieving intended English words or translating ideas from their first language, while ChatGPT can generate, rewrite, paraphrase, summarize, translate, and provide vocabulary or grammar assistance [23]. These are documented language-related affordances and possible classroom uses, not direct evidence that the 21 participants received or benefited from each function. The most defensible coding for this study is therefore **language-enhancement potential, implementation not fully reported**.

At the category level, language-enhancement tools can provide grammar and spelling correction, vocabulary development, translation, fluency support, structural coherence, formal style, and feedback on language use. Secondary syntheses describe a shift from surface correction toward discourse-level support for organization, argumentation, and stylistic coherence [20]. Other review-level evidence identifies feedback and revision support, content development, and vocabulary and grammar aid, while also warning about learner over-reliance, inconsistent feedback, and misinformation [30]. These risks are conditions for implementation, not findings that can be attributed directly to Woo et al.’s participants.

The school-writing review groups chatbot practice, automated assessment, comments on writing, polishing, and second-language writing support under language enhancement [31]. It also reports that AI feedback is not necessarily superior to expert teacher feedback and emphasizes that teacher guidance remains important. Its description of an 18-week Taiwan high-school case states that language proficiency predicted outcomes and that lower-performing students needed sustained support; AI functioned as a facilitator rather than a decisive determinant of results [31]. Because these claims are reported through a secondary review and the underlying primary reports are not supplied here, they remain contextual leads rather than verified final-study findings.

Additional review records name automated writing evaluation, machine translation, ChatGPT, NLG systems, and intelligent tutoring systems as possible language-enhancement examples [95][31]. The supplied evidence does not establish their K–12 EFL eligibility or connect them to the Woo et al. intervention. A separate automated-corrective-feedback and Paragraph Punch intervention documented grammar, lexicon, punctuation, spelling, brainstorming, sentence formation, and paragraph organization, but its record does not identify it as the ChatGPT study and it should not be transferred to that example [46].

**Functional overlap and evidence boundaries**

The deciding rule is whether the primary report documents a function in the actual writing activity. On that basis, Woo et al.’s 2024 study is best coded as **cognitive-support-related through prompt-engineering instruction and ChatGPT-assisted composition** [15]. Its creative-support and language-enhancement codes are **potential or unresolved**, because the supplied primary-study evidence does not directly document creative-writing development or language-focused feedback. A secondary synthesis labels the study’s ChatGPT use as generative and mixed active/passive and records increased motivation alongside high cognitive load, but it does not supply enough task-level detail to replace the primary report [20].

Several records must not be merged with Woo et al.’s study. *The Use of ChatGPT in Source-Based Writing Tasks* involved 27 Italian university students writing in Italian as their first language on 19 June 2024; despite its ChatGPT writing activity, it is not K–12 EFL evidence [33]. A 2025 ChatGPT-4 intervention with 105 fourth-grade students in Bursa, Türkiye, ran for 10 weeks and 80 class hours but addressed reading texts, comprehension questions, visuals, reading errors, comprehension, and motivation rather than English writing [32]. A separate 2025 GPT-4 argumentative-feedback study and later machine-in-the-loop reports are outside the November 2024 cutoff and are not eligible examples [18][40][42]. These identity and scope limits prevent generic ChatGPT functions from being incorrectly attributed to the eligible 2024 secondary-school writing study.

## Part 2b — Thematic Analysis: Impact on Student Agency

**Agency is clearest in purposeful action:** The strongest direct agency-related outcome is improved SRL strategy use in CALLA-LLM. Other studies illuminate more local expressions of agency: choosing content, interpreting feedback, deciding what to revise, and rejecting suggestions that conflict with intended meaning. These are important forms of participation in writing, but they should be distinguished from a broad developmental claim about becoming an autonomous author. [1][7]

### Autonomy in writing decisions

**Selective uptake demonstrates local autonomy:** The Korean middle-school case provides a concrete example of students acting on feedback rather than merely receiving it. Across three sessions, both learners analyzed Grammarly’s suggestions and chose what to incorporate. The significance for agency is the presence of selection and interpretation: students remained involved in determining the revised text. [12]

**Rejection can be productive engagement:** The ProWritingAid pilot strengthens this interpretation by showing that learners could remain cognitively engaged while rejecting corrections. Their decisions depended on whether suggestions matched what they intended to say. Thus, an accepted suggestion and a rejected suggestion can both express agency when the learner understands the alternatives and makes a reasoned decision. [7]

**Choice requires an intelligible alternative:** A menu of suggested revisions does not automatically create meaningful autonomy. If a learner cannot understand the explanation or evaluate the proposed wording, the apparent choice may be largely procedural. The Korean study’s proficiency and cognitive-burden differences show why autonomy depends on feedback comprehensibility. Differentiation is therefore not simply a means of improving scores; it can make students’ revision decisions more informed. [12]

**Content autonomy extends beyond correction:** Lee’s contextual L2 evidence broadens the focus from accepting language corrections to deciding what a text should communicate. All six adolescents prioritized content, and the proficiency groups worked toward completing their writing in their own ways. This offers a useful counterexample to equating AI-supported writing with passive dependence. It documents student reasoning within a technology-assisted task, while its contribution remains a description of those composing processes. [8]

**Generative options create a different challenge:** A correction tool usually responds to something the student has already written; a generative system can also supply material before the student has developed a position or idea. These arrangements distribute decision-making differently. The first can leave content choices relatively intact while supporting expression; the second requires deliberate attention to who selects the content and why. WRTN’s free-writing benefit makes generative assistance relevant, but the learner’s authorship must still be judged through the decisions made with that assistance. [2]

**Autonomy is compatible with guidance:** Teacher involvement need not mean that the teacher makes every decision. In EAT, help with interpreting feedback enabled students to participate in revision. A useful implication is to make assistance explanatory: discuss what a comment means, ask which goal it serves, and leave an understandable choice with the student. This applies the study’s mediation mechanism without assuming that independent work must be unsupported work. [3]

**Judgment on decision autonomy:** AI can support local autonomy when students understand, select, and sometimes reject its assistance; access to options alone is a weaker indicator than a reasoned choice.

### Self-regulated learning behaviors

**Explicit strategy instruction has the strongest support:** Liu et al.’s randomized trial directly targeted SRL strategy instruction in elementary EFL writing. The CALLA-LLM group improved significantly in strategy use, writing performance, and motivation and outperformed traditional CALLA. This is the most persuasive agency-related result because regulation was an explicit outcome, not inferred solely from students working alone or reporting satisfaction. [1]

**The active comparison matters:** Both groups learned SRL strategies over five weeks. Consequently, the comparison supports the LLM-enhanced instructional model relative to traditional strategy teaching. It does not support attributing the whole result to unstructured chatbot use. For teacher educators, the relevant alternative is therefore not simply “AI or no AI,” but how AI changes a strategy-based learning arrangement.

**Regulation connects several writing activities:** In practical terms, self-regulation involves setting a goal, choosing an approach, monitoring progress, interpreting feedback, and evaluating the resulting text. The CALLA-LLM result is consistent with treating AI as support for these linked activities. The educational aim is not to increase the number of interactions with the tool, but to help students use assistance in service of a writing goal.

**Follow-up supports bounded durability:** Most CALLA-LLM gains remained at the one-month follow-up. This strengthens the interpretation beyond an immediate post-intervention advantage. The appropriate conclusion is retention across the tested interval; transfer to every new genre, classroom, or unaided writing situation is a further developmental claim. The follow-up is particularly useful because agency concerns what students can continue to manage, not only the quality of a single supported product. [1]

**Observed strategies complement measured outcomes:** Lee’s verbal reports and stimulated recalls showed planning, evaluation, questioning, and problem-solving during writing. These observations help explain what cognitive activity can look like in an AI-assisted environment. Their role is complementary to Liu et al.’s outcome comparison: one makes composing processes visible, while the other tests an instructional model against an alternative. [8]

**Autonomy-oriented design is a different claim:** Hsiao and Chang designed their 18-week Linggle-supported course to cultivate autonomous EFL learners. The online–offline learning loop and the more favorable experience during student-centered presentations show how a course can organize participation around learners. However, the stated design objective should be distinguished from Liu et al.’s reported SRL strategy-use outcome. The Linggle study is strongest as evidence about a learning arrangement and its experiences. [6]

**Motivation findings are not interchangeable:** Woo et al.’s motivation questionnaire concerned learning about ChatGPT, including its value and students’ interest in learning to use it. The nonsignificant pre/post change therefore does not directly contradict Liu et al.’s writing-motivation result. The studies differ in construct, instructional duration, and design. This comparison illustrates why liking a tool, wanting to write, and regulating writing should remain separate analytical questions. [4]

**Judgment on self-regulation:** The strongest supported agency conclusion is that LLM assistance can improve SRL strategy instruction when it is integrated into a teacher-supported model with explicit learning goals.

### Control over the writing process

**Process control is exercised across stages:** Control concerns more than making one revision choice. It includes deciding when to seek assistance, which problem to address, how to move between drafting and revision, and when the text communicates the intended message. The reviewed process studies make parts of this activity visible through successive drafts, feedback logs, change tracking, and screencasts. [3][12]

**Revision records reveal more than final scores:** Krange et al. examined feedback logs and different versions of students’ essays alongside classroom interactions. This combination helps connect an automated comment with how students and teachers interpreted it and what changed in the writing. For an agency-focused reading, the crucial point is not just that a later version differs, but that the path toward it involves learner participation in understanding the revision problem. [3]

**Control can be shared productively:** The EAT findings indicate that students’ engagement with revision was supported by teachers and peers. This makes a purely individual definition of process ownership inadequate. A student can retain a meaningful role while discussing alternatives with others, just as a student can appear to work independently while following automated instructions without understanding them. The important distinction is whether the learner participates in directing the writing.

**Conversational flexibility brings management demands:** Woo et al.’s students had to learn prompt engineering while completing a 500-word composition. Their high reported load highlights the work involved in managing an open-ended interaction. A conversational system can offer flexible assistance, but students must also decide what to ask, recognize whether a response is useful, and decide whether another request is worth the time. Greater technical flexibility can therefore increase the need for process support. [4]

**Preparation can preserve composing time:** Jeong and Ahn’s higher-level learners encountered time-management difficulties, addressed through preparing the AI tool before class. This is an instructive contrast with the assumption that only lower-proficiency students need scaffolding. Proficient writers may understand English feedback yet still struggle to manage the tool within the writing period. Process control requires managing resources and time as well as language. [2]

**Operational control is not enough:** Typing a prompt or clicking “accept” shows that a student operates the interface; it does not by itself show that the student directs the composition. The Korean and ProWritingAid studies point toward a stronger indicator: students compare recommendations with their writing intentions and make selective revisions. This distinction gives teachers a practical way to interpret apparent independence without treating every visible interaction as equivalent evidence of agency. [12][7]

**Instruction should preserve a process trace:** As a pedagogical implication, a brief record of the student’s goal, requested help, adopted revision, and reason for rejecting another suggestion can make process ownership visible. Such a record applies the observational logic of the reviewed process studies. Its purpose is not to impose documentation on every sentence, but to distinguish student-directed revision from an unexplained substitution of AI-generated text.

**Judgment on process control:** AI-supported writing is most agency-preserving when students can direct the use of assistance and explain consequential changes across the writing process.

### Voice/identity expression

**Meaning-sensitive revision is the strongest anchor:** The ProWritingAid pilot’s evidence that students judged suggestions against intended meaning is directly relevant to protecting expression. It shows learners treating the machine’s recommendation as negotiable rather than authoritative. This is an important foundation for voice because a writer must be able to preserve what a text is meant to communicate. [7]

**Voice extends beyond correct wording:** Authorial voice concerns how a writer’s stance, tone, perspective, and relationship with an audience are expressed. Identity adds questions about how the student positions themselves and represents experiences or affiliations through writing. A text can become more accurate without becoming more personally distinctive, and a student can reject one correction without showing a sustained change in authorial identity. These distinctions define what the agency question asks beyond language improvement.

**Content attention creates an opening:** Lee’s adolescents’ concern with what to write suggests that students’ meanings remain active within automated-feedback writing. That observation supports taking their intentions seriously during revision. It should not be converted into a claim that AI strengthened identity expression. The study’s contribution is that it makes students’ content-related thinking visible and demonstrates why expression cannot be reduced to correcting form. [8]

**Confidence and voice describe different developments:** Jeong and Ahn reported favorable changes in writing-related affect, varying by writing level. Increased confidence can make expression more approachable, but confidence alone does not identify whose perspective is present in the final text. A learner may feel more confident using conventional language supplied by a system, or more confident developing an independently chosen message. The affective result supports an enabling condition for expression, not an interchangeable measure of voice. [2]

**A polished text can alter intention:** Consider a hypothetical learner who uses an informal sentence to express closeness to a family member. A more formal AI revision might be grammatically appropriate while changing the relationship conveyed to the reader. The agency-preserving response is not automatically to reject formal language, but to let the student judge which version fits the intended meaning. This example illustrates why meaning-sensitive feedback decisions matter for voice.

**Choice should remain expressively consequential:** The practical implication is to discuss edits in terms of communicative purpose, not only correctness. A student might accept a grammatical correction while retaining a preferred image, tone, or culturally meaningful expression. The ProWritingAid findings support the underlying principle of judging suggestions against intention; the broader claim about identity development remains separate from that observed revision behavior. [7]

**Judgment on voice and identity:** The literature supports preserving authorial intention through negotiable feedback, while its strongest agency findings concern strategy use and revision decisions rather than a demonstrated developmental change in identity.

**Practical synthesis for agency:** An agency-preserving application of these findings begins with a student’s writing purpose, uses AI for bounded help, requires evaluation of consequential suggestions, and ends with reflection on the strategy used. This is a pedagogical synthesis of the literature, not an additional tested intervention. Its central principle is that assistance should make students better able to explain and manage their writing, rather than make their decision-making less visible.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Agency evidence boundary and overall result**

**Supported result.** Woo, Wang, Guo, and Susanto’s peer-reviewed study was published on **14 June 2024** and involved **21 Hong Kong secondary-school EFL students** completing a **500-word English writing task** with ChatGPT support [36][39][15]. The mixed-methods workshop used a pre-workshop motivation questionnaire, think-aloud protocols during writing, and a post-workshop questionnaire measuring motivation, cognitive load, and satisfaction [15]. The study therefore directly documents an AI-supported writing activity, but it does **not** establish that ChatGPT increased or decreased student agency: no formal agency instrument or agency-specific outcome was reported [15][127].

The deciding condition across all four dimensions is therefore **learner judgment during interaction**, not the mere availability of ChatGPT or the polish of the final text. The study describes how control could be allocated, but its reported outcomes cannot show whether students retained, developed, or surrendered agency.

**Autonomy in writing decisions**

Woo et al.’s instructional design provides a conditional route to autonomy. In the described machine-in-the-loop cycle, a learner gives ChatGPT an instruction, receives generated text, evaluates that text, and decides whether to insert all, part, or none of it; the learner may then modify the material and complete the composition independently [23]. This arrangement differs from automatic text substitution because it formally assigns the learner responsibility for selection and integration. The reported task also asked students to distinguish their own words from chatbot-generated words, making the location of authorship more visible [23].

That mechanism should not be confused with an empirical autonomy result. The study did not report the frequency with which students rejected or modified suggestions, whether students generated their own ideas before prompting, or whether ChatGPT changed their authority over goals, content, wording, or revision. Its questionnaires addressed motivation, cognitive load, and satisfaction rather than decision authority [15][127]. Thus, the appropriate conclusion for **autonomy in writing decisions** is: *the scaffold was designed to permit learner control, but the study did not measure whether autonomy was strengthened, weakened, or unchanged*.

A separate 2024 Indonesian EFL-writing study reported that immediate ChatGPT feedback in a less anxiety-provoking environment **might** stimulate autonomy, but students also struggled to produce effective prompts and understand the feedback; the authors therefore emphasized teacher guidance [97]. Because the supplied record does not establish a K-12 population for that study, it is adjacent evidence rather than direct K-12 support. It nevertheless illustrates the relevant condition: immediate assistance may widen opportunities for independent decisions only when learners can interpret and judge the assistance.

**Self-regulated learning behaviours**

The strongest reported result for this dimension is a qualification rather than an improvement. Woo et al. collected think-aloud data during the task, but the reported questionnaires measured motivation to learn about ChatGPT, cognitive load, and satisfaction; they did not report a direct measure of planning, monitoring, reflection, revision regulation, or strategic help-seeking [15][127]. The study found **no significant difference in motivation before and after the workshop**, although the mean motivation score increased slightly; students reported **high cognitive load**, especially while learning prompt engineering, and **high overall satisfaction** with the workshop [15].

These measures should remain separate. Motivation indicates an affective response, satisfaction indicates perceived value or experience, and cognitive load indicates the demands imposed by the activity; none is equivalent to self-regulated writing behaviour. The high prompt-engineering load is particularly important because asking students to formulate effective instructions adds a new regulation task to planning and composing. The authors consequently recommended iterative activity and instructional-material design and careful scaffolding of prompt-engineering instruction so that ChatGPT would support writing without overwhelming students [15].

The study’s workshop structure could have supported self-regulation by requiring students to learn prompts and monitor generated responses, but the available results do not show whether that support transferred to independent writing. A stronger conclusion would require process traces linked to planning, monitoring, evaluation, revision, or later unaided writing. In the absence of those measures, Woo et al. provide evidence of **engagement potential and cognitive demand**, not evidence that ChatGPT produced more self-regulated learners.

**Control over the writing process**

Woo et al.’s reported writing model is control-preserving in principle. The student prompts the system; ChatGPT generates an option; the student evaluates it; and the student accepts, rejects, or modifies it before integrating it into the composition [23]. The cycle can occur during planning, drafting, and revision, while the student remains formally responsible for the completed text [23]. The instruction to mark student and AI words separately also creates a trace of how the text was assembled [23].

This mechanism supports process control only if learners actually perform the evaluative and authorial work. The study does not report how students used those choices as an outcome, nor does it compare students who critically revised output with students who transferred it with little examination. The available report instead records high cognitive load during the writing task, especially prompt engineering, and does not report a control-group comparison [15][16]. Consequently, the design demonstrates a **possible distribution of responsibility**, not an observed increase in control.

Teacher mediation and task structure are material conditions. Woo et al. introduced the tool in a workshop, taught prompt engineering, and recommended iterative scaffolding rather than unrestricted access [15]. This means that any agency-supporting mechanism belongs to the instructional arrangement—prompt instruction, opportunities to inspect output, and permission to modify it—as much as to ChatGPT itself. A polished AI-assisted composition would not, by itself, show that students controlled drafting, sequencing, revision, or authorship.

**Voice and identity expression**

No direct conclusion about voice or identity can be drawn from Woo et al. The supplied study summary reports motivation, cognitive load, satisfaction, experiences, and perceptions, but not learner voice, identity, cultural positioning, originality, ownership, or stance [16][127]. The own-word/AI-word distinction makes authorship an explicit design concern [23], yet the study does not report whether students felt that the resulting writing represented them or whether ChatGPT homogenized their expression.

This is an evidence gap rather than evidence that ChatGPT harmed voice. It is equally inappropriate to infer preserved voice from high satisfaction or to infer loss of identity from the use of generated text. Voice/identity claims would require analysis of students’ textual choices, reflections about ownership and stance, interviews focused on identity, or process evidence showing how learners accepted and reshaped suggestions. None of those outcomes is reported for the study.

**Conditions and limits on interpretation**

The evidence is limited by the study’s context and design. The participants were a **voluntary convenience sample of 21 students in one Hong Kong secondary school**, and the available report describes a workshop rather than a long-term comparison [23]. The evidence consists largely of questionnaires and think-aloud data; the supplied record does not report a control-group comparison or a follow-up test of independent writing [16]. These features support cautious description of student experience and task demands, not a causal claim that ChatGPT changed agency.

The result is also context-dependent. A 500-word task, initial prompt-engineering instruction, teacher framing, students’ prior writing proficiency, and the short workshop exposure may all affect how much responsibility students can retain. The reported high cognitive load indicates that the same tool that offers assistance can introduce additional regulation demands [15]. The most defensible synthesis is therefore conditional: ChatGPT can be embedded in a writing process that leaves formal decisions with the learner, but agency depends on whether the instructional design makes evaluation, revision, and authorship visible and manageable. For Woo et al. (2024), those conditions were described, while changes in autonomy, self-regulation, process control, and voice were not directly measured.

**Direct finding.** Woo et al.’s 2024 study (peer-review and exact-date verification remain pending) involved 21 Hong Kong secondary EFL students learning prompt engineering and attempting a 500-word composition with ChatGPT in a workshop [15][28]. Questionnaires and think-alouds found no significant motivation change (only a slight increase), high satisfaction, and high cognitive load during prompting [28]. Engagement is therefore only indirect; autonomy, self-regulation, process control, and voice were not measured [28][37].

**Autonomy/self-regulation.** For Woo et al.’s study, these relationships are **not reported**, not null [19][37]. A screening synthesis found autonomy in only *k* = 4 learner-perception studies, without instruments or separation from other agency dimensions [30]. A 2023 university ChatGPT intervention reported movement from external regulation to independent writing management, but its 50 Chinese undergraduates and 12-week design are non-K-12; its evidence comprised writing outcomes, motivation, interviews, and self-regulation discussion—not direct agency measurement [109]. Citation-only 2022–24 Grammarly, AWE, natural-language-generation, and story-writing records lack verified grade, measures, or direction [129][108].

**Process control.** No verified K-12 evidence here records planning, drafting, acceptance or rejection of suggestions, or ownership. A 2025 university study used a five-dimension, 1–5 rubric (maximum 25); AI and teacher gains were similar (*t*(118) = 0.55, *p* = .586, *d* = .10), making this a writing-performance proxy rather than agency evidence [18]. An ineligible college trace study found lower perceived ownership after whole-essay generation but comparable ownership with planning or reviewing use (H(5) = 27.876, *p* < .001) [110]. Adjacent higher-education evidence warns that low proficiency, prompt burden, over-reliance, and absent human oversight can replace learner effort [128].

**Voice/identity.** No eligible primary study here directly measures this dimension. A 2026 mixed-method study found re-editing to restore preferred style and possible enabling or suppressing effects, but its 20 Algerian first-year university students are out of scope [93]. Genre, tone, voice, or formality adjustment is an affordance, not evidence of learner expression [30]. The candidate study therefore contributes only indirect cognitive-load and satisfaction evidence; eligible K-12 studies must observe decisions, revisions, reflection, and independent transfer.

## Part 2c — Thematic Analysis: Impact on Student Creativity

**Creative opportunity exceeds demonstrated creative development:** WRTN’s free-writing result, language-resource gains in the Jeddah ChatGPT study, and evidence of students’ revision judgments support useful conditions for creative expression. They show ways AI assistance can help learners write and revise. The four creativity dimensions, however, require attention to what students themselves generate, combine, explore, and risk—not simply whether a supported text receives a higher writing score. ### AI-enhanced ideation and brainstorming

**Free-writing gains support an entry point:** Jeong and Ahn’s positive free-writing result is relevant because open-ended writing places substantial demands on learners to begin, sustain, and develop a text. The finding supports the value of the WRTN-supported instructional arrangement for this activity. It gives a firmer basis for discussing access to writing than for assigning a numerical improvement in the originality of students’ ideas. [2]

**Idea support has two routes:** AI can support ideation by offering candidate content, or it can reduce language-related obstacles that prevent students from expressing their own content. These routes can produce a superficially similar result: a more developed English text. They have different implications for creativity, however. In one, the system contributes possible ideas; in the other, it helps the learner make an existing idea expressible.

**Language difficulty can conceal ideas:** Jeong and Ahn’s lower-level students struggled with vocabulary selection and benefited from teacher guidance and modified commands. This finding cautions against interpreting a limited English draft as a direct reflection of limited imagination. A student’s idea may be more elaborate than the language they can initially produce. Language assistance can therefore scaffold ideation-related activity by allowing the learner to develop a meaning that would otherwise remain difficult to express. [2]

**Generated alternatives still require judgment:** A plausible brainstorming mechanism is that AI provides alternatives that stimulate comparison and elaboration. Yet producing alternatives is not the same as developing a student’s capacity to generate and evaluate them. The educational value depends on what happens next: whether the learner selects deliberately, changes the suggestion, adds personal knowledge, or develops a different possibility. The agency evidence about selective feedback use provides a relevant instructional principle for this stage.

**Prompting can compete with brainstorming:** Woo et al.’s workshop demonstrates that students may spend considerable effort learning how to obtain useful assistance. High cognitive load, especially during prompt engineering, matters because brainstorming also requires attention to emerging meanings. The result does not establish an originality cost, but it supports the judgment that complex prompting should not consume the writing opportunity it is intended to support. [4]

**A bounded prompt preserves a task:** For example, a hypothetical activity could ask AI for three possible obstacles facing a fictional character while explicitly withholding a finished story. The student would then choose, alter, or reject those possibilities and develop the narrative. The numbers in this example are task instructions, not research findings. The point is to leave meaningful generative work with the learner while using AI to make starting less difficult.

**Brainstorming quality has several meanings:** More ideas, more varied ideas, more unusual ideas, and more useful ideas are different achievements. A student might produce many suggestions that repeat one pattern, or one idea that develops an original perspective. The reviewed free-writing outcome supports engagement in idea development, while the interpretation of creative growth depends on distinguishing these achievements and identifying the student’s contribution.

**Judgment on ideation:** AI can scaffold the conditions for brainstorming by supplying starting points and helping students express emerging ideas, with WRTN providing the clearest school-level writing outcome related to this process.

### Novel language combinations

**Lexical-resource growth is a useful partial answer:** Alzahrani and Alotaibi reported an increase from 3.0 to 4.4 in lexical resource over eight weeks—a calculated gain of 1.4 rubric points. This is evidence of improvement in the study’s vocabulary-related writing criterion. It supports the conclusion that ChatGPT-assisted instruction can accompany a broader or more effective linguistic repertoire in that setting. [5]

**Lexical improvement differs from linguistic novelty:** A higher lexical-resource score can reflect more appropriate word choice or a wider range of vocabulary. A novel combination concerns how words or structures are brought together to create a fresh effect or meaning. A student can improve the appropriateness of conventional phrasing without creating an unusual expression; equally, a creative expression may use ordinary words in an unexpected relationship.

**Language tools enlarge the available repertoire:** Grammarly and ProWritingAid support revision through feedback on form, clarity, or style, while ChatGPT can be used for language assistance within broader writing tasks. The plausible creative mechanism is that learners gain access to more ways of expressing an intention. The crucial developmental step is then selection and recombination: students must decide which language communicates their meaning, rather than assume that the most elaborate suggestion is the best one. [11][7]

**Meaning-sensitive choice provides a bridge:** The ProWritingAid learners’ acceptance and rejection of suggestions according to intended meaning shows a form of judgment necessary for creative language use. It is a bridge, not an equivalent outcome. The same ability to evaluate whether wording fits a purpose could support experimentation with an image or phrase, but the observed result is about revision decisions rather than measured linguistic originality. [7]

**Accuracy can make experimentation manageable:** In the Korean Grammarly case, selective uptake accompanied improved writing accuracy. One instructional interpretation is that timely feedback may help students test an unfamiliar expression and then examine whether it works. This suggests a potential connection between language support and experimentation. The supported empirical claim remains the observed combination of active feedback processing and accuracy improvement. [12]

**Correction can also narrow expression:** The opposite mechanism is possible when every deviation from conventional language is treated as an error to remove. Hypothetically, a deliberate fragment in a poem might serve rhythm or emphasis, even though a general-purpose language revision would favor a complete sentence. Creative scaffolding therefore requires distinguishing an unintended language problem from an intentional stylistic choice. This is a pedagogical judgment about tool use, not a reported negative effect in the school studies.

**Complexity is not a creativity ranking:** The Jeddah findings reinforce the need to evaluate language dimensions separately: lexical resource improved while grammatical range and accuracy declined slightly. The pattern does not identify whether students took more creative linguistic risks. It does show why a single label such as “better language” is insufficient. Teachers should consider whether wording is meaningful, appropriate to the text, understandable to the student, and deliberately chosen. [5]

**Judgment on novel combinations:** AI-supported vocabulary and revision can expand the linguistic resources available for creative expression, while the strongest reported school outcomes concern language quality rather than the originality of combinations.

### Genre exploration and development

**The evidence supports work within forms:** The reviewed school studies involve essays, compositions, free-writing, and recount/narrative writing. They demonstrate that AI assistance can be integrated into different writing activities. The more specific developmental question is whether students become better able to choose, adapt, or move between genres—not merely complete a task that happens to have a genre label.

| Writing context | Study | AI’s reported role | Empirical contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free-writing | Jeong and Ahn | WRTN-supported instruction with differentiated assistance | Positive writing-achievement result |
| Individual essays | Krange et al. | Automated feedback interpreted with teachers and peers | Evidence about feedback use and revision |
| Recount/narrative writing | Asyifa | Grammarly feedback on language, style, clarity | Reported writing-performance benefit |
| 500-word composition | Woo et al. | ChatGPT support after prompting instruction | Evidence about motivation, load, satisfaction |

These activities and outcomes are reported in the WRTN study [2], EAT study [3], Grammarly experiment [11], and ChatGPT workshop [4].

**Genre learning concerns purposeful choices:** Genres organize expectations about purpose, audience, structure, and language. Scaffolding genre development therefore involves helping students understand why a text takes a particular form and what alternatives are possible. An AI-generated model can potentially make a form more approachable, but its educational role depends on whether the student examines those choices or simply reproduces the model.

**Essay feedback illustrates supported development:** Krange et al.’s students worked on individual essays and interpreted automatic feedback with teachers and peers. This demonstrates support for developing a text through revision within an assigned writing context. It is particularly relevant to learning how to assess and improve a draft. The finding does not require assuming that the same instruction also broadened students’ repertoire of genres. [3]

**Open-ended success has a different scope:** WRTN’s free-writing benefit indicates that generative assistance can be useful where students have comparatively open opportunities for expression. That is important for creative writing instruction, but it should not be treated as evidence of movement among poetry, narrative, explanation, and argument. The distinction matters practically: reducing difficulty within one activity and developing flexible control across forms are different instructional accomplishments. [2]

**Task labels do not establish transfer:** Asyifa’s title identifies recount writing, while the abstract describes narrative writing. The positive result is best interpreted as improvement in the reported recount/narrative task, not as evidence of two separately tested genres. Similarly, Woo et al.’s 500-word composition should be discussed as the stated composition task rather than relabeled as a particular creative genre. [11][4]

**Exploration requires permissible departures:** A genre scaffold becomes creatively useful when it makes conventions available for choice rather than treating them as a single compulsory template. A proposed classroom application could compare two possible openings or organizations and ask the student which better serves their purpose. The student’s explanation would connect genre knowledge with agency. This is an instructional implication of the process evidence, not a reported genre-transfer result.

**Development is more than variety:** Asking students to produce several text types may increase activity variety without deepening control over any one form. Conversely, sustained work within one form can involve substantial creative development through changes in perspective, tone, organization, and audience. The literature therefore supports evaluating what students learn to do with a form, rather than counting genre labels as a proxy for creativity.

**Judgment on genre development:** AI scaffolding is supported as assistance for writing and revising within particular forms; creative genre development depends on students understanding and deliberately adapting those forms.

### Support for creative risk-taking

**Affective support offers the strongest connection:** Jeong and Ahn reported favorable changes in writing-related interest, motivation, confidence, and anxiety, varying across writing levels and affective factors. These findings matter because a student who feels better able to write may be more willing to attempt a demanding task. The supported connection is an enabling condition for experimentation, rather than a direct equivalence between confidence and creative risk. [2]

**Creative risk involves uncertain choices:** A learner takes a creative risk by attempting an unfamiliar perspective, language pattern, structure, or idea without knowing whether it will succeed. This differs from simply making an error. Risk-taking involves a deliberate departure or experiment, while an error may be unintended. The distinction is especially important in EFL classrooms, where unfamiliar English can make any writing feel difficult without necessarily making it creatively adventurous.

**Rapid feedback can lower revision costs:** A plausible supportive mechanism is that students can try an expression and receive feedback quickly enough to reconsider it during the same composing process. The language-feedback studies establish the availability and use of such assistance, including selective revision and accuracy improvement. Their practical implication is that experimentation can be accompanied by manageable opportunities to review and improve, rather than requiring students to commit immediately to a final version. [11][12]

**Rejection demonstrates judgment, not necessarily risk:** ProWritingAid learners’ rejection of unsuitable corrections shows that students can contest automated authority. This is relevant to protecting the possibility of creative departures. Nevertheless, rejecting a suggestion may simply preserve a conventional sentence that already expresses the intended meaning. It should therefore be read as evidence of revision judgment, not automatically counted as a creative-risk event. [7]

**High load creates a competing pressure:** Woo et al.’s high cognitive-load result introduces a different possibility: managing prompts and responses may make the task feel demanding even when the workshop is enjoyable. A plausible consequence is that some learners may favor the most immediately usable option rather than pursue a difficult alternative. The study supports attention to that pressure through its load findings; it does not establish that students actually became more conservative writers. [4]

**Classroom response determines acceptable experimentation:** As an instructional implication, students need to know that a deliberate attempt can be worth discussing even when it requires revision. If AI feedback is treated as a final verdict, an unconventional expression may be abandoned before its purpose is examined. If feedback is treated as information, the teacher and student can distinguish a communicative problem from an intentional effect. This applies the meaning-sensitive principle documented in ProWritingAid use.

**Support should preserve the challenge:** Removing every uncertainty would also remove much of the student’s creative work. A useful scaffold makes a difficult attempt possible while leaving the learner responsible for developing it. Jeong and Ahn’s differentiated responses illustrate the relevant instructional direction: address the obstacle—time management, feedback complexity, or vocabulary selection—without assuming that every learner needs the same kind of assistance. [2]

**Judgment on creative risk-taking:** AI can provide supportive conditions for experimentation through manageable feedback and improved writing-related affect, provided that students retain permission to question suggestions and pursue uncertain choices.

**Conditions for stronger developmental claims:** Conclusions about growth in voice, idea originality, novel combinations, genre breadth, and creative risk depend on evidence of student-authored change: what the learner intended, what assistance contributed, which alternatives were considered, and how the final decision developed. Baseline or comparison evidence distinguishes improvement from participation; later or unaided performance distinguishes a stronger supported product from an enduring change in the writer. These conditions clarify the interpretation of the present findings without treating promising activities as established developmental effects.

**Interpretation across the evidence:** Population terms are retained, with contextual L2 evidence distinguished from explicit EFL findings; publication eligibility follows first journal appearance. Two school process studies use linked descriptive citations. Related Poetry Machine research by Kangasharju et al. (2022) and the NLG idea-generation article by Woo et al. (2023) serve only as bibliographic context, not school-EFL outcome evidence. [13][14] Sample descriptions are not imputed counts; arithmetic differences use study-specific score units, not standardized effects. Alzahrani and Alotaibi’s criterion means sum to 12.2→17.5, while their overall scores are separately reported as 12.3→17.4. Ofrita et al.’s grade labels and t-test reporting are inconsistent: t = 2.192, thresholds 1.666/1.674, and reported p = .005. Asyifa’s recount/narrative labels describe the same reported intervention. Small cases, short interventions, and heterogeneous measures warrant study-specific judgments rather than a pooled effect.

**Closing judgment for writing instruction:** The most defensible use of AI is to scaffold students’ capacity to decide, regulate, express, and revise. The evidence supports language-feedback and strategy-based applications more strongly than broad claims of creative development. For agency and creativity together, the decisive instructional question is whether AI assistance leaves students with more understandable, meaningful choices about their writing—and supports them in making those choices themselves.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Analytical frame and evidence boundary**

**Supported result.** In the verified K–12 EFL record available here, ChatGPT functioned as a mediated writing partner rather than as an independently demonstrated cause of greater agency or creativity. Woo et al.’s 2024 study documented a classroom writing workshop, prompt-engineering instruction, student evaluation of chatbot output, high satisfaction, and heavy cognitive demands; it did not directly measure student agency or creativity. The defensible contribution is therefore evidence about scaffold design and learner experience, not a causal claim that ChatGPT improved either construct. [15][23]

| Construct | Operational boundary used in this review |
|---|---|
| **AI-integrated writing tool** | An AI system deliberately embedded in writing to generate, evaluate, adapt, prompt, organize, or provide language support; merely mentioning AI does not qualify. |
| **Writing scaffolding** | Contingent or mediated support that helps learners compose, regulate, revise, or reflect beyond unaided performance; tool availability alone is insufficient. |
| **Student agency** | Learner choice, ownership, monitoring, process control, authorship, voice, or identity expression, retaining each study’s own measure. |
| **Student creativity** | Novel and appropriate ideas, language, forms, elaboration, or experimentation; fluent or AI-generated text alone is not evidence of creativity. |
| **Evidence directness** | Direct measures assess the named construct; proxies, perceptions, and author interpretations are indirect. An unreported measure is not a null effect. |

**Study-to-purpose relation**

The principal candidate is **Woo, Wang, Guo, and Susanto (2024), “Teaching EFL students to write with ChatGPT: Students’ motivation to learn, cognitive load, and satisfaction with the learning process,”** published in *Education and Information Technologies*, DOI 10.1007/s10639-024-12819-4. The publisher records a formal publication date of **14 June 2024** and an issue date of **December 2024**; the formal publisher date places the study within the requested 2018–30 November 2024 window. [15] A secondary record lists 1 July 2024, while an accessible preprint is dated December 2023; the publisher’s formal date is the appropriate eligibility basis. [19][23] The supplied records establish a published journal article reporting original mixed-methods data, but they do not display an explicit peer-review statement; peer-review verification should therefore remain visible as a screening status rather than being silently assumed. [15][23]

The study directly matches the requested population and activity more closely than the preliminary metadata suggested. It involved **21 Hong Kong secondary-school students** in an English-as-a-foreign-language classroom, all female, with a mean age of **13 years**. Grades 1–4 were represented: grade 1, **2 students (9%)**; grade 2, **16 (76%)**; grade 3, **2 (9%)**; and grade 4, **1 (4%)**. Seven students lived in public housing, and 11 were described as mediocre writers whose most recent EFL writing-examination marks fell between 40 and 60 out of 100. The students attended the same all-girls secondary school; the sample was voluntary and convenience-based, and the first author was their EFL teacher. [23] These details support a separable K–12 EFL sample while also limiting generalizability.

The intervention was a **1-hour-45-minute workshop**, including a **45-minute “Completing a Writing Task with ChatGPT” activity**. The abstract describes a **500-word English-language writing task**; the design description also presents a feature-article task and a letter-to-the-editor task, but does not clarify whether the 500-word description applied to both formats. Students used school-supplied iPads, the POE chatbot application, and Google Docs. They were introduced to ChatGPT, chatbot interaction, prompting, and prompt engineering before composing. [15][23] The study collected a pre-workshop motivation questionnaire, think-aloud protocols during writing, and a post-workshop questionnaire addressing motivation, cognitive load, and satisfaction. [15]

For the requested study-summary fields, e001 therefore supplies the following: the study identity is Woo et al. (2024); the implemented technology is ChatGPT; the sample is 21 female Hong Kong secondary students; the scaffold is a workshop combining prompt-engineering instruction with chatbot-supported composition; the writing activity is an English composition task; and the reported outcomes are motivation, cognitive load, satisfaction, and learner experience rather than a direct writing-quality, agency, or creativity score. [15][23] The study can consequently inform both required report parts, but any table or synthesis cell concerning direct agency or creativity should state **not measured or not reported**, not infer a positive or negative effect.

**What the intervention documents**

The design describes a machine-in-the-loop writing cycle: a student supplies a prompt or instruction, ChatGPT generates an output, and the student evaluates, accepts, rejects, or modifies that output before integrating material into the composition. The design explicitly places control of the final composition with the student. Students were also asked to distinguish their own words from chatbot-generated words by highlighting them in different colors. [23] This is strong process-design evidence for a control-preserving scaffold, but it is not by itself a measured increase in agency.

Functionally, e001 overlaps more than one scaffold category:

- **Cognitive support:** Prompt engineering, question formulation, text generation, rewriting, summarizing, and iterative evaluation of output support planning and problem-solving around composition. The same process also produced the study’s principal limitation: students reported high cognitive load, especially during prompt engineering. [15][23]
- **Language enhancement:** The workshop introduced use cases involving paraphrasing, translation, text completion, explanation, and revision, and students combined their own language with chatbot output. However, the reported results do not provide a grammar, vocabulary, fluency, cohesion, or language-accuracy outcome. [23]
- **Creative support:** Brainstorming and fresh-idea generation are discussed as possible affordances, but the supplied e001 results do not show that students generated more original ideas or overcame writer’s block. Those claims are attributed in the paper to earlier creative-writing research, not to a measured outcome of this workshop. [23]

Secondary review pages describe ChatGPT more generally as an interlocutor, content provider, teaching assistant, evaluator, chatbot, or virtual tutor, and list grammar correction, vocabulary building, planning, drafting, feedback, and automated essay evaluation as possible functions. They also associate continuous feedback with writing improvement and caution that high use of AI-generated words does not guarantee high performance. These are discovery-level or review-level statements, not e001-specific results, and they should not fill the candidate’s empirical outcome fields. [92][30]

The following map keeps e001’s reported process evidence separate from direct construct measurement.

The available primary report instead supports a narrower conclusion: students were given opportunities to exercise control within an AI-mediated process, while the requested agency and creativity outcomes remained empirically unresolved.

**Comparability, limitations, and evidence gaps**

The e001 results should not be expressed as an AI effect. The study used a small convenience sample from one all-girls school, a single short workshop, and no reported untreated comparison group. Its pre/post comparison concerned motivation, not writing quality, agency, or creativity. The result was **no significant difference in motivation before and after the workshop**, although mean motivation increased slightly; students reported **high cognitive load during writing**, particularly during prompt engineering, and **high overall satisfaction** with the workshop. [15] These findings support a mixed experience—engagement and satisfaction alongside substantial prompt-related burden—not a general improvement claim.

Several conditions could explain the observed responses besides ChatGPT itself: the first author’s teacher mediation, explicit instruction in prompt engineering, access to school iPads and the POE application, students’ baseline EFL writing proficiency, voluntary participation, familiarity with digital tools, the novelty of generative AI, the short duration, and the particular genres and prompts. The paper itself recommends iterative activity and instructional-material design and careful instructional scaffolding, especially for prompt engineering. [15][23] Because these conditions were not experimentally separated from the AI tool, causal language should remain restricted to what the mixed-methods design supports.

Cross-study comparisons should therefore preserve each study’s original sample denominator, grade or age, EFL setting, writing task, intervention dose, comparison condition, instrument, and assessment time. No pooled sample or common effect estimate is justified from the available records. A discovery-only meta-review in the packet illustrates why: it reports 69 included ChatGPT studies, 62 of them meta-analysed, with 58 studies classified as university-level and 10 as K–12; sample sizes ranged from 18 to 600 participants, with an average of 106, and only 5 of 62 studies (8.06%) reported power analysis. It also reports intervention durations ranging from a ten-minute session to a 16-week semester and inconsistent reporting of whether ChatGPT was available during post-tests. These values are not the present review’s corpus count, pooled sample, or effect estimates, because the source is secondary and its population is broader than K–12 EFL writing. [27]

The same discovery-only literature flags over-reliance, inconsistent feedback, erroneous feedback or misinformation, lack of contextual understanding, and ethical concerns as recurring risks; it also describes idea expansion, story generation, and text structuring as possible affordances. One cited study reported harm to creative writing and another no discernible difference between generative-AI and human-supported feedback, but neither result is attributable to e001. [30] These observations are useful boundary hypotheses, not replacements for eligible primary evidence.
