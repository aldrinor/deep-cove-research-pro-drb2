# IoT in Healthcare, Agriculture, and Smart Homes Through the First Half of 2023

**IoT was already in practical use:** by the first half of 2023, connected devices supported healthcare monitoring and remote care, precision farming and irrigation, and mass-market home automation. Adoption was uneven, with security, privacy, interoperability, connectivity, power consumption, and lifetime costs limiting deployment. Connected devices had indeed surpassed the world population: IoT Analytics estimated **14.3 billion active IoT endpoints in 2022**, compared with the UN’s 2022 population figure of **7.942 billion people**—approximately 1.80 endpoints per person. [1][2]

**The smart-home market answer:** a directly relevant forecast put the global market at **US$107.4 billion in 2023**, with a **9.5% compound annual growth rate, or CAGR, for 2018–2023**. These are forecast figures published in 2018, rather than measured full-year 2023 sales. [3]

**How the report proceeds:** the healthcare section explains the main medical applications, their deployment challenges, and the wireless technologies connecting them. Agriculture then shows how sensing becomes practical farm management and why adoption varies. Smart homes covers household applications and challenges, followed by the two-decade device–population comparison and the interpretation of smart-home market forecasts.

## Healthcare Applications

**Monitoring led practical deployment:** healthcare IoT was most firmly established in wearable sensing, remote patient monitoring, telemedicine, and connected hospital systems. An April 2023 systematic review identifies these as the principal application classes, while highlighting security, scalability, latency, power consumption, and integration costs as continuing constraints. [4] Advanced applications such as robotic telesurgery were also demonstrated, but their evidence and operational requirements differed substantially from those of routine monitoring.

### Main applications and concrete examples

**The main uses were diverse:** medical IoT connected physiological measurements, care teams, treatment devices, and hospital operations. The following examples distinguish commercial products and services from research systems, because a functioning prototype demonstrates a different level of maturity from a sustained clinical service.

| Application | Product or application example | Connected function | Evidence position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wearable glucose monitoring | Guardian 3, Dexcom G6, FreeStyle Libre and Libre 2 | Glucose measurements to readers, phones, or compatible systems | Commercial diabetes products |
| Remote chronic-disease care | Philips Virtual Care Management | Connected measurements, cloud records, monitoring, coaching | U.S. service announced March 2023 |
| Respiratory telemonitoring | Airgo band, SAT-30 oximeter, uHoo sensor | Respiratory, oxygen-saturation, and environmental data | Published home-monitoring system |
| Treatment support | Compatible CGM–insulin-pump systems | Glucose data inform insulin suspension or adjustment | Product-dependent functionality |
| Intelligent hospital monitoring | Arduino/Blynk vital-sign systems | Patient measurements to cloud dashboards | Research prototypes |
| Identification and tracking | RFID patient tags and readers | Identity or location information to hospital systems | Established application category |
| Assisted living and fall detection | Haider and colleagues’ movement-monitoring system | Body-sensor signals classified into activities and falls | Research system |

The diabetes examples are described in Remote Patient Monitoring in Diabetes [5]; Philips details its service in the March 2023 announcement [6]. Respiratory monitoring and fall-detection systems appear in the January 2023 smart-healthcare review [7], while the health-monitoring review [8] and March 2023 telemedicine survey [9] describe cloud-connected prototypes and RFID applications.

**Diabetes provides a concrete example:** connected blood-glucose meters, continuous glucose monitoring systems, and insulin-delivery devices allow care to extend beyond occasional clinic measurements. The diabetes review names Medtronic Guardian 3, Dexcom G6, and FreeStyle Libre and Libre 2 among commonly used personal continuous glucose monitoring systems in the United States at the time of writing. [5] Continuous glucose monitoring, usually shortened to CGM, provides repeated measurements that reveal patterns and excursions that isolated readings may miss.

**Some systems also support treatment:** compatible CGM transmitters can send measurements to sensor-augmented insulin pumps or automated insulin-delivery systems. The receiving system can suspend basal insulin to help prevent hypoglycemia—blood glucose becoming too low—or adjust basal delivery in response to low and high glucose. [5] This is a stronger form of IoT integration than displaying a number: sensing becomes an input to a controlled physical action. Its usefulness depends on the particular approved device combination and treatment workflow.

**Wearables extend beyond glucose:** reviewed systems measured body temperature, heart rate, and an electrocardiogram, or ECG, which records the heart’s electrical activity. One wearable developed by Wu and colleagues combined ECG and optical pulse measurements to estimate blood pressure using pulse arrival time—the timing relationship between electrical cardiac activity and a detected pulse. [8] That example also illustrates an important distinction: some devices directly measure a signal, while software estimates another physiological variable from it.

**Remote monitoring connects clinical services:** Philips announced Virtual Care Management on 27 March 2023, combining connected devices and engagement tools with a cloud platform, licensed clinical monitoring, and personalized health coaching. Its condition-specific protocols covered diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and gestational programs for diabetes and hypertension. The announcement specified availability in the United States. [6]

**The service layer creates value:** in this type of arrangement, the device supplies measurements, software organizes trends and alerts, and a care professional interprets the information and contacts the patient when appropriate. Philips’ inclusion of coaching and implementation services is significant because uploading measurements alone does not establish who reviews them or how an intervention occurs. Its announcement presents potential clinical and operational benefits, but the product description itself should be read as evidence of an offered care model, rather than proof of identical results across all conditions and providers.

**Telemedicine becomes richer with sensors:** a consultation can incorporate remotely collected oxygen saturation, blood pressure, temperature, or respiratory measurements instead of relying only on conversation and video. Angelucci and colleagues’ home respiratory-monitoring system combined an Airgo band, a SAT-30 pulse oximeter, and a uHoo environmental sensor, with data collected through an iPad-based application. [7] The clinical significance lies in connecting symptoms to repeated physiological measurements and the patient’s environment.

**Timing depends on the clinical task:** the March 2023 telemedicine survey distinguishes synchronous interaction, store-and-forward assessment, and remote monitoring. Synchronous care involves live interaction; store-and-forward care sends records or images for later review; remote monitoring collects measurements outside conventional facilities. [9] These alternatives require different network capacities and response arrangements. A daily measurement submitted for routine review does not need the same communications performance as live guidance during an emergency.

**Hospitals connect patients and operations:** intelligent hospital systems include bedside or wearable monitoring, environmental sensing, identification, records access, and asset management. One reviewed prototype combined patient temperature and heart-rate measurements with room carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and humidity sensing; another used Arduino hardware and Wi-Fi to send measurements to Blynk cloud dashboards. [8] These are useful demonstrations of integrated sensing, although prototype functionality and hospital-wide operational effectiveness remain different claims.

**Identification supports the wider workflow:** the April 2023 healthcare review describes a diabetes-record workflow in which scanned identification cards connect users to cloud-held electronic health records, prescriptions, laboratory results, and medical histories. [4] RFID-based patient identification and tracking provide another example of connecting physical activity to information systems. [9] These applications show that medical IoT is not restricted to biosensors: identifying the correct patient, record, or physical resource is also part of dependable care.

**Assisted living emphasizes behavioral change:** connected sensors can support treatment-compliance monitoring, medication assistance, activity monitoring, and emergency notification. [10] Haider and colleagues’ research system, for example, classified body movements, including falls, using sensor signals and machine-learning methods. [7] Such systems aim to identify when a person may need help, but the response still depends on identifying the person correctly and connecting the alert to an available caregiver.

**Advanced interventions had mixed maturity:** the January 2023 review of 44 studies classified 25 as proposals, showing the importance of separating envisaged 5G applications from deployed services. Nevertheless, its examples included substantive demonstrations: the “Micro Hand” surgical robot was operated on a pig approximately 3,000 km from the surgeon, and the MGIUS-R3 robot-assisted teleultrasound system was used in an intensive-care study enrolling 32 patients, with one excluded because of poor image quality. [7] These cases establish technical and clinical experimentation, not universal readiness for routine remote surgery.

**The resulting maturity judgment:** by H1 2023, healthcare IoT was clearly beyond the concept stage for connected monitoring and remote-care workflows; advanced interventions required application-specific evidence of safety, reliable communications, and sustained clinical operation.

### Wireless technologies used by medical IoT devices

**Medical systems usually combine links:** Bluetooth/Bluetooth Low Energy, ZigBee, and Wi-Fi commonly serve the local connection between sensors, a patient’s phone, a gateway, or a facility network. Cellular systems, NB-IoT, and LoRaWAN address the wider connection to remote systems and caregivers. A January 2023 review describes the common two-hop architecture explicitly: short-range communication carries measurements to a gateway, and cellular or another access network forwards them to the medical data system. [7]

**Local and wide-area describe roles:** a local or personal-area link covers nearby devices, such as a body sensor and phone. A wide-area link connects geographically separated locations, usually through an operator network or gateway infrastructure. A device can therefore use Bluetooth locally while its data reaches a distant hospital through cellular service; describing that device as “Bluetooth-connected” does not describe the entire communications path.

| Technology | Main medical applications | Typical range or coverage | Area class | Main design trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bluetooth / BLE | Wearable vital signs, ECG, sensor-to-phone links | 1–100 m; commonly reported as 10–100 m | Local/personal | BLE saves energy; needs nearby receiver |
| ZigBee | Temperature, pulse, sensor-to-gateway monitoring | About 100 m; review range 10–200 m | Local | Low power and data rate; interference |
| Wi-Fi | Facility monitoring, gateway upload, medical multimedia | About 100 m; review range 100–300 m | Local | Higher capacity; greater power demand |
| GSM / 3G / 4G / LTE | Remote monitoring, mobile patient-data transmission | Operator coverage across service areas | Wide area | Mobility and reach; power and service costs |
| 5G | Remote care, medical video, robotics, mobile units | Operator- and deployment-dependent coverage | Wide area | High-capacity or low-delay services need suitable deployment |
| NB-IoT | Low-throughput medical-IoT uplinks | Up to 35 km in a smart-health review | Wide area | Small payloads; coverage and timing requirements |
| LoRaWAN | Long-range patient-data and telemonitoring links | Kilometre-scale; WAN benchmark 10–30 km | Wide area | Low data rate; gateway dependence |

The local ranges come from a 2021 wireless body-area-network survey [11] and a 2023 IoMT communications table [12]. The NB-IoT range is reported in the smart-healthcare networking review [10]. Wide-area medical roles, including LoRaWAN and cellular systems, are described in the March 2023 telemedicine survey [9]; the 10–30 km figure is a WAN-category benchmark, rather than a measured medical LoRaWAN deployment.

**Bluetooth fits the body-to-phone link:** a small wearable often needs to send modest amounts of data while preserving a small battery. BLE addresses that requirement, while a phone or gateway performs the more energy-demanding onward communication. The telemedicine survey describes an ECG system in which BLE carries measurements to a mobile application and Wi-Fi then transfers them to a provider database. [9] This division of work explains why short range can be an advantage rather than a defect.

**Bluetooth versions should be distinguished:** classic Bluetooth and BLE have different energy characteristics and operating modes. The 2021 body-area-network survey identifies power consumption as a drawback for Bluetooth in some settings, whereas the 2023 telemedicine survey describes BLE’s optimization for small-battery devices. [11][9] Thus, “uses Bluetooth” is insufficient information for estimating a wearable’s battery life or suitability for continuous operation.

**ZigBee suits small repeated measurements:** low-power temperature or pulse reporting can fit a low-data-rate sensor network. The body-area-network survey nevertheless warns that limited throughput and interference can complicate high-rate applications and multi-patient hospital deployment. [11] The distinction is between transmitting occasional numerical measurements and accommodating many simultaneous signal streams. A technology that handles the first adequately may need much more careful engineering for the second.

**Wi-Fi favors capacity over miniaturization:** it can carry larger data transfers, medical records, audio, and video over an existing local network. In body-area-network architectures, the 2021 survey places it primarily between a personal device and an access point rather than directly on the smallest body sensor, because of its power requirements. [11] Managed hospital access points can support mobility within a facility, but interference, congestion, authentication, and handovers still affect the service experienced by the patient.

**Cellular extends monitoring beyond premises:** GSM, 3G, 4G, and LTE allow patient information to travel from a mobile device or gateway to a remote caregiver. The March 2023 survey describes their use in remote physiological monitoring and medical multimedia transmission. [9] Their main advantage is geographic mobility rather than an inherently superior choice for every sensor. Coverage, energy consumption, subscription costs, and continuity while moving between access networks remain relevant.

**NB-IoT addresses a narrower role:** the smart-healthcare review classifies Narrowband Internet of Things as a wide-area medical-networking option and reports a range of up to 35 km. [10] Its strongest supported role here is a low-throughput uplink within a medical-IoT architecture. Whether it fits a particular clinical product is decided by operator coverage, payload size, reporting interval, mobility, battery budget, and the deadline for receiving the measurement—not by the technology name alone.

**LoRaWAN favors distance and small payloads:** the telemedicine survey includes it among technologies that can carry patient information to remote caregivers. [9] Its long-range, low-data-rate design makes it more naturally suited to intermittent measurements than high-definition medical video or tightly controlled robotic movement. A gateway also creates an additional dependency: the sensor-to-gateway radio link and the gateway’s onward connection must both work.

**5G contains different service ambitions:** its healthcare relevance comes from several distinct capabilities rather than a single promise of “faster wireless.” The Medical Device Innovation Consortium’s December 2022 analysis separates enhanced mobile broadband, ultra-reliable low-latency communication, and massive machine-type communication. [13]

| 5G service category | Main communications objective | Relevant medical application |
|---|---|---|
| Enhanced mobile broadband, eMBB | High data capacity | Medical video and immersive training |
| Ultra-reliable low-latency communication, URLLC | Timely, dependable communication | Remote medical-device control |
| Massive machine-type communication, mMTC | Many low-data-volume devices | Dense sensing and monitoring |

**Network capability is not end-to-end performance:** a remote clinical interaction includes sensing, processing, transmission, server handling, and display or actuation. Improving the radio link alone does not remove delays elsewhere. MDIC emphasizes that achieved 5G performance varies by equipment, network operator, geography, and spectrum. [13] The 3,000 km separation in a telesurgery demonstration is likewise an end-to-end distance across a network, not the coverage radius of one radio transmitter.

**The practical networking judgment:** low-power local sensing combined with an appropriate onward connection was the central medical-IoT architecture through H1 2023; the best technology depended on the clinical task’s data volume, response deadline, mobility, and failure consequences.

### Main deployment difficulties and their consequences

**Security is a patient-safety issue:** medical IoT needs confidentiality, integrity, authentication, and availability. In plain language, information must remain private, accurate, associated with the correct person or device, and accessible when required. The body-area-network survey emphasizes that false or inaccessible medical data can contribute to incorrect treatment. [11] Consequently, security failures can affect both information rights and physical care.

**More components create more responsibilities:** sensors, phones, gateways, cloud platforms, applications, and clinical systems may be supplied and maintained by different organizations. A secure sensor does not automatically make the complete service secure. The January 2023 5G healthcare review found that 21 of its 44 included papers did not mention or prioritize security mechanisms. [7] That is a finding about the reviewed literature, but it reinforces why technical demonstrations and deployment-ready systems should be distinguished.

**Interoperability remains a concrete barrier:** the diabetes review reports that certain insulin pumps interact only with particular CGM systems, while some meters communicate only with selected pumps. [5] Wireless compatibility is only one layer of the problem. Devices also need compatible data formats, patient identifiers, access permissions, and treatment interfaces. Without these, staff may face separate dashboards and manual transfers even though every device is technically connected.

**Reliability requirements vary by task:** quality of service, or QoS, describes whether communication meets the application’s requirements for matters such as delay, throughput, and dependable delivery. The March 2023 telemedicine survey identifies end-to-end QoS, coexistence, interference, and mobility as deployment challenges. [9] A brief interruption may delay routine review, while the same interruption during time-sensitive remote control could be much more consequential. The response deadline must therefore be defined clinically, not inferred from a network’s advertised speed.

**Battery constraints affect continuity:** small wearable devices have limited space for batteries, yet sensing, computation, and wireless transmission all consume energy. Increasing reporting frequency can improve timeliness while increasing power demand. The telemedicine survey identifies energy efficiency and device miniaturization as connected design challenges. [9] A device that requires frequent charging or maintenance can produce gaps in monitoring even when its radio technology performs well.

**Measurement quality precedes useful analysis:** algorithms cannot compensate reliably for every calibration error, missing observation, or incorrectly interpreted signal. This matters especially when a device estimates a physiological variable or classifies an activity rather than directly measuring the clinical condition of interest. The telemedicine survey identifies data integrity, outliers, model accuracy, and interpretability as issues for machine-learning applications. [9] The operational consequence is that a successful data transmission should not be mistaken for a clinically valid conclusion.

**Maintenance creates continuing expenditure:** the April 2023 healthcare review identifies battery replacement, software upgrades, and replacement of outdated devices as substantial ongoing expenses. Integration with existing systems and the need for specialized personnel add further costs, particularly for smaller or budget-constrained providers. [4] The relevant financial question is therefore the lifetime cost of a functioning service, not simply the purchase price of a sensor.

**Regulation follows clinical risk and responsibility:** the diabetes review describes different oversight for high-risk functions, such as insulin-dose calculation, and lower-risk tracking or lifestyle-support functions. [5] The April 2023 review also identifies evolving questions around data ownership and responsibility. [4] These distinctions affect who may act on a reading, how a device or algorithm is evaluated, and who is accountable when information is wrong or an alert is missed.

**Remote access can reproduce inequalities:** telemedicine can reduce travel and connect patients to distant expertise, but those benefits depend on usable devices, suitable infrastructure, and affordable services. The March 2023 survey links infrastructure costs and inadequate communications coverage to difficulties serving remote communities. [9] A design that assumes a reliable household network, a compatible smartphone, and confident device use can place additional burdens on the very patients it aims to reach.

**Healthcare’s central takeaway:** connected monitoring was already useful and commercially established, but successful deployment required a complete care arrangement—reliable measurements, suitable networking, interoperable records, clear clinical responsibility, and funded maintenance.

**Main healthcare IoT challenges**

Healthcare IoT’s deployment effects are mediated by interoperability, security/privacy, connectivity, cost and human factors: heterogeneous devices and weak standardisation create data silos, manual entry, errors and inefficiency; weak authentication or cumbersome security lowers trust and adoption; and bandwidth/latency, cost and digital-literacy constraints delay or limit use [97][101][127][73]. Networked or automated devices can consequently threaten clinical-process effectiveness, safety, reliability and liability through compromised records or cloud services and undetectable malfunction, although a reported failure may instead reflect human error, hacking, software/firmware defects or infrastructure failure, so IoT should not be treated as the sole cause [125][96][126].

Use IoT for physical devices or objects with sensors, software and connectivity that communicate and exchange data, while keeping that conceptual boundary separate from any particular count metric [36][42]. In Healthcare, remote monitoring, telemedicine/virtual care, wearables, connected imaging and infusion systems, smart hospitals, asset/cold-chain/medication management and 5G-enabled surgical or ambulance services are documented; SmartSock was incorporated into routine care in three acute-care wards, Vcare and Whzan were implemented in London care homes, and 5G-supported uses span implemented studies and future or pilot projects [58][72][78][98].

An evidence-status rubric can distinguish proposed/concept work, such as the UHF-RFID/NFC point-of-care platform; pilot or future work associated with 5G; deployed or operational systems, such as hospital RFID; widely adopted use, such as remote patient monitoring; and routine clinical integration, such as SmartSock [92][72][70][58][78]. UWB should remain a research-stage candidate: the evidence covers medical body-area networking, patient monitoring, capsule endoscopy and biomedical sensing, but does not establish it as a primary or common medical-IoT link [89].

At the healthcare cutoff, remote patient monitoring was described as widely adopted and connected devices were in hospitals and clinical care, whereas 5G applications such as telesurgery, mobile ultrasound, robotic surgery and connected ambulances had evidence ranging from study or pilot to emerging use [58][72]. The retained wireless evidence covers NB-IoT multi-device remote monitoring with wide-area coverage, Bluetooth/BLE wearable monitoring, Wi-Fi respiration monitoring, Zigbee remote or home monitoring, RFID asset and medication tracking, and NFC point-of-care or identification; coverage is not established for every latter technology in the supplied evidence [118][171][87][94][68][63][92].

4G/5G cellular is documented for telesurgery, remote monitoring, mobile ultrasound, biosensors, robotic surgery and connected ambulances, while a 5G healthcare proof of concept had connection and device limitations; UWB is supported only by research on body-area networking, patient monitoring, capsule endoscopy and biomedical sensing [72][83][89]. Healthcare barriers include medical-device integration, dependence on adequate bandwidth, latency and infrastructure, interoperability, cybersecurity/privacy and regulatory requirements, constrained sensors and energy, and staff training; these can delay adoption or compromise safety and reliability [122][121][127][116][96].

Healthcare IoT applications are connected sensing systems that collect, transmit and analyse patient or operational data: documented categories include remote monitoring, telemedicine or virtual care, wearables, continuous glucose monitoring, smart infusion pumps and connected imaging [36][58]. Maturity is heterogeneous rather than uniformly commercial or mature—remote patient monitoring is described as widely adopted, hospital RFID as fully operational, SmartSock as a routine-care augmentation, and 5G-related work as including future or pilot projects [58][70][78][72].

Table-ready medical-wireless evidence is: NB-IoT—multi-device remote monitoring—wide area [168][171]; Bluetooth/BLE—patient and vital-sign sensing—coverage not established in the supplied evidence [81][120]; Wi-Fi—hospital RFID/database and respiration monitoring—local area [70][94]; Zigbee—remote or home vital-sign monitoring—coverage not established [68][109]; RFID—identification, asset and drug tracking—coverage not established [92][63]; and NFC—point-of-care or near-patient sensing and identification—coverage not established [92][110]. The table should mark those evidence gaps rather than infer a universal radius; UWB is research-stage, and the supplied material does not establish LTE-M or LoRaWAN as medical-IoT rows [89].

Additional roster evidence supports Wi-Fi for hospital local networking and respiration monitoring, Zigbee for remote patient or home sensing, RFID for wireless identification and asset or medication tracking, NFC for point-of-care and patient identification, and 4G/5G for connected-ambulance and related remote-care uses [70][94][68][92][63][110][114]. UWB remains an uncertain research candidate rather than a primary or common link, while no medical-use evidence for LoRaWAN or LTE-M—and no complete annual device/population series for the global table—is supplied here [89].

An operational IoT frame is a network of physical devices with sensors, software and connectivity that collect or share data and may act; Healthcare examples include smart inhalers, glucose monitors, heart-rate sensors, remote monitoring and RFID tracking [36][37][60][98][70]. Status is mixed—remote monitoring is widely adopted, the UHF-RFID/NFC platform is proposed and 5G healthcare work includes pilot or future projects—while interoperability and cybersecurity can reduce utility, trust and safety [58][92][72][97][53].

Healthcare has enough documented evidence for a standalone status assessment: remote monitoring, virtual care or telemedicine, vital-sign wearables, smart hospitals, asset or cold-chain management, medication management, connected imaging or infusion and 5G surgical or ambulance uses are reported, with Vcare and Whzan care-home deployment, operational hospital RFID, routine-care SmartSock and 5G work involving pilot or future projects as concrete status anchors [58][57][87][70][98][78][72]. IoT is the networked sensor-equipped physical-object frame [36][37], and UWB should be labelled research-stage or uncertain rather than primary [89].

NB-IoT is documented for chronic-disease management and continuous or remote monitoring and is a wide-area cellular LPWAN; Bluetooth/BLE supports wearable physiological monitoring and glucose-monitor/insulin-pump links in local-area use [65][167][58][124][172]. Connected-device growth cannot be read as one exact global series: one metric counts active nodes, devices or gateways concentrating end sensors, whereas other sources report connections; reported benchmarks include 8.1 billion connected devices at the end of 2015 and 14.4 billion active connections in 2022 [140][139][143]. Because the definitions and counting units differ, the evidence supports broad growth context but not a complete aligned device-versus-population table or a per-person result [137][144].

## Agriculture Applications

**Agricultural IoT was operational but uneven:** by early 2023, connected sensing, precision management, and remote control had practical agricultural uses, while adoption differed sharply by technology, crop, farm size, and connectivity. USDA’s February 2023 report documented substantial use of selected digital-agriculture tools in U.S. row crops, and a March 2023 NIST briefing described precision farming, livestock monitoring, and supply-chain tracking as major IoT applications. [14][15]

### Main applications and concrete examples

**The objective is better-timed action:** agricultural IoT collects information about soil, crops, animals, machinery, and goods, then makes it available for management decisions or automated control. The important connection is between a measurement and an operation: when to irrigate, where to apply fertilizer, which animal needs inspection, or where a shipment is moving.

| Application | Product or application example | Information collected or exchanged | Practical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soil and crop monitoring | CropX agronomy platform | Soil, weather, satellite, crop, operational data | Field-specific recommendations |
| Irrigation management | Semios with WiseConn and Nelson integrations | Moisture, weather, flow, pressure, plant indicators | Remote pump and valve scheduling |
| Variable-rate inputs | Trimble GreenSeeker and prescription-based equipment | Crop sensing, maps, application records | Adjust fertilizer or other inputs |
| Machinery coordination | Guidance systems and agricultural telematics | Position, coverage maps, equipment data | Reduce overlaps and exchange field records |
| Livestock management | Sparked cattle-ear sensors | Animal health and movement information | Identify animals needing attention |
| Supply-chain tracking | NIST’s farm-to-table tracking example | Movement of agricultural goods | Inventory and delivery management |

The commercial examples are described by CropX [16] and Semios [17]. USDA explains GreenSeeker, guidance, and telematics in its precision-agriculture report [14]. Sparked appears in Cisco’s agricultural example [18], while NIST [15] describes livestock and supply-chain applications.

**Crop monitoring combines several perspectives:** field sensors can provide local measurements, weather stations describe environmental conditions, and satellite or drone imagery supplies broader spatial information. NIST describes sensors, cameras, and drones monitoring moisture, nutrients, and other environmental factors to support more targeted water, fertilizer, and pesticide use. [15] These sources are complementary: a detailed sensor reading at one point and an image covering an entire field answer different management questions.

**CropX illustrates integrated agronomic advice:** its platform combines soil data, weather conditions, crop performance, satellite imagery, and farm operations, using agronomic models to generate recommendations. Its product description includes irrigation, crop health, disease risk, and nutrient management, and reports support for more than 80 crop types and use in over 70 countries. [16] That breadth illustrates the commercial form of an integrated platform; the dated adoption evidence for the H1 2023 assessment comes principally from the agricultural studies discussed below.

**Irrigation connects sensing to physical control:** soil moisture alone is not the whole decision. Weather, evapotranspiration—the loss of water through evaporation and plant transpiration—soil properties, crop needs, and the performance of the irrigation system all matter. Semios describes a service combining those kinds of information with pressure and flow monitoring and remote pump and valve scheduling through WiseConn and Nelson integrations. [17] This closes the loop from observing field conditions to carrying out and checking an irrigation operation.

**Feedback makes irrigation more useful:** scheduling a pump remotely is convenient, but checking flow and pressure can help distinguish a command being issued from water actually being delivered. Plant-stress, salinity, and fruit-growth information can add context to a moisture reading. Semios includes these measurements within its water-management offering, together with installation and maintenance services. [17] The practical value is therefore a combination of agronomy, communications, control, and field service rather than remote switching alone.

**Variable-rate application responds to spatial differences:** a uniform input rate treats the field as if its needs were the same everywhere. Variable-rate technology instead changes seeding, fertilizer, lime, or pesticide application across locations. USDA describes both map-based prescriptions and on-the-go systems, including Trimble GreenSeeker, which uses sensor data and algorithms to inform application rates. [14] The mechanism can improve the match between inputs and local conditions, although the benefit depends on whether those differences are measured and interpreted correctly.

**Precision equipment is not automatically IoT:** a stand-alone guidance system or sensor-controlled implement can operate without exchanging information over the internet. IoT becomes especially clear when field measurements, prescriptions, or machine records move wirelessly between equipment, farm offices, and cloud services. USDA defines agricultural telematics as this wireless transfer through cellular systems or local networks such as Bluetooth. [14] This distinction is essential when interpreting adoption statistics for digital agriculture.

**Machinery coordination reduces duplicated work:** automated guidance can reduce skipped or overlapping passes, while automated section control switches parts of a sprayer or planter off where inputs have already been applied. USDA also describes coverage-map sharing between machines, allowing equipment to exchange information about completed operations. [14] The operational benefit comes from coordinating movement and application, rather than simply adding more data collection.

**Livestock sensing changes where attention goes:** NIST identifies monitoring animal nutrition, health, well-being, disease, and movement as agricultural IoT applications. [15] Cisco’s earlier Sparked example describes cattle-ear sensors used to monitor health and movement. [18] The information can help a farmer decide which animals to inspect, but a behavioral signal and a veterinary diagnosis are different outputs.

**Supply-chain tracking extends beyond the farm:** NIST describes tracking agricultural goods from farm to table to support inventory management, timely delivery, and waste reduction. [15] The mechanism is straightforward: knowing where goods are and how their movement compares with the expected sequence supports decisions about storage, dispatch, and delivery. Its value depends on consistent identification and information exchange between organizations, not merely on a tracking device at the first stage.

### What adoption evidence showed by early 2023

**Digital adoption had grown substantially:** USDA’s February 2023 report analyzed Agricultural Resource Management Survey data from 1996–2019. It found a marked expansion in automated guidance and continuing variation in mapping, variable-rate technologies, and aerial imagery. [14] These are strong indicators of agricultural digitalization, but they measure a broader set of tools than continuously connected IoT devices.

|---|---|---|---|

| Corn auto-steer/guidance | 2016 | About 58% | U.S. planted corn acres |
| Sorghum auto-steer/guidance | 2019 | 72.9% | U.S. planted sorghum acres |
| Cotton auto-steer/guidance | 2019 | 64.5% | U.S. planted cotton acres |

**Acreage and farm counts tell different stories:** GPS use on approximately 12% of farms but 40% of farm and ranch acreage indicates that users tended to operate more land. USDA explicitly connects the difference to farm-size distribution and higher technology adoption among larger operations. [14] Consequently, a technology covering a large share of agricultural land need not be used by a similarly large share of farmers.

**Connected workflows lagged some component tools:** a January 2023 Agriculture paper summarized earlier studies of Midwestern, particularly Indiana, row-crop producers. Yield monitoring was much more common than cloud analytics, telematics, or variable-rate irrigation. [19] The practices overlap, so the following percentages describe separate adoption questions rather than parts of one total.

**The contrast reveals integration’s importance:** collecting harvest information can be useful without sending it automatically to another system. Cloud analytics and telematics add requirements for connectivity, subscriptions, compatible formats, and confidence in sharing farm data. The regional pattern therefore supports a more specific conclusion than “farming is becoming connected”: adoption of individual digital components was stronger than adoption of some integrated data-and-control workflows.

**Benefits were plausible but context-dependent:** USDA describes pathways to lower input use, improved management, reduced operator fatigue, and better risk management. It also distinguishes correlations between technology use and crop performance from causal effects. [14] Larger, better-resourced farms may both adopt technology and have other advantages. Higher yields among adopters should therefore not automatically be attributed entirely to IoT.

**The adoption judgment is qualified:** the strongest early-2023 evidence shows operational precision agriculture with substantial uptake of selected tools, while fully integrated connectivity and automation remained more uneven across technologies and farming contexts.

### Main deployment difficulties and their consequences

**Rural connectivity is a binding constraint:** the March 2023 NIST briefing cited a USDA estimate that 60% of U.S. farmland lacked good internet connectivity. It also noted that point-to-point or satellite solutions can become expensive while still failing to reach the middle of a farm. [15] This is a land-coverage problem, not simply a question of whether the farmhouse has broadband.

**Farm traffic differs from household traffic:** USDA notes that agricultural telematics may upload large field-operation files while downloading relatively small prescription files. Residential internet services, by contrast, often emphasize download capacity. [14] A connection adequate for browsing at an office can therefore perform poorly when machinery needs to transfer detailed field records from a distant location.

**Latency depends on the operation:** the January 2023 agriculture study contrasts machinery navigation, which may require responses within milliseconds or seconds, with some monitoring tasks that can tolerate delays of days or weeks. It also links lower latency to higher power consumption. [19] The distinction does not mean every monitoring task can wait: irrigation faults or animal emergencies may need prompt attention. It means that the required response time must be specified for the task.

**Long range trades off against capacity:** low-power wide-area networking can fit soil sensors sending small, intermittent measurements over large distances. A 2023 networking review describes LoRa communication exceeding 10 km and reaching up to 20 km in suitable conditions, with low data rates. [20] That architecture is useful for sparse field measurements, but it is not an equivalent substitute for transmitting video or supporting tightly timed machinery control.

**Upfront costs combine with recurring fees:** NIST identifies hardware, software, installation, and connectivity subscriptions as barriers. [15] USDA adds training, repairs, replacements, activation charges, and annual service fees. In its 2018 soybean-farm data, average yield-monitor replacement costs were US$8,051, with a US$1,041 annual fee; guidance-system replacement averaged US$20,165, with a US$1,154 annual fee. [14] These figures illustrate why purchase price alone understates the adoption decision.

**Scale changes the financial calculation:** fixed equipment costs can be spread across more output or acreage on a larger farm. USDA’s 2016 corn data provide a concrete contrast: guidance adoption was 10% among farms with less than 200 cropland acres and 73% among those above 1,725 acres. [14] Larger operations may also have better access to finance and specialized managers. Lower adoption on smaller farms can therefore reflect economic constraints rather than a simple reluctance to innovate.

**Field conditions can undermine valid sensors:** NIST highlights extreme weather, wildlife, and configuration changes as sources of failure. Its example of a working soil-moisture sensor being displaced to a different depth is particularly instructive. [15] The electronics may continue reporting, yet the measurement no longer represents the same soil layer. An automated decision based on that reading can then be inappropriate even though communication remains intact.

**Power access adds another dependency:** remote devices need batteries, regular servicing, or an alternative energy supply. NIST identifies limited electricity access and the cost and expertise needed for alternative power systems as obstacles, particularly for small-scale farms. [15] Long radio range does not remove this problem: increasing the distance between devices and staff can also make physical maintenance more burdensome.

**Vendor silos obstruct integration:** NIST reports that agricultural systems often use different protocols, standards, and proprietary smartphone applications. [15] A farmer may therefore collect moisture, machinery, and weather information in separate systems without an easy way to combine them. The resulting difficulty is not a shortage of readings but an incomplete connection between information and management action.

**Data sharing involves commercial trust:** field records can reveal production practices, input use, operational performance, and other business-sensitive information. NIST notes farmers’ concerns about sharing data, while USDA discusses ownership, security, privacy, and the potential benefits of combining information across fields. [15][14] Restricting access may protect the farmer’s interests, while wider sharing can improve advice; adoption depends partly on whether the terms of that exchange are trusted.

**Skills and support determine practical usability:** installing sensors, maintaining communications, interpreting anomalies, and applying agronomic recommendations require different kinds of expertise. NIST identifies training needs and notes that poor broadband can also limit remote technical assistance. [15] USDA finds technical and consultant services more commonly used alongside digital technologies, showing how support services can be part of adoption rather than an optional extra. [14]

**Agriculture’s central takeaway:** IoT’s strongest practical case was targeted management where actionable information, reliable field connectivity, and farm-level returns justified the full operating cost; affordable coverage, cross-vendor integration, and accessible support would broaden that case.

## Smart Home Applications

**Consumer automation had reached substantial scale:** by H1 2023, smart homes offered connected lighting, heating and cooling, security, access control, appliances, entertainment, and voice interfaces. Amazon’s May 2023 announcement reported cumulative purchases of well over 500 million Alexa-enabled devices, providing concrete evidence of consumer-platform scale. [21] Privacy, compatibility, reliability, and user acceptance remained important challenges, while health-focused smart-home systems had a more research-oriented evidence base. [22]

### Main applications and concrete examples

**Connected homes serve several purposes:** some applications offer convenience, others manage energy or security, and some attempt to support independent living. These purposes can share sensors and platforms, but they do not necessarily have the same reliability requirements or evidence of benefit.

| Application | Product or application example | Connected function | Main purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lighting | Philips Hue | App, voice, and supported platform control | Convenience and coordinated lighting |
| Heating and cooling | Nest thermostats; remote HVAC control | Temperature settings and remote management | Comfort and energy management |
| Security and access | Ring or Nest doorbells; connected locks | Camera viewing, alerts, access commands | Awareness and access control |
| Appliances and plugs | Smart plugs controlled through Echo Pop | Remote switching and routines | Convenient appliance management |
| Voice and entertainment | Echo Pop, Echo Show 5, SmartThings | Voice commands, displays, device coordination | A common household interface |
| Home health and assisted living | Motion, chair, and bed-occupancy sensors | Activity and sleep-pattern monitoring | Support for care and independent living |

The named consumer functions are described in Philips Hue’s compatibility documentation [23], Amazon’s 2023 Echo announcement [21], and Google’s device documentation [24]. The broader application classes and home-health examples appear in the 2023 networking review [20] and smart-home health review [22].

**Lighting illustrates simple coordinated control:** Philips Hue supports voice interaction through platforms such as Apple Home and Siri and Google Assistant, and integration with supported smart-home systems. Its documentation also describes a Yale integration in which unlocking the door can trigger lighting. [23] The useful feature is not merely remote access to a lightbulb; it is the ability to connect an event, a user preference, and an action.

**Climate control adds a feedback loop:** a thermostat measures temperature, compares it with a desired setting, and controls heating or cooling. Connectivity adds remote adjustment, coordination, and information access. The 2023 home-networking review gives smartphone, tablet, or computer control of HVAC—heating, ventilation, and air conditioning—as a common smart-home application. [20] Energy savings arise when connected control reduces unnecessary operation, rather than from internet connectivity by itself.

**Security connects observation to response:** connected cameras and doorbells let residents inspect events remotely, while locks and access systems permit controlled entry. Amazon’s May 2023 Echo Show 5 announcement specifically describes viewing a Ring doorbell camera from the device’s screen. [21] The value depends on whether the right person receives and understands the information and can respond; a recorded event is not equivalent to preventing an incident.

**Smart plugs extend basic automation:** Echo Pop’s announced functions included controlling compatible smart lights and plugs by voice. [21] A plug can add remote switching to some otherwise ordinary equipment, while a purpose-built smart appliance may expose more detailed settings and status information. These are different levels of integration, even though both may appear in a smart-home application.

**Hubs simplify the household interface:** residents otherwise face separate controls for lights, cameras, thermostats, speakers, and locks. Platforms such as SmartThings, Google Home, and voice-enabled Echo devices aim to coordinate compatible products through a common interface. [20] The mechanism can be straightforward: a command or sensor event reaches a hub or platform, a configured rule evaluates it, and one or more devices receive an instruction.

**Matter addressed cross-brand friction:** Amazon’s May 2023 announcement described Matter support for compatible smart-home products across brands. [21] Google’s documentation explains the relationship between Matter devices, Wi-Fi, and Thread-connected equipment linked through suitable hubs. [24] Matter concerns common device interaction, while Wi-Fi and Thread provide underlying network connections; a common application standard and a radio connection solve different parts of compatibility.

**Health applications inferred everyday behavior:** Ojo and Sullivan’s 2023 scoping review describes wearable trackers, passive infrared motion sensors, and chair-occupancy sensors used to study activity, and smart thermostats and bed-occupancy sensors used to examine sleep patterns. [22] These systems attempt to turn patterns of movement and occupancy into useful care information. Their output is often an inference about behavior, rather than a direct measurement of a medical condition.

**Consumer scale did not establish clinical maturity:** the review included 49 papers published between January 2008 and August 2021; 29 papers, approximately 59%, focused on technological validation, while 20, approximately 41%, addressed health purposes. It concluded that real-world implementation of smart-home healthcare remained scarce. [22] Its findings concern research publications, not the percentage of homes using health monitoring. They nevertheless support treating consumer automation and clinically useful home monitoring as different maturity questions.

**The application judgment is therefore split:** convenience, security, and environmental control had strong commercial-product evidence by H1 2023, whereas health-related interpretation required more application-specific evidence about residents, settings, and outcomes.

### Main deployment difficulties and their consequences

**Privacy concerns arise from ordinary functions:** cameras reveal visitors and movements, voice assistants process spoken interaction, and occupancy sensors can reveal household routines. These data can be sensitive even when the device is not marketed as medical equipment. The January 2023 IEEE smart-home cybersecurity survey identifies insecure communication, leakage, and device vulnerabilities as routes to spying or interference with residents. [25] Privacy is consequently part of the product’s everyday operation, not only an issue after a breach.

**Weaknesses span the whole system:** the IEEE survey summarizes six recurring vulnerability classes from prior work: outdated protocols, weak encryption, limited processing and storage, insecure applications, poor authentication, and firmware failure. [25] Adding more endpoints increases the number of components that need suitable configuration and maintenance. Strong protection in a router or cloud service does not remove weaknesses in an inadequately maintained camera or application account.

**Consumer controls provide partial responses:** Amazon’s 2023 announcement describes microphone on/off buttons and controls for viewing and deleting voice recordings. [21] These are concrete mechanisms for user control, but their presence and effective use are separate matters. Privacy also depends on who has account access, what information is retained, and whether household members understand the system’s behavior.

**Compatibility remains conditional:** products can support the same radio technology yet expose different functions, permissions, and automation rules. Partner ecosystems and Matter support reduce some friction, but their descriptions consistently refer to compatible or supported products. [21][23] For residents, this can mean that purchasing one additional device requires checking more than whether it connects to Wi-Fi.

**Reliability depends on where control happens:** a locally executed rule and a cloud-dependent command have different failure paths. If an application needs an external service, an internet interruption can affect it even when the local radio network still works. The 2023 networking review identifies connectivity loss, coverage gaps, congestion, power constraints, and deteriorating QoS among smart-home operating problems. [20] The practical question is which functions continue operating when one component fails.

**Bandwidth and power requirements differ:** cameras and entertainment devices create different traffic demands from motion sensors or locks sending short messages. The trade-offs discussed for medical networking also apply in homes: high-capacity Wi-Fi can be useful for multimedia, while low-power local links can better suit small battery-powered sensors. [20] Expanding a home with many devices can therefore require attention to placement, gateways, traffic, and battery maintenance rather than simply buying a faster broadband plan.

**Home-health sensing faces identity ambiguity:** Ojo and Sullivan identify differentiating several participants in one space as the largest challenge reported across their reviewed studies. Interoperability and scaling came second, followed by security and privacy. [22] In a hypothetical multi-person household, a motion sensor may register activity without establishing which resident produced it. An algorithm could then attribute a visitor’s activity to the person being monitored, undermining the intended health interpretation.

**Sensor error can become behavioral error:** malfunction, poor calibration, inconsistent installation, or missing data can alter an inferred pattern. The home-health review lists these issues alongside variable accuracy, data streaming, integration, and bandwidth. [22] The consequence is particularly important when a system is used to infer inactivity, disrupted sleep, or a possible emergency: incorrect information can generate unnecessary alerts or conceal a genuine change.

**Acceptance matters alongside functionality:** smart-home research on older adults identifies cost, mistrust, limited knowledge, privacy concerns, technology anxiety, and negative social effects as adoption barriers. [26] A system can be technically capable while feeling intrusive or reducing a resident’s sense of control. The practical value of assisted living therefore depends partly on understandable operation, consent, and the ability to retain meaningful choice.

**Lifetime costs include continuing support:** residents may face additional hubs, installation, batteries, subscriptions, troubleshooting, and eventual device replacement. The networking review describes the trade-off between low-cost systems with fewer functions and more capable but more expensive alternatives. [20] A home’s usefulness over time depends on the continuing operation of its devices and services, not solely on the initial feature list.

**Smart-home deployment’s central takeaway:** rapid sales and expanding compatibility supported real consumer value, but dependable homes required privacy controls, maintainable devices, resilient operation, and systems that residents were willing and able to use.

The CAGR calculated from those rounded endpoints is approximately 9.8%, consistent with Cisco’s description of about 10% annual growth, compared with about 1.0% population growth. [28] The central finding is growth in connections per person, not merely growth caused by having more people.

**Machine-to-machine links were especially important:** Cisco projected M2M connections increasing from 6.1 billion in 2018 to 14.7 billion in 2023. That is approximately 2.41 times the baseline and a calculated CAGR of about 19.2%, close to its reported 19%. [28] Monitoring, metering, surveillance, healthcare, transportation, and tracking help explain why device growth can exceed human population growth: one person or organization can operate many connected endpoints.

[1][31] Relative to the stated 2022 base of 14.3 billion, those endpoints imply approximately 11.9% and 16.8% growth respectively. Applying exactly 16% instead gives 14.3 × 1.16 = 16.588 billion, or about 16.6 billion. The printed forecast versions therefore span 16.0–16.7 billion; that is a source-version range, not a statistical confidence interval.

[1] This explains why an earlier forecast and a later reported estimate should not be joined into an apparently exact observed growth series.

[32] Using the revised denominator gives approximately 1.98 endpoints per person for the 16.0 billion forecast and 2.06 for 16.7 billion. Brookings’ 2015 synthesis of more than 30 sources placed a crossover in the connected-device and population trend lines around 2014. [34] Gartner’s 2017 forecast showed 8.4 billion connected things against 7.5 billion people. [27] These are definition-specific results, not interchangeable estimates of one universally defined milestone.

**Smart homes contributed to the multiplication:** Cisco projected connected-home applications at 48% of its 14.7 billion M2M connections in 2023. That implies approximately 7.1 billion connected-home connections, calculated as 14.7 × 0.48. [28] This is a connection count covering home automation, security, video surveillance, connected appliances, and tracking—not 7.1 billion smart homes or households.

**More endpoints do not mean equal access:** a highly equipped home, hospital, or farm can contain many connected objects, while another has few or none. Amazon’s cumulative Alexa-device purchases likewise measure neither unique households nor the currently active installed base. [21] The device–population comparison establishes the scale of machine connectivity, not universal participation or a particular level of social benefit.

**The global comparison’s takeaway:** connected counts clearly exceeded population under several definitions by the period in question, while the defensible account of growth preserves separate device series, forecast versions, and population measurement dates.

### Global smart-home market size and five-year CAGR

**The primary answer is US$107.4 billion:** Research and Markets’ February 2018 release forecast the global smart-home market reaching that value in 2023, at a 9.5% CAGR for 2018–2023. It describes market sizing in shipment-value terms, with value and volume analysis segmented by application, product and service, technology, and region. [3] This is the most direct match to the requested five-year interval.

The first forecast is set out in the Research and Markets release [3]; the alternative appears in ASDReports’ 2017 release [35].

**CAGR describes compounded annual growth:** it is the constant yearly growth rate that connects a starting value to an ending value over a specified number of annual intervals. For 2018–2023, there are five such intervals.

\[
\mathrm{CAGR}=\left(\frac{V_{2023}}{V_{2018}}\right)^{1/5}-1
\]

**The implied 2018 baseline is an estimate:** using the forecast’s US$107.4 billion endpoint and 9.5% rate, US$107.4 billion ÷ 1.095⁵ gives approximately **US$68.2 billion**. This is a calculated baseline implied by the forecast pair, rather than a separately reported observation of the 2018 market. Over the five years, the compounded increase is approximately 57.4%, calculated as 1.095⁵ − 1, equivalent to an increase of about US$39.2 billion from the implied baseline.

**The interpretation is substantial expansion:** a 9.5% CAGR means more than adding 9.5% of the original market each year, because each year’s growth applies to a larger base. It does not mean that every product category, country, company, or household expanded at that rate. Nor does an increase in market value distinguish automatically between more units, different product mixes, service income, and changing prices.

**The alternative is a higher forecast:** the ASDReports release projected US$137.91 billion in 2023 at a stated 13.61% CAGR for 2017–2023. Its coverage included lighting, security and access control, HVAC, entertainment and other controls, home healthcare, smart kitchens, appliances, and software/services. [35] Its 2023 endpoint is US$30.51 billion, or approximately 28.4%, above the primary forecast, calculated from the two published totals.

**Different forecasts should remain distinct:** both reports include products and services, but their descriptions organize the market differently and use different forecast intervals. The 13.61% figure is therefore not a substitute for a directly stated 2018–2023 five-year rate. The difference between their totals should be interpreted as a contrast between commercial forecasting models and market boundaries, rather than attributed to one proven cause or averaged into a supposedly more accurate number.

**Demand drivers explain the forecast logic:** Research and Markets identified safety and security awareness, demand for simplicity and personalized experiences, and adoption of cloud-based technologies as growth drivers. It expected safety and security to remain the largest application segment, entertainment to grow fastest, and wireless to be the largest technology segment. [3] Those are the forecast’s qualitative expectations; they help explain the projected spending without establishing measured 2023 segment shares.

**Connections, shipments, and market value differ:** Cisco’s connected-home M2M figures count connections, while the Research and Markets figure estimates monetary market value. A household may add many inexpensive sensors but little spending, or purchase a smaller number of expensive systems and associated services. Similarly, an annual shipment count records devices entering the market during that period, whereas an installed-base count concerns equipment remaining in use. These measures answer related but different questions about growth.

**The forecast judgment is conditional:** the US$107.4 billion and 9.5% pair is the appropriate primary answer because it explicitly matches the requested global market and 2018–2023 interval. Actual achievement of that forecast depended on purchases, replacement cycles, prices, service uptake, interoperability, and consumer acceptance; the higher alternative reflects a different forecast specification, not a measured H1 2023 outcome.

**How to read the figures:** sector status ends on 30 June 2023; undated vendor pages illustrate functions rather than dated adoption. Device totals use separate publisher definitions and labelled forecast versions, while population dates and revisions are identified. Agricultural percentages are U.S. digital-agriculture indicators, and medical or home-health examples include research systems. Radio distances are review benchmarks, not service guarantees. Market amounts are USD commercial forecasts; implied populations, ratios, growth checks, and the 2018 market baseline are calculations from cited inputs.

**The report’s closing judgment:** IoT’s practical maturity is best judged by whether connected measurements reliably support a useful decision or action—safer treatment, better-timed irrigation, or dependable household control—rather than by device counts or market value alone.
