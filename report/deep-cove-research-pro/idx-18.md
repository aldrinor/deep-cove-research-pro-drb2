# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants: Parasitic Connections and Findings Through 2020

**Parasitic relationships create genuine genetic connections.** Plants can acquire genetic material from organisms other than their parents—a process called horizontal gene transfer, or HGT. Parasitic plants are particularly revealing because their haustoria, specialized feeding organs that penetrate host tissues, create sustained contact between different species: experiments demonstrate RNA movement, while genome comparisons identify established foreign genes. These are related but distinct findings; RNA crossing a feeding connection is not automatically an inherited genetic change. [1]

**The four contexts have different answers.** Fungus–plant HGT is supported in both directions, including proposed fungal contributions to grass disease resistance. *Agrobacterium* provides the clearest experimentally characterized DNA-delivery mechanism, and sweet potato naturally contains inherited bacterial genes. Viral sequences can also become permanent components of plant chromosomes, and viruses can package plant-derived genetic material; the strongest carrier experiments establish capture, packaging and replication across compatible hosts, rather than a completed case of inherited transfer of a named plant coding gene between species. [2][3][4]

**The report follows four questions.** The emphasis is on discoveries published through 2020. First comes the parasite–host interface and the contrasting evidence from *Cuscuta* and *Striga*; next, the direction and consequences of fungus–plant exchange; then, bacterial DNA delivery and natural transformants; finally, viral persistence and carrier activity. A specifically identified 2021 result is used only to qualify an earlier generalization about *Cuscuta* mitochondria.

| Context | Donor | Recipient | Interface or vector | Transferred material | Stable genomic outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parasitic plants | Host or parasite | Parasite or host | Haustoria; vascular associations; cell connections | Nuclear or organellar DNA; mobile RNA | Established foreign loci; RNA traffic is separate |
| Fungi and plants | Fungal or plant lineage | Plant or fungus | Symbiotic or pathogenic contact | Genes including *LpBGNL* and *CPLS* | Genomic residence supported; ancient transfers inferred |
| Bacteria and plants | *Agrobacterium* | Plant cells and lineages | Protein-mediated secretion system | Bordered plasmid DNA | Integration and inheritance demonstrated |
| Viruses and plants | Virus; sometimes captured plant DNA | Plant cells | Infection, viral particles and insect vectors | Viral genes; hybrid virus–plant DNA | Viral insertions inherited; carrier cargo remains an experimental intermediate |

## 1. Between Parasitic Plants

**Finding: exchange accompanies intimate contact.** Parasitic plants and their hosts exchange several kinds of molecules, and some parasitic lineages have acquired substantial inventories of foreign genes. The central distinction is between a molecule moving into another organism and a DNA sequence becoming part of that organism’s inherited genome. *Cuscuta* supplies especially strong examples of both molecular traffic and historical genomic acquisition; *Striga hermonthica* supplies a named nuclear HGT case whose physical transfer route is inferred from its biology and sequence features. [1][5]

### Haustoria create the exchange interface

- **Haustoria bring living tissues together.** In dodder, including *Cuscuta campestris*, a haustorium grows into the host and associates with its vascular system. Xylem carries water and dissolved substances; phloem distributes sugars and other organic compounds. By accessing these transport systems, the parasite establishes a persistent feeding relationship rather than merely contacting the host’s outer surface. [6]

- **Plasmodesmata provide cell-to-cell access.** Plasmodesmata are microscopic, membrane-lined channels through plant cell walls. In *Cuscuta*, they occur in the combined host–parasite walls formed around invading haustorial “searching hyphae”—elongated parasite cells, not fungal filaments. Such connections provide access to the symplast, the interconnected contents of living cells, and offer a plausible route for macromolecules to cross the species boundary. [1]

- **Phloem access is physiologically compelling.** Experiments summarized by LeBlanc, Kim and Westwood in 2012 showed that the fluorescent transport marker carboxyfluorescein crossed established *Cuscuta* attachments within two hours; a fluorescently tagged viral movement protein also traversed the interface. These observations support functional access to phloem-associated transport. The anatomical interpretation is more specific: association with host sieve elements is clear, while direct sieve-tube continuity was not established in the studies reviewed. [1]

- **Different parasites construct different junctions.** Direct sieve-element connections were imaged between the root parasite *Orobanche crenata* and its host *Vicia narbonensis*. In *Striga hermonthica*, haustoria penetrate cereal roots and connect with host vasculature; interspecific plasmodesmata were also reported between *Striga gesnerioides* and pea, *Pisum sativum*. These examples support exchange opportunities without making every parasite’s interface anatomically identical. [1][7]

**Contact increases opportunity, not certainty.** A feeding junction repeatedly exposes recipient cells to material from another species and can bypass some barriers that normally keep plant tissues separate. Nevertheless, inherited acquisition requires additional events: DNA must reach the appropriate cellular compartment, integrate, survive cell divisions and enter a lineage that produces offspring. Consequently, convincing transport anatomy and convincing genomic HGT evidence answer different parts of the mechanism. [8]

**Host compatibility also matters.** A successful attachment maintains close associations between living cells, whereas host defenses can restrict penetration or disrupt transport. Moreover, access to extracellular spaces—the apoplast—is not equivalent to access to connected cell interiors. This helps explain why broad vascular contact does not produce identical RNA mobility or HGT inventories in all parasitic plants. [1]

### Cuscuta demonstrates direct movement of host RNA

**Tomato transcripts enter the parasite.** The 2008 work on *Cuscuta pentagona* parasitizing tomato, *Solanum lycopersicum*, detected four named tomato messenger RNAs inside dodder. Messenger RNA, or mRNA, is the working transcript copied from a gene and potentially used to make protein. Reverse-transcription PCR and in situ detection connected host sequence identity with its location in parasite tissue. [9]

| Tomato transcript | Encoded protein or role | Observed recipient | Demonstrated endpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| *LePFPα* | Phosphofructokinase α subunit | *Cuscuta pentagona* | Host mRNA inside parasite |
| *LePFPβ* | Phosphofructokinase β subunit | *Cuscuta pentagona* | Detected up to 30 cm from attachment |
| *RbcS* | Small subunit of RuBisCO | *Cuscuta pentagona* | Host mRNA inside parasite |
| *LeGAI* | Gibberellic-acid-insensitive regulator | *Cuscuta pentagona* | Host mRNA inside parasite |

- **Movement extends beyond the junction.** Tomato *LePFP* RNA occurred in parasite parenchyma and phloem-associated regions, and *LePFPβ* remained detectable up to 30 cm along dodder from the attachment. That spatial separation makes simple inclusion of a little host tissue at the junction an inadequate explanation for the result. The measured endpoint is long-distance RNA traffic, not insertion of tomato *LePFPβ* into a dodder chromosome. [9]

- **Multiple hosts supply mobile transcripts.** Earlier work used pumpkin, *Cucurbita maxima*, and mobile transcripts such as *CmNACP* and *CmWRKYP*; other experiments included alfalfa, *Medicago sativa*. A tomato microarray screen produced 474 putatively mobile transcripts, while confirmation experiments summarized in 2012 brought the then-confirmed inventory entering *Cuscuta* to 27. The difference between these numbers reflects screening candidates versus individually supported transport observations. [1]

- **Transport need not be equally reciprocal.** The tomato–*C. pentagona* experiments detected host transcripts entering dodder, while assays for dodder phosphofructokinase RNA in parasitized tomato and alfalfa gave negative results under those conditions. This supports the observed direction for that experiment, not a rule that every molecule can move only toward the parasite. Feeding creates a strong host-to-parasite flow, but the behavior of a particular transcript also depends on its transport and retention properties. [9]

**RNA traffic has several possible consequences.** A mobile transcript might be translated, participate in regulation or be degraded; its presence alone does not distinguish these outcomes. Even translation would not, by itself, establish HGT in the evolutionary sense used here. For an RNA-mediated route to inherited acquisition, an additional copying step would be needed to produce DNA, followed by genomic integration and transmission. [1]

**The distinction has practical value.** Cross-species RNA movement can affect an interaction without changing either species’ inherited gene inventory. In experiments with tomato and *Phelipanche aegyptiaca*, host-produced RNA targeting the parasite’s *M6PR*, encoding mannose 6-phosphate reductase, reduced parasite gene expression. Similarly, lettuce-produced silencing signals affected a *GUS* reporter in *Triphysaria versicolor*. These are examples of information acting across a parasitic interface, not acquisition of the host’s silencing construct as a new parasite gene. [1]

### Cuscuta genomes preserve evidence of host-derived DNA

**Established foreign loci tell another story.** Genome and gene-structure studies identify sequences that reside in parasitic plants rather than merely passing through them. Several examples retain introns—the noncoding intervals removed when a gene’s RNA is processed—or preserve neighboring genes in corresponding order. Such features favor transfer of genomic DNA over acquisition through a fully processed mRNA.

| Recipient | Donor lineage or named comparator | Gene or sequence | Main genomic evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Cuscuta campestris* | *Daucus carota* comparator | *Cc021598*, *Cc021599* | Adjacent loci resemble neighboring carrot genes |
| *Cuscuta australis* | Brassicaceae; *Arabidopsis thaliana* comparator | *CaSSL* | Foreign affinity and retained introns |
| *Orobanche aegyptiaca* | Brassicaceae; *A. thaliana* comparator | *OaSSL* | Foreign affinity and retained introns |
| *Cuscuta pentagona* | Legume donor lineage | *albumin 1*, KNOTTIN-like | Phylogeny and intron-bearing sequence |

The carrot-linked loci and earlier *Cuscuta* examples are described in Vogel and colleagues’ 2018 genome analysis; the SSL comparison comes from Zhang and colleagues’ 2014 study. The named modern comparators identify sequence relationships, not necessarily the individual species responsible for an ancient transfer. [6][10]

- **Neighboring genes support DNA-fragment acquisition.** In *C. campestris*, *Cc021598* and *Cc021599* most closely resemble two neighboring genes in carrot, *Daucus carota*, with additional nearby gene remnants. Preserved neighborhood—called synteny—supports acquisition of a DNA segment rather than independent arrival of unrelated transcripts. A subsequent parasite duplicate, *Cc021601*, is 85% identical to *Cc021599* across the gene sequence and 93.9% identical across its coding sequence. [6]

- **SSL genes illustrate independent acquisitions.** *Cuscuta australis* carries *CaSSL*, while the root parasite *Orobanche aegyptiaca*, also called *Phelipanche aegyptiaca*, carries *OaSSL*. Both are strictosidine synthase-like genes with Brassicaceae affinities. Against *Arabidopsis thaliana AT2G41300*, *CaSSL* showed 84% nucleotide and 88% amino-acid identity; *OaSSL* showed 88% and 89%, respectively. Their retained introns support DNA-level acquisition in the two separate parasitic lineages. [10]

- **Foreign genes can remain transcriptionally active.** *CaSSL* was expressed across all nine examined developmental stages or organs of *C. australis*, and its expression increased after wounding. *OaSSL* expression increased approximately three- to fivefold during haustorial formation and vegetative growth in *O. aegyptiaca*. These observations connect the acquired genes to parasite development or stress responses, while the name “strictosidine synthase-like” describes sequence affinity rather than establishing their precise biochemical reaction. [10]

- **Legume genes show repeated acquisition.** An *albumin 1* acquisition was reported in *C. pentagona*, and comparative work identified two KNOTTIN-like *albumin 1* copies in *Cuscuta*. A separate acquisition occurred in the lineage containing *Phelipanche aegyptiaca* and related Orobanchaceae. KNOTTIN proteins have a characteristic disulfide-stabilized structure; preservation of introns and conserved cysteine residues supports retention of recognizable gene architecture after transfer. [6][11]

**Genome-scale inventories broadened the conclusion.** Vogel and colleagues reported 64 novel high-confidence *C. campestris* HGT candidates associated with at least 32 donor sequences in 2018. Yang and colleagues’ 2019 analysis identified **108 transcribed and probably functional HGT events** in *C. campestris* and related species, together with 42 additional host-derived regions containing transposons, pseudogenes or noncoding sequences. These inventories were generated with different analyses and overlapping biological material; they are separate study results, not numbers to add together. [6][12]

**Repeated retention suggests biological relevance.** Eighteen of the 2019 *Cuscuta* acquisitions involved the same gene families independently acquired by Orobanchaceae parasites, and most were strongly expressed in haustorial feeding structures. Between 16 and 20 transcribed events were inferred to predate diversification within *Cuscuta*. Independent acquisition, persistent retention and expression at the feeding interface together make adaptation a stronger explanation than expression alone, although they do not assign a demonstrated phenotype to every acquired gene. [12]

**Acquired DNA can produce regulatory RNA.** Many horizontally acquired *C. campestris* genes generate 24-nucleotide small RNAs, a class commonly associated with RNA-directed DNA methylation. One transferred leucine-rich-repeat protein kinase gene overlaps a microRNA implicated in regulating host gene expression. This creates a plausible connection between an old genomic acquisition and present-day regulation across the host interface, but the proposed contribution of HGT-derived small RNAs should remain distinct from the demonstrated genomic presence of their source loci. [12]

### Striga supplies a named nuclear acquisition with a different sequence signature

**The key locus is ShContig9483.** Yoshida and colleagues’ 2010 study identified a nuclear gene in *Striga hermonthica* that grouped with grass genes rather than the expected eudicot relatives. Its sequence relationship to sorghum, *Sorghum bicolor*, was particularly informative because sorghum is a natural host; related sequences also occur in rice, *Oryza sativa*. The appropriate donor assignment is therefore a grass lineage, with sorghum providing a host-linked phylogenetic comparison. [5][13]

- **The sequence is identifiable and genomic.** *ShContig9483* is a locus identifier rather than a biochemical gene name. The original report deposited its cDNA and genomic sequences as AB530325 and AB530324, respectively, and the *S. gesnerioides* homologue as AB542056. These records anchor the claim to particular sequences and support a historical acquisition into the parasite genome, rather than detection of an unspecified host transcript during feeding. [5]

- **Its poly-A remnant suggests RNA ancestry.** A run of adenines near the sequence’s 3′ end was interpreted as a degraded poly-A tail, the terminal feature of many processed mRNAs. Together with the reported absence of introns, this supports a possible processed-RNA intermediate. The inference differs from the intron-bearing *CaSSL*, *OaSSL* and many *C. campestris* acquisitions, which favor transfer as genomic DNA. [7]

- **Root haustoria supply the biological opportunity.** *S. hermonthica* penetrates roots of hosts including *Sorghum bicolor*, *Zea mays* and *Oryza sativa*. Its haustorium therefore places parasite cells beside host tissues and vasculature. In this case, however, the HGT argument joins anatomical opportunity to a genomic sequence signature; it is not an experiment following *ShContig9483* across the attachment as it happens. [14][7]

- **Other Striga acquisitions favor larger fragments.** Zhang’s 2013 dissertation reported two adjacent high-confidence HGT genes in *S. hermonthica*, supporting an acquired region longer than a single gene. That finding should not be merged automatically with *ShContig9483*: adjacency is evidence relevant to DNA-fragment acquisition, whereas the poly-A signature motivates an RNA-related history for the named 2010 locus. [11]

**The resulting judgment is specific.** *Striga* supports natural acquisition of a grass-related nuclear gene, while its cellular route remains an inference. The presence of a *ShContig9483* homologue in *S. gesnerioides* also suggests persistence through parasite diversification rather than an explanation confined to contemporary feeding on one cereal host. The 2013 review placed the acquisition before the evolution of that species’ dicot-host specialization. [7]

### Organellar transfers show that exchange is not uniformly one-way

**Mitochondria preserve extensive foreign ancestry.** A pre-2021 mitochondrial phylogenomic analysis of *Rafflesia cantleyi*, *Rafflesia tuan-mudae* and *Sapria himalayana* identified substantial fractions of sampled mitochondrial gene sequences with HGT support. These parasites associate with *Tetrastigma* vines, while inferred sequence donors also included other plant lineages. Candidate genes across the analysis included *atp1*, *atp4*, *cox1*, *rps1*, *rps7*, *rps13* and *sdh3*. [15]

| Parasite | HGT-supported sequences | Informative sampled sequences | Rounded fraction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| *Rafflesia cantleyi* | 5 | 21 | 24% |
| *Rafflesia tuan-mudae* | 5 | 19 | 26% |
| *Sapria himalayana* | 11 | 27 | 41% |

These values use the study’s conservative threshold of at least 70% maximum-likelihood bootstrap support. They describe sampled mitochondrial sequences, not the percentage of an entire plant genome derived from a host. [15]

- **Gene neighborhoods favor larger DNA transfers.** Seven assembled regions preserved gene-order relationships with *Vitis* sequences, and transferred *cox1* and *rpl2* copies retained introns. Together, these observations favor movement of larger DNA fragments over short, fully processed mRNAs. Homologous recombination—exchange between related DNA sequences—was proposed as a way foreign mitochondrial copies could replace or modify resident ones. [15]

- **Parasites can also be donors.** The *Cuscuta*–*Plantago* literature includes inferred parasite-to-host transfers involving mitochondrial *atp1*, *atp6* and *matR*. Thus, nuclear genes entering *Cuscuta* from hosts and mitochondrial genes leaving *Cuscuta* for hosts are both represented in the pre-2021 record. The correct conclusion is reciprocity across systems and genome compartments, not equal two-way movement of every gene within one pair of plants. [10]

- **Plastid ancestry requires separate interpretation.** Studies also described horizontal acquisition of the plastid gene *rps2* among *Orobanche* and *Phelipanche*. A sequence’s organellar origin and its eventual location in a recipient are separate questions: plastid-derived DNA can have a different history from DNA retained in an intact chloroplast genome. This is why “organelle gene transfer” should identify both ancestry and destination whenever the evidence resolves them. [1]

**Later clarification narrows the generalization.** A 2021 study of the complete mitochondrial assemblies of *Cuscuta australis* and *C. campestris* found little clear evidence of foreign acquisition into those mitochondrial genomes, despite the established nuclear examples. Their assemblies measured 265,696 and 275,898 base pairs, respectively; 26 of 34 mitochondrial genes examined across three *Cuscuta* species supported the expected vertical relationship with *Ipomoea nil*. This later result qualifies the idea that parasitism necessarily produces extensive HGT in every genome compartment. [16]

### What the transferred material means biologically

**Different materials imply different outcomes.** The parasitic interface transports more than inherited genes. Classifying the material prevents a striking movement result from being mistaken for a permanent genetic acquisition.

| Material | Named example | Immediate significance | Inherited outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genomic DNA | *CaSSL*, *OaSSL* | Established foreign gene architecture | Supports persistent acquisition |
| Mitochondrial DNA | *atp1*, *cox1*, *matR* | Foreign organellar sequence or recombination | Can persist in recipient mitochondria |
| Messenger RNA | Tomato *LePFPβ* in dodder | Long-distance transcript movement | Additional DNA-copying and integration steps required |
| Small regulatory RNA | *M6PR*-targeting RNA | Cross-species gene silencing | Regulation does not require gene acquisition |
| Protein | GFP entering *Cuscuta reflexa* | Physiological macromolecule movement | Protein itself is not inherited DNA |

The DNA, RNA and protein examples are supported by the genomic and transport studies discussed above; tobacco, *Nicotiana tabacum*, expressing GFP supplied the classic *C. reflexa* protein-movement system. [1]

**The evolutionary mechanism has multiple stages.** Sustained contact explains repeated opportunities for transfer, but the surviving genomic inventory also reflects integration, expression, selection and chance loss. Genes expressed in haustoria may have been retained because they assist parasitism; alternatively, some sequences may persist with little effect. The strongest adaptive argument combines foreign ancestry with genomic residence, long-term preservation and a relevant functional phenotype, rather than relying on any one of those observations.

**Section takeaway:** Haustoria make parasitic plants unusually informative systems for genetic exchange, but directly observed RNA traffic, inferred DNA-transfer mechanisms and inherited genomic acquisitions must remain distinct.

## 2. Between Fungi and Plants

**Finding: exchange is bidirectional, not necessarily balanced.** Richards and colleagues’ 2009 phylogenomic survey supported nine plant–fungus HGT events: five from fungi to plants and four from plants to fungi. Later studies supplied additional examples involving endophytic fungi, pathogenic fungi and mycorrhizal associations. These results support both directions, without implying that transfers are equally frequent or use one universal cellular mechanism. [2]

### Directionality is established across separate evolutionary cases

**The original comparison was broad but bounded.** The 2009 analysis compared the proteomes of *Arabidopsis thaliana*, *Oryza sativa*, *Populus trichocarpa*, *Selaginella moellendorffii*, *Sorghum bicolor* and the moss *Physcomitrella patens* with 159 prokaryotic and eukaryotic comparator species. None of its five fungus-to-plant events had an angiosperm recipient. Consequently, its early result supported exchange involving plant lineages without establishing the later grass examples. [2]

**Gene trees identify unexpected ancestry.** A fungal gene that groups inside a plant gene family, despite occurring among ordinary fungal neighboring genes, can support plant-to-fungus transfer. The reverse pattern can support fungal ancestry in a plant chromosome. Researchers also compare alternative evolutionary histories, including ancestral genes followed by repeated losses, because unusual similarity alone does not determine transfer direction.

**Independent examples strengthen the verdict.** Some plant-to-fungus assignments in the 2009 survey depended strongly on taxonomic distribution and were treated cautiously by its authors. Later evidence from *Colletotrichum*, *Pyrenophora* and *Rhizophagus irregularis* provides additional grounds for recognizing plant-derived genes in fungi. Conversely, grass loci related to *Epichloë* genes provide independently studied fungus-to-plant cases. [2][17]

### Fungi to plants: resistance-associated and other grass genes

**Several grass genes have fungal affinities.** The principal pre-2021 examples include *Fhb7*, associated with resistance to *Fusarium*, and three other gene families studied in cool-season grasses. In these cases, the historical donor is assigned to an ancestral fungal lineage; named modern fungi supply comparative sequences rather than necessarily identifying the individual ancient donor.

| Plant recipient | Fungal donor lineage or comparator | Gene | Reported evidence or significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Thinopyrum elongatum* | Proposed *Epichloë* lineage | *Fhb7* | GST; associated with *Fusarium* resistance |
| *Lolium perenne* and related grasses | Ancestral *Epichloë* | *LpBGNL* | Genomic locus; conserved intron position |
| *Lolium perenne* and broader grass lineages | *Epichloë festucae* comparator | *LpFTRL* | Approximately 85% sequence identity |
| *Lolium perenne* and Loliinae relatives | *E. festucae* comparator | *LpDUF3632* | Approximately 96% sequence identity |

The *LpBGNL* analysis was published in 2017; the additional grass candidates and discussion of *Fhb7* appeared in 2020. [18][13]

- **Fhb7 answers the resistance question.** In the wheat relative *Thinopyrum elongatum*, *Fhb7* encodes a ligE-type glutathione S-transferase, or GST. The 2020 literature presented it as a proposed *Epichloë*-derived gene contributing to resistance against *Fusarium* pathogens, with up to 97% DNA sequence identity to fungal homologues. It is therefore a resistance-associated fungal-to-plant example, rather than a conventional plant immune-receptor gene simply being moved from one plant to another. [13]

- **Its proposed transfer is relatively recent.** The grass analysis placed acquisition after *T. elongatum* diverged from wheat, a divergence estimated at 4.77–4.96 million years ago. This constrains a possible transfer interval; it does not date the transfer itself to exactly that divergence. The proposed donor is the *Epichloë* lineage, while *Epichloë festucae* is a specifically named donor-lineage comparator for the separate ryegrass genes discussed here. [13]

- **The Fhb7 interpretation merits discrimination.** A 2020 preprint found related ligE-type GSTs in additional organisms, including some liverworts and green algae, and reported high similarity to the fungus *Coniosporium apollinis*. That broader distribution complicates a simple argument based on the apparent absence of related genes outside fungi. It does not, by itself, disprove an *Epichloë*-related origin for *Fhb7*; it makes comprehensive family relationships more important than a single high-scoring sequence match. [19]

**Function and ancestry are separate claims.** The resistance association concerns what *Fhb7* does in a grass genetic background; fungal ancestry concerns how that sequence arrived there. Evidence strengthening one claim does not automatically settle the other. For plant breeding, the useful judgment is therefore that *Fhb7* is a named resistance-associated gene with a proposed fungal origin, while the exact historical donor assignment remains an evolutionary reconstruction.

- **LpBGNL provides another genomic example.** Perennial ryegrass, *Lolium perenne*, contains the fungal β-1,6-glucanase-like gene *LpBGNL*. The 2017 study inferred acquisition from an ancestral *Epichloë* endophyte into the common ancestor of grass subtribes Loliinae and Dactylidinae, approximately 9–13 million years ago. Its occurrence in *Lolium*, *Festuca* and *Dactylis*, together with genomic localization, supports an inherited grass locus rather than merely fungal RNA sampled from an infected leaf. [18]

- **Retained introns favor fungal genomic DNA.** A shared intron position between *LpBGNL* and the corresponding fungal gene favors transfer of a piece of the fungal genome rather than a reverse-transcribed mature transcript. The authors proposed uptake of DNA released after endophyte cell death. Because *Epichloë* can colonize reproductive tillers and inflorescences near tissues involved in seed formation, its life cycle supplies a plausible opportunity for eventual inheritance. [18]

- **Two further genes extend the pattern.** The 2020 analysis identified *LpFTRL*, named for similarity to a fungal transcriptional regulator, and *LpDUF3632*, named for a domain of unknown function. *LpFTRL*-related sequences occur in grasses including *Aegilops tauschii*, *Hordeum vulgare*, *Triticum aestivum* and *Secale cereale*; *LpDUF3632*-related sequences occur in relatives including *Lolium multiflorum* and *Festuca arundinacea*. Their proposed independent acquisitions fall within the last 39 million years. [13]

**Plant-genome context is especially important.** Endophytes live inside plants, so fungal contamination is an obvious alternative explanation for a fungal-looking sequence in plant material. The grass studies addressed this using endophyte-free material, surrounding plant-like sequences, independent genomic datasets and, for *LpDUF3632*, genetic linkage to a ryegrass chromosome group. These observations support residence in the plant genome, which is a stronger claim than sequence detection alone. [13]

### Plants to fungi: host-like genes in pathogens

**Plant-derived genes also occur in fungi.** The clearest examples here are plant-like subtilisins in *Colletotrichum* and a barley-related leucine-rich-repeat protein gene in *Pyrenophora*. The former supplies well-defined fungal locus identifiers; the latter supplies an explicitly proposed named plant donor.

| Ancestral plant lineage | *Colletotrichum gloeosporioides* | *CGLO_07890*, *CGLO_10271* | Related intact and truncated copies |

These are phylogenetic transfer assignments supported by fungal genomic context, rather than observations of the original ancient transfer. [17][20]

- **CPLS retains a plant-like protein architecture.** *GLRG_05578* in *C. graminicola* encodes a Colletotrichum plant-like subtilisin, abbreviated CPLS. Subtilisins are protein-cutting enzymes; the candidate grouped with plant subtilisins rather than ordinary fungal counterparts and retained characteristic domain arrangements. Related sequences occurred in *C. gloeosporioides*, *C. higginsianum*, *C. acutatum* and *C. sublineolum*, supporting acquisition before diversification of the sampled fungal species. [17]

- **Its plant donor was ancestral.** The 2013 study estimated the transfer at approximately 150–155 million years ago using relationships among plant subtilisins and fungal divergence dates. Maize, *Zea mays*, is the modern experimental host and a source of comparison genes; it should not be described as the identified ancient donor. This distinction preserves the strong plant-to-fungus inference without assigning a modern crop species to an event reconstructed deep in evolutionary time. [17]

- **Expression links CPLS to infection.** During *C. graminicola* infection of maize, *GLRG_05578* was induced at 48 and 72 hours after infection. At roughly the same stage, maize subtilisin genes *GRMZM2G091578* and *GRMZM2G414915* decreased in expression. The authors proposed that the fungal protein might mimic, replace or interfere with host protease activity; the temporal pattern supports involvement in infection but does not establish that CPLS caused the host changes. [17]

**The fungal locus is physically credible.** *GLRG_05578* occurred on a 192-kilobase fungal contig containing 64 predicted genes, with neighboring sequences showing fungal affinities. It was also amplified from fungal material grown without the host. Those observations address contamination separately from the gene-tree evidence establishing plant-like ancestry. [17]

- **Pyrenophora supplies a barley-linked example.** Sun and colleagues inferred acquisition of an LRR-protein gene from *Hordeum vulgare* into the lineage containing *P. teres* and *P. tritici-repentis*. Their broader inventory contained 14 HGT genes in each fungus, with 12 shared and two specific to each—16 distinct genes altogether. Most were assigned bacterial origins; the LRR-protein gene was the single plant-derived member of that inventory. [20]

**Resistance-like domains do not determine direction.** LRR domains occur in plant proteins involved in recognition and defense, but the *Pyrenophora* example concerns acquisition by a pathogen, not transfer of fungal resistance into a plant. The proposed benefit is interference with host biology. Likewise, the well-known *ToxA* transfer discussed in this literature is a separate fungus-to-fungus case, and the wheat gene *Tsn1* is a host susceptibility component with resistance-gene-like architecture. Keeping these cases separate avoids reversing the answer to the user’s question. [20]

- **Close association supplies an opportunity.** Fungal growth in or beside plant tissues creates repeated exposure to foreign nucleic acids. Nevertheless, physical proximity alone does not explain passage through membranes, entry into nuclei and chromosomal integration. The transformation-like proposal for *LpBGNL* is therefore a specific mechanistic hypothesis, not an established delivery apparatus equivalent to the *Agrobacterium* system considered next. [18]

**The biological consequences can oppose each other.** A fungal-derived gene retained by a grass may improve resistance or another aspect of plant physiology; a plant-derived gene retained by a fungus may help that fungus exploit its host. HGT direction therefore does not identify the “beneficiary” by itself. The recipient’s use of the sequence, rather than its original kingdom, determines the resulting ecological role.

## 3. Between Bacteria and Plants

**Finding: natural bacterial integration is well established.** *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* and related bacteria deliver a defined DNA segment into plant cells, and plant mechanisms can integrate that segment into chromosomes. Naturally inherited *Agrobacterium*-derived sequences occur in plants including *Nicotiana glauca*, *Linaria vulgaris* and sweet potato, *Ipomoea batatas*. Their existence is not a consequence of modern laboratory transformation. [22][23]

### How Agrobacterium delivers T-DNA

**T-DNA is a transferable plasmid segment.** “Transferred DNA,” abbreviated T-DNA, is the portion of a bacterial plasmid delivered to a plant cell. Tumor-inducing Ti plasmids and root-inducing Ri plasmids also carry much of the bacterial machinery needed for delivery. The transfer machinery and the transferred payload are distinct: the bacterium normally exports the bordered DNA segment, not its entire genome. [22]

1. **Plant signals activate bacterial virulence.** Plant-derived phenolic compounds, including acetosyringone, together with sugar signals activate the bacterial VirA/VirG regulatory system. This induces genes needed to process and export T-DNA. The bacterium is therefore responding to a host-associated chemical environment, rather than releasing DNA without a coordinated transfer program. [22]

2. **Border sequences define the cargo.** T-DNA is bounded by left- and right-border repeats approximately 24–25 base pairs long. VirD1 and VirD2 process the region into a single-stranded DNA intermediate called the T-strand; VirD2 remains covalently attached to its 5′ end. Transfer proceeds from the right-border side toward the left, helping explain why established insertions can be incomplete or left-truncated. [22][23]

3. **A secretion system exports the strand.** The VirD2–T-strand complex enters the plant through a type IV secretion system, a protein apparatus used to move molecules between cells. The *Agrobacterium* system contains 11 VirB proteins and VirD4. Bacterial effectors including VirE2 and VirF are exported through the same machinery; VirE2 can protect the transferred strand inside plant cells. [24]

4. **Bacterial and plant factors support nuclear entry.** VirD2 contains nuclear-localization information, while VirE2 participates in protection and intracellular trafficking of the DNA. In a 2001 assay using permeabilized tobacco BY-2 cells, artificial complexes containing VirD2, single-stranded DNA and VirE2 entered nuclei efficiently; removing the relevant VirD2 localization signal strongly reduced nuclear fluorescence. This supports components of the transport model, while VirE2’s exact targeting role has differed among experimental systems. [25][24]

5. **Plant repair machinery integrates the DNA.** *Agrobacterium* does not supply a dedicated integration enzyme analogous to the integrases of some viruses. Instead, host factors join T-DNA to plant chromosomes. Work in *Arabidopsis thaliana* implicated DNA double-strand breaks and DNA polymerase theta, consistent with repair pathways that can join sequences sharing only short stretches of similarity. [22]

6. **Expression and inheritance follow different requirements.** Integrated T-DNA can be expressed in the initially transformed tissue, producing a crown gall or hairy roots. Inheritance requires that transformed cells also contribute to a reproducing plant lineage. A transformed gall on an otherwise untransformed plant is therefore a real somatic HGT event, but it is not equivalent to an insertion transmitted throughout a species’ descendants. [26][8]

**Delivery is better resolved than integration.** The roles of borders, VirD processing proteins and the secretion apparatus provide an experimentally grounded sequence of bacterial actions. Integration is more dependent on the plant’s own cellular state and DNA-repair machinery, with different repair pathways potentially contributing in different contexts. It is therefore useful to regard *Agrobacterium* as a specialized DNA-delivery system whose cargo is subsequently handled by the recipient cell. [22]

### What the bacterial genes do after transfer

- **Hormone-related genes reshape plant growth.** T-DNA oncogenes—genes promoting abnormal tissue proliferation—alter auxin or cytokinin production or responses. In particular, *iaaM* and *iaaH* encode enzymes in an auxin-biosynthesis pathway, while *rol* genes influence rooting and plant development. These effects help explain why DNA transfer is integral to the bacterium’s pathogenic strategy rather than an incidental by-product of infection. [8]

- **Opine genes modify the feeding environment.** Other T-DNA genes encode enzymes producing opines, small compounds that can provide carbon and nitrogen to the bacteria. Examples include *Acs*, encoding agrocinopine synthase; *mis*, encoding mikimopine synthase; and *mas*, encoding mannopine synthase. The plant becomes a producer of compounds useful to the colonizing bacterium—a process often called genetic colonization. [8]

**Laboratory transformation redirects a natural capability.** Plant biotechnology uses the bacterial transfer system to deliver selected DNA constructs. Natural transformants, by contrast, preserve insertions acquired during their evolutionary history. The distinction concerns the origin and circumstances of the event, not whether the underlying process can produce genuine chromosomal integration. [23]

### Plants naturally carrying Agrobacterium-derived genes

**Natural insertions are called cellular T-DNA.** Once bacterial T-DNA is established in plant chromosomes, it is often described as cellular T-DNA, or cT-DNA. Its structure can include partial copies, direct repeats or inverted repeats, and individual genes may remain active, become silenced or accumulate mutations.

| Natural plant | Named bacterial-derived genes | Insert organization | Expression or phenotype |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Nicotiana glauca* | *NgrolB/C*, *NgORF13/14*, *NgmisL/R* | Imperfect inverted repeat | Low transcription; altered *NgrolB* activity |
| *Linaria vulgaris* | *LvrolA/B/C*, *Lvmis* and ORFs | Two imperfect direct-repeat copies | Conserved *rolC*; tissue-dependent activity |
| *Nicotiana tomentosiformis* | TA, TB, TC, TD; *TB-mas2′* | Four cT-DNA regions | Related *mas2′* expression in tobacco roots |

These natural systems were characterized before 2021 using sequence analysis, DNA hybridization, PCR and expression or functional assays. [23]

- **Tree tobacco preserves recognizable bacterial loci.** Untransformed *Nicotiana glauca* contains *NgrolB*, *NgrolC*, *NgORF13* and *NgORF14*, together with mikimopine-synthase homologues *NgmisL* and *NgmisR*. Their organization and sequence affinities point to a mikimopine-type *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* Ri-plasmid ancestry. Both *mis* homologues were transcribed at low levels, and NgMisR retained mikimopine-synthesis activity in a heterologous bacterial assay. [27][28]

- **A retained gene can change function.** Present-day *N. glauca* does not simply display the bacterial hairy-root program. Its *NgrolB* carried two point mutations that prevented root induction in the tested complementation system; correcting those mutations restored that capacity. This is unusually informative because it demonstrates how a naturally acquired gene can remain recognizable while its present biological effect diverges from the donor’s original pathogenic function. [27]

- **Toadflax contains an extensive gene set.** *Linaria vulgaris* carries two cT-DNA copies containing *LvORF2*, *LvORF3*, *LvORF8*, *LvrolA*, *LvrolB*, *LvrolC*, *LvORF13*, *LvORF14* and *Lvmis*. Related natural T-DNA sequences also occur in *Linaria dalmatica*. Although *rolC* retained an intact coding sequence, assays on in-vitro-grown *L. vulgaris* internodes, leaves and roots did not detect expression of the examined cT-DNA genes under those conditions. [26][23]

- **Tobacco ancestry records repeated acquisitions.** *Nicotiana tomentosiformis* contains four regions, TA, TB, TC and TD, attributed to different *Agrobacterium* strains. The TB region includes intact *TB-mas2′*, a mannopine-synthase gene related to one highly expressed in roots of some *Nicotiana tabacum* cultivars. Multiple inserts in one plant lineage show that natural transformation need not be a unique, unrepeated event. [23]

**Integration does not require continued disease.** Natural cT-DNA can persist after mutation or regulation has reduced the original growth-disrupting program. Conversely, an intact or expressed gene may acquire a subtler role in development or metabolism. The combination of *NgrolB* mutations, silent *Linaria* loci and expressed tobacco opine genes shows why insertion, expression and adaptive benefit should not be treated as interchangeable observations.

### Sweet potato: a particularly strong natural example

**Sweet potato contains two bacterial-derived regions.** Kyndt and colleagues’ 2015 study identified *IbT-DNA1* and *IbT-DNA2* in cultivated *Ipomoea batatas*. Their gene content resembles the right- and left-T-DNA regions of root-inducing *Agrobacterium* plasmids. The evidence combines sequence similarity with plant-flanking DNA, genomic assays, transcription and transmission to offspring. [3]

| Region | Named bacterial homologues | Main functional association | Distribution in the 2015 screens |
|---|---|---|---|
| *IbT-DNA1* | *iaaM*, *iaaH*, *C-prot*, *Acs* | Auxin pathway; agrocinopine synthesis | 291/291 cultivated hexaploid cultigens |
| *IbT-DNA2* | *ORF14*, *ORF17n*, *RolB/RolC*, *ORF13*, *ORF18/ORF17n* | Rooting-related and other T-DNA proteins | *ORF13* marker in 45/217 genotypes |

- **Plant-flanking DNA establishes chromosomal residence.** A bacterial artificial chromosome, or BAC—a large cloned DNA fragment—from sweet-potato variety Xu781 contained *IbT-DNA1* within an intron of an F-box gene. The 2015 primary paper reported a 22,146-base-pair complete region within a 79,099-base-pair BAC. A 2017 review described the inverted-repeat region as 21,564 base pairs; these source-specific descriptions are retained separately here, with the primary paper’s figure used for its BAC result. [3][8]

- **Inheritance supports established natural transformation.** The regions pass from parent to progeny, and their genes produce detectable transcripts in sweet-potato tissues. In landrace Huachano, low but measurable RNA occurred in leaf, stem, root, shoot-apex and storage-root samples. Those observations support inherited, transcriptionally active bacterial-derived DNA, rather than residual bacteria attached to or living within the plant. [3][8]

- **The two regions have different distributions.** In the 2015 study, *IbT-DNA1* occurred in all 291 tested hexaploid cultivated cultigens. The separate *IbT-DNA2 ORF13* screen found 45 positives among 217 genotypes: 42 hexaploid cultigens, two tetraploid *I. batatas* genotypes and one diploid *Ipomoea trifida*. Thus, the second region was polymorphic in the tested material rather than uniformly present wherever the first was detected. [3]

**The gene content makes the case biologically interesting.** *iaaM* encodes tryptophan-2-monooxygenase and *iaaH* encodes indole-3-acetamide hydrolase, linking *IbT-DNA1* to bacterial auxin synthesis. *Acs* links it to agrocinopine production, while the *RolB/RolC*-related sequence in *IbT-DNA2* belongs to a class associated with root development. These are credible routes by which bacterial DNA could influence a plant, but the functions of the present sweet-potato copies must be judged in their plant regulatory context. [3]

### Wild relatives clarify the evolutionary history

**The insertions extend beyond cultivated sweet potato.** A 2019 survey detected related T-DNA genes in additional members of *Ipomoea* series Batatas, including tetraploid *I. batatas* and wild relatives. This qualifies the early interpretation based on the narrower 2015 sample, in which *IbT-DNA1* had not been detected in the tested wild relatives. [29]

| Plant group | Region or marker | Positive accessions | Accessions examined |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Tetraploid *Ipomoea batatas* | *IbT-DNA1* | 3 | 15 |
| Tetraploid *I. batatas* | *IbT-DNA2* | 8 | 15 |
| *Ipomoea cordatotriloba* | *IbT-DNA1* | 1 | 5 |
| *Ipomoea tenuissima* | *IbT-DNA1* | 1 | 1 |
| *Ipomoea trifida* | *IbT-DNA2* | 9 | 28 |

- **Shared insertion sites support common ancestry.** Tetraploid and hexaploid *I. batatas* shared the *IbT-DNA1* insertion context, while *I. trifida* and the sweet-potato groups shared the examined *IbT-DNA2* insertion context. Such plant–insert junctions provide stronger historical evidence than bacterial-gene similarity alone because they connect the foreign sequence to the same inherited plant location. [29]

- **Sequence divergence gives an age estimate.** The 2019 study reported 1.2% divergence between *IbT-DNA1* repeats and used a calibration of 0.7% per million years. Under that study’s rate convention, 1.2 ÷ 0.7 gives approximately **1.7 million years**, a model-based estimate for the insertion’s age. That is an evolutionary-timescale estimate, not a date inferred from the beginning of sweet-potato cultivation. [29]

- **Several histories can produce today’s distribution.** One proposed history starts with a single ancestral infection transferring both regions, followed by inheritance, differential loss and lineage diversification. Another invokes at least two independent acquisitions, later brought together through shared ancestry or hybridization. The important distinction is that an original bacterial-to-plant transfer can subsequently spread among related plants through ordinary reproduction, which is not a new bacterial HGT event each time. [29]

**Natural integration is stronger than domestication causation.** It is tempting to connect auxin-related and rooting genes with sweet potato’s storage roots and vegetative propagation. However, the reported association tests for *IbT-DNA2* and root traits were generally weak, with a root-yield association appearing at one location. The supported conclusion is that bacterial-derived genes became inherited components of a major food crop; a claim that they caused domestication or storage-root formation goes beyond that result. [8]

**Section takeaway:** *Agrobacterium* demonstrates an active bacterial DNA-delivery mechanism, and sweet potato provides particularly strong evidence that natural integration can become inherited and transcriptionally active without preserving the original disease phenotype.

**Mechanism and transferred material**

- **DNA cargo:** The transferred object is DNA, principally the border-defined T-DNA region of a Ti plasmid. In engineered systems, researchers replace or “disarm” natural T-DNA genes and introduce a plasmid carrying genes of interest; the T4SS can then deliver the engineered cargo. Bacterial chromosomal DNA and other mobile genetic elements can also be transferred in some experimental systems, but a cited 2008 study on *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* chromosomal DNA does not, in the retained evidence, establish natural integration, inheritance or expression in a wild plant lineage. [214]

- **General bacterial routes are context, not an Agrobacterium mechanism:** Bacterial HGT more generally includes transformation, in which competent cells take up environmental DNA; transduction, in which bacteriophages package and inject DNA; and conjugation, in which cell contact, a pilus and a mating bridge transfer plasmid DNA. Stable maintenance can occur through homologous recombination or independent plasmid replication. A 2025 teaching chapter also describes Hfr chromosome transfer and the engineered *E. coli* pGLO example, which carries ampicillin-resistance and green fluorescent protein (**GFP**) genes; these are later, bacteria-to-bacteria or laboratory examples, not evidence for the pre-2021 *Agrobacterium* pathway. [172]

**Natural plant integrations and the sweet-potato case**

- **Named natural plant examples:** A later synthesis reports historical HGT from naturally occurring *Agrobacterium* in sweet potato, several *Nicotiana* species, banana and more than 30 dicot species, including peanut, walnut, guava, hops and tea (*Camellia sinensis*). It contrasts these naturally occurring integrations with engineered *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation. The cited 2017 and 2019 reviews provide pre-2021 context for natural *Agrobacterium* transformants, although the retained spans do not give the relevant gene names, genomic coordinates or inheritance tests for every listed plant. [214]

- **Earlier *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* leads:** A review cites a 2001 study titled “The horizontal transfer of *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* genes and the evolution of the genus *Nicotiana*.” It also cites a 1983 report of sequences homologous to *A. rhizogenes* T-DNA in uninfected plants and a 2002 report that tobacco plants were transformed by *A. rhizogenes* infection during their evolution. These are relevant pre-2021 leads, but the retained reference-list spans do not supply the species-level genomic, inheritance, expression or contamination-control evidence needed to classify each as a confirmed natural integration. [88]

**Evidence quality and reverse-direction controls**

- **Genome-wide screening:** A study published on 1 January 2021—therefore later than the report’s 2020-12-31 primary-evidence cutoff—used a bacterial–plant interaction system and identified 373 candidate HGT events, of which 90 were confirmed as true events; the reported sequence-similarity range was 27.27%–86.5%. It also classified 309 “one-species” events as sequence errors or inaccurate annotations. The study proposed both ancient transfer and recent specific transfer involving *Agrobacteria*, and reported enrichment of donor genera among plant-associated bacteria; at the same time, it stated that no clade- or species-specific HGTs were detected and that the detected events were ancient at the origin of angiosperms. Because of its date and internally mixed ancient/recent framing, it is best used as retrospective corroboration rather than as a pre-2021 discovery source. [115]

- **Environmental and field qualifications:** The same account reports a transfer frequency of \(5.8 \times 10^{-2}\) per recipient bacterium under optimum conditions but an extrapolated \(2.0 \times 10^{-17}\) under assumed “natural conditions”; the latter is a projection, and natural conditions were described as unknown and unpredictable. In field material, transgenic DNA reportedly persisted in soil for up to two years; after 18 months, two of seven total bacterial-DNA samples were positive, whereas none of 4,000 isolated bacterial colonies tested positive. Because fewer than 1% of soil bacteria were considered culturable and DNA adsorbed to bacterial surfaces could not be excluded, the authors treated the result as possible rather than conclusive HGT. [213]

- **Additional controls and alternatives:** Plant-to-*Acinetobacter baylyi* transformation with pure plant DNA was reported at \(5.5 \times 10^{-11}\) transformants per recipient, but the review judged this higher than would be expected in nature because DNA persistence and bacterial competence would be lower. Bacteria may encounter plant DNA through commensal, symbiotic or parasitic contact, soil, water, decomposition products and herbivore guts, yet the role of HGT in bacterial adaptation to plant-associated niches remains poorly understood. Phylogenetic incongruence, nucleotide composition, experimental transfer and evolutionary scenarios are relevant tests, but gene loss, poor alignments, long-branch attraction, incomplete sampling, organelle-to-nucleus transfer and DNA contamination can mimic bacterial HGT. [88]

## 4. Between Viruses and Plants

**Finding: viral genes sometimes persist permanently.** Plant chromosomes can contain endogenous viral elements, or EVEs—DNA sequences derived from viruses and retained as part of the host genome. Their presence demonstrates historical viral-sequence acquisition, but integration is not a universal consequence of infection. Some elements are defective or silenced; others remain expressed, and a few can regenerate infectious virus. [30]

**Finding: viruses can carry plant material.** Viral particles can package host-derived nucleic acids, and experiments with Beet curly top Iran virus demonstrated replication and transcription of beet-derived DNA in other plant species. A vector is a carrier that transports biological material; here it is important to distinguish a virus carrying plant DNA from an insect vector carrying the virus. Both can contribute to delivery, while neither automatically establishes chromosomal integration or inheritance. [4]

### Ordinary infection differs from endogenous integration

- **Replication can occur outside plant chromosomes.** Plant pararetroviruses are DNA viruses that use reverse transcription—copying RNA back into DNA—during replication. Unlike retroviruses whose life cycles require a proviral stage, plant pararetroviruses have no established obligatory integration step or dedicated integrase mechanism. Their integrated descendants are called endogenous pararetroviruses, or EPRVs. [30]

- **Host repair can capture viral DNA.** Analyses of endogenous Rice tungro bacilliform virus-like elements, called eRTBVLs, in rice, *Oryza sativa*, support incidental incorporation during repair of broken plant DNA. AT-rich regions are susceptible to double-strand breaks, and viral DNA present in the nucleus can become joined to those breaks through nonhomologous end joining. This provides a plausible route to integration without requiring the virus to encode an integration program. [30]

- **Inheritance changes the meaning of persistence.** A viral genome replicating in an infected leaf persists during that infection. An integrated viral element transmitted to offspring persists as part of a plant lineage, even if no virus particles remain. Subsequent mutation, rearrangement or epigenetic silencing—chemical and chromatin-based regulation of DNA—can transform an initially infectious sequence into a genomic remnant. [30]

**Historical insertions answer the persistence question.** Plant–virus sequence junctions, chromosome localization and segregation in offspring establish viral DNA as part of plant genomes. They are stronger evidence for integration than detecting viral DNA in total tissue extracts, which can also contain freely replicating viral genomes. Conversely, the existence of old insertions does not mean that integration is a routine, obligatory stage of the virus responsible for them.

### Bananas contain inherited and sometimes infectious viral elements

**Musa provides a particularly informative case.** The wild banana *Musa balbisiana*, which contributes the B genome to many cultivated bananas, contains endogenous banana streak virus sequences. An early integrated Obino l’Ewai-related sequence reported in banana breeding material had 99% identity to the corresponding virus. Subsequent work established multiple viral species, loci and activation behavior. [31]

- **ORF III identifies a viral gene region.** Geering and colleagues’ 2005 survey sequenced 103 clones from sampled *Musa acuminata*, *M. balbisiana*, *M. schizocarpa* and cultivated banana material. All resembled the reverse-transcriptase/RNase H region of badnavirus open reading frame III, or *ORF III*. An 85% sequence-identity grouping threshold separated 36 sequence groups; these are molecular sequence groups, not 36 experimentally demonstrated infectious viruses. [31]

**Three endogenous BSV species were genetically characterized.** In the seedy *Musa balbisiana* accession Pisang klutuk wulung, abbreviated PKW, a 2013 study combined BAC sequencing, offspring segregation and fluorescence in situ hybridization, which locates DNA on chromosomes. Each of the three endogenous virus species occupied a single locus. [32]

- **Rearranged sequences can still yield infection.** Endogenous BSV insertions contain duplicated, inverted and fragmented viral sequences rather than a simple intact virus genome pasted into a chromosome. Their organization can nevertheless preserve the information required to reconstruct an infectious genome. For eBSImV, the two structurally identical alleles prevented the study from distinguishing which allele supplied the infectious sequence. [32]

- **Activation is conditional, not automatic.** Earlier work proposed that homologous recombination could remove scrambled regions and rejoin viral segments into a circular genome. Tissue culture was identified as a stimulus associated with activation in particular banana material, while interspecific hybridization can also alter the context in which endogenous sequences are regulated. These mechanisms explain how apparently healthy parental material can produce infected hybrid or propagated descendants. [31]

- **Banana genomic backgrounds matter.** Cavendish bananas, with a pure *Musa acuminata* background, lacked the specific *M. balbisiana*-linked Obino l’Ewai integration discussed in the early work, but they carried another nonfunctional endogenous badnavirus sequence associated with *M. acuminata*. Thus, absence of one infectious integrant does not imply absence of all viral-derived DNA. [31]

**Breeding consequences follow from the distinction.** Screening a banana for an integrated viral sequence answers a different question from screening it for an active episomal infection, where the viral genome exists outside the chromosome. An endogenous sequence can be harmless in one genetic background yet contribute to activation in a particular hybrid or tissue-culture context. This makes inherited viral DNA a practical breeding issue, not merely a molecular fossil.

### Other plants preserve viral genes with different outcomes

**Retention ranges from fragments to expression.** Pre-2021 findings include geminivirus-like DNA, pararetrovirus-like repeats and sequences related to RNA-virus genes. Their outcomes differ substantially: some are silent genomic repeats, some produce RNA or protein, and some retain the potential for viral reactivation.

- **Yam elements can produce viral protein.** *Dioscorea alata* and 22 other examined *Dioscorea* species in the Enantiophyllum clade contained endogenous geminivirus-like sequences, EGV1 and EGV2. - **Tobacco GRD preserves replication-related DNA.** Bejarano and colleagues identified geminivirus-related DNA, or GRD, at one nuclear locus in eight *Nicotiana tabacum* cultivars in 1996. - **TPVL repeats illustrate extensive inactivation.** A 1999 study of healthy *N. tabacum* estimated approximately 1,000 tobacco pararetrovirus-like copies per diploid genome by slot-blot analysis. Twenty-two independent insertions collectively allowed reconstruction of an approximately 8-kilobase viral genome, and 14 plant–virus junctions supported genomic integration. The sequenced copies contained frameshifts and stop codons, demonstrating that abundant viral ancestry can persist without intact infectious coding capacity. [35]

- **RNA-virus ancestry also appears in DNA.** Chiba and colleagues’ 2011 study identified plant chromosomal sequences related to non-retroviral RNA viruses and confirmed most by direct molecular analysis of plant DNA. Common examples resembled partitivirus coat-protein genes, abbreviated *CP*, while cytorhabdovirus- and varicosavirus-like nucleocapsid genes occurred across more than nine plant families. These insertions imply an RNA-to-DNA step somewhere in their history, even though the viruses themselves lack a normal DNA stage. [36]

- **ILR2 links a plant locus and mycovirus.** The coat protein of Rosellinia necatrix partitivirus 2, RnPV2, showed its highest sequence similarity to *Arabidopsis thaliana ILR2*, named IAA/LEU resistant 2. RnPV2 infects the fungus *Rosellinia necatrix*. This supports investigation of viral–cellular sequence exchange, but sequence relatedness does not establish a directly observed transfer between that modern fungus and that modern plant. [36]

- **Dahlia supplies another expressed endogenous element.** In *Dahlia variabilis*, the endogenous pararetrovirus sequence DvEPRS showed multiple genomic integrations by Southern hybridization, and RNA assays detected transcripts corresponding to ORFs I, V and VI. This is evidence for expression from endogenous viral-derived sequences, which should be distinguished from the separate episomal dahlia viruses examined in the same study. [37]

**Some endogenous elements regenerate virus.** By the pre-2021 period, infectious endogenous systems included Tobacco vein clearing virus in *Nicotiana edwardsonii*, Petunia vein clearing virus in *Petunia hybrida* and banana streak viruses in interspecific *Musa* hybrids. Their activation contrasts with the defective tobacco TPVL copies. It also shows why an integrated viral element is not necessarily a permanently inert fossil. [32]

**Antiviral benefit remains a distinct hypothesis.** Endogenous viral sequences can generate small RNAs or become methylated, and homologous promoter constructs were silenced in tobacco carrying corresponding endogenous sequences. This makes antiviral silencing biologically plausible. However, a sequence being silenced—or producing potentially targeting small RNAs—is different from demonstrating improved resistance of the whole plant to infection. [30]

### BCTIV demonstrates capture and carriage of plant DNA

**The strongest carrier example begins in beet.** Catoni and colleagues’ 2018 study examined *Beta vulgaris* infected with Beet curly top Iran virus, BCTIV, a single-stranded DNA geminivirus. It discovered small circular molecules combining viral regulatory DNA with beet chromosomal DNA. These hybrid minicircles provide a concrete example of host DNA being recruited into a virus-dependent replicating system. [4]

- **Natural infections contained hybrid circles.** Three minicircles cloned from two field-collected beet plants measured 1,294–1,503 nucleotides. Their viral portions included the BCTIV intergenic region and replication origin, while the remaining segments matched beet chromosomal DNA. The captured beet fragments were AT-rich and lacked open reading frames, meaning they were not identified protein-coding host genes. [4]

- **Controlled infection reproduced DNA capture.** Within four weeks of experimental BCTIV inoculation, beet plants produced additional hybrid circles. Across four characterized circles, viral intergenic portions ranged from 459 to 664 nucleotides and captured beet portions from 820 to 998 nucleotides. These are component ranges across different molecules; the biological finding is repeated joining of host DNA to viral sequences that support replication. [4]

**Formation and propagation have different host ranges.** Under the tested infection conditions, beet produced host–virus hybrid circles, whereas *Arabidopsis thaliana* and *Nicotiana benthamiana* produced smaller virus-only defective molecules. Nevertheless, an experimental clone of the naturally identified hybrid MC#1 replicated and spread systemically in all three species when BCTIV was supplied alongside it. [4]

| Plant | Small circles formed during BCTIV infection | Introduced MC#1 with BCTIV |
|---|---|---|
| *Beta vulgaris* | Hybrid host–virus and virus-only circles | Replication and systemic spread |
| *Arabidopsis thaliana* | Virus-only defective circles | Replication and systemic spread |
| *Nicotiana benthamiana* | Virus-only defective circles | Replication and systemic spread |

- **Captured beet DNA was transcribed elsewhere.** RNA spanning the beet-derived MC#1 segment was detected in both *B. vulgaris* and *N. benthamiana* during co-replication with BCTIV. Thus, a sequence originating in one plant species could be maintained and transcribed in another compatible host in the experimental system. The product remained a transcript of captured noncoding DNA, not evidence that the fragment encoded a new protein. [4]

- **Viral particles packaged the hybrid cargo.** Purified BCTIV virions from co-infected *N. benthamiana* contained both ordinary BCTIV DNA and the beet-derived DNA carried by MC#1. Detection with separate probes for viral and host-derived portions established encapsidation—enclosure within viral particles. The BCTIV coat protein therefore supplied a physical carrier for the hybrid DNA rather than merely helping it replicate inside a cell. [4]

- **A leafhopper supplies a plausible delivery route.** BCTIV is transmitted by *Circulifer haematoceps*, a leafhopper that feeds on multiple plant hosts. Transmission is circulative and nonpropagative: virus travels through the insect and reaches its salivary glands without replicating in the vector. Because MC#1 was packaged in BCTIV particles, the authors considered export to other plants by this route very likely; the direct experimental cross-host tests themselves used inoculated constructs. [4]

**The mechanism contains two ecological filters.** First, a particular host–virus combination must generate the hybrid molecule; in these experiments that happened in beet. Second, another plant must support the helper virus and the captured molecule’s propagation. This explains why a carrier’s potential host range need not match the range of species that initially donate DNA, and why delivery can be directional even when several plants share susceptibility to the virus.

**The established endpoint is viral carriage.** MC#1 capture, replication, systemic movement, packaging and transcription are directly supported steps. Chromosomal integration is a different process from those observed viral DNA circles. The decisive standard for completed, inherited host-gene HGT would be a named coding gene recovered from the carrier, transmitted into a different plant species, joined to recipient chromosomal DNA and inherited by progeny; this standard identifies exactly what would change the present interpretation.

### RNA packaging and engineered vectors answer narrower questions

**RNA can also enter viral particles.** The BCTIV paper explicitly discusses earlier plant RNA-virus packaging work, including a 2015 study of host-RNA encapsidation by cucumber necrosis virus coat protein and a 2018 study of host-RNA packaging by brome mosaic virus. Accordingly, host-RNA packaging should not be dismissed as biologically absent from plants. Its significance is that it supplies a physical transport intermediate; conversion to inherited plant DNA would still require additional copying and integration events. [4]

- **Viral movement proteins transport viral genomes.** Geminivirus coat protein, *CP*, and the bipartite begomovirus movement proteins *BV1* and *BC1* support movement within plants. Whiteflies such as *Bemisia tabaci* acquire begomoviruses from one plant and introduce them into another. These mechanisms explain efficient interplant transmission of viral genomes, but they should not be reclassified automatically as transfer of a plant’s own genes. [38]

- **Engineered cargo can alter host expression.** A 2016 experiment used a Cotton leaf crumple virus vector carrying a 354-base-pair fragment of cotton sequence *Contig28334*. After delivery to upland cotton, *Gossypium hirsutum*, its target’s expression fell by more than 30%, and subsequent cotton leaf curl disease-virus accumulation was approximately threefold higher than in the negative control. This demonstrates virus-induced gene silencing using a selected host fragment; it is not a natural, inherited transfer between different plant species. [39]

**Parasitic bridges provide another transport connection.** *Cuscuta* can convey viruses between attached plants: experiments transferred potato virus Y isolate N between *Nicotiana tabacum* plants through dodder, with little accumulation in the bridge itself. Such results connect the parasitic and viral sections of this report, but the endpoint is transmission of an infectious agent. They do not turn the virus or the bridge into demonstrated vehicles of inherited host-gene integration. [1]

**Carrier capacity and genetic permanence differ.** A virus is well suited to protecting nucleic acids, entering cells and moving between hosts. It is therefore a credible contributor to HGT opportunities. Yet a replicating viral or hybrid molecule can disappear with the infection, whereas an endogenous element has crossed the additional threshold of chromosomal retention and inheritance. The virus evidence is strongest when these endpoints are reported separately.

**Section takeaway:** Viruses sometimes leave inherited sequences in plants and can package host-derived material, but endogenous viral DNA, ordinary infection, engineered gene silencing and completed plant-to-plant host-gene HGT are different biological outcomes.

### Overall judgment across the four contexts

**The strongest claims concern different endpoints.** The evidence does not support a single ranking in which one interaction is simply “more HGT” than another. *Agrobacterium* is strongest for a characterized active delivery mechanism; sweet potato is especially persuasive for natural inherited bacterial integration; parasitic plants combine direct molecular exchange with extensive genomic footprints; fungus–plant studies illuminate ancient bidirectional acquisition; and viruses demonstrate both inherited viral insertions and experimentally supported carrier intermediates.

**Evidence and measurement boundaries matter.** The inventories above count different units—transfer events, loci, sequence groups, transcripts or informative mitochondrial sequences—and are not global transfer rates. Sequence identities describe similarity, while expression assays describe transcription rather than adaptive benefit. Ancient donor assignments and dates are evolutionary reconstructions; the reported ages are model-based estimates. Named modern comparators identify related lineages, not necessarily the individual historical donors.

**The central conclusion is durable.** Plant genomes are not closed to foreign genetic contributions. Parasitic and other intimate biological relationships repeatedly create opportunities for exchange, but evolutionary HGT requires more than contact or transport: foreign material must become established in a recipient lineage. The most informative pre-2021 discoveries show both sides of that story—the molecules crossing biological boundaries and the genomic changes that sometimes survive long afterward.
