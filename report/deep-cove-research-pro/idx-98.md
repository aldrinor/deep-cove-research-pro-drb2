# Mary Lou Williams as Jazz Educator and Historian, 1970–1981

**Mary Lou Williams taught history through sound.** In the 1970s, she combined university teaching, ensemble direction, lecture-recitals, recordings, and work with children to explain jazz as a living African American inheritance. Duke University became her sustained institutional base in fall 1977: she taught jazz history with Father Peter O’Brien and wrote for and directed the Duke Jazz Ensemble. Her teaching emphasized listening, singing, historical understanding, and musical feeling—not technique alone. [1]

**Her Jazz Tree expressed an argument.** Its central lineage runs from the suffering of slavery through spirituals, ragtime, Kansas City swing, and bop; blues rises alongside that development rather than occupying a single chronological stage. Its flourishing leaves represent musicians, while its dead branches express her objections to commercial rock, certain avant-garde practices, cults, “black magic,” and instruction reduced to exercises. Yet Williams’s judgments were selective: she admired John Coltrane and Eric Dolphy, experimented herself, and attempted an ambitious collaboration with Cecil Taylor. [2]

**Four questions organize this report.** The first section establishes where and how she taught; the second reconstructs the Tree’s architecture and source-specific labels. The third explains her historical and spiritual principles, and the fourth compares her judgments of styles and musicians, distinguishing personal appraisals from broader aesthetic criteria.

## Educational Activities and Teaching Philosophy

### Teaching locations and the late-career chronology

**Duke anchored an already mobile practice.** Williams’s educational work did not begin with a university appointment. By the time she settled into Duke’s music department, she was already presenting jazz history through performances, public explanation, and recordings. The Mary Lou Williams Foundation dates her full return to the jazz world to 1970 and describes her final four years as combining Duke responsibilities with college workshops, festivals, club appearances, broadcasting, and recording. [3]

**The institutional pattern matters more than titles.** Duke provided continuity: she could return to students over a semester, hear their development, direct an ensemble, and place her historical ideas inside a university curriculum. Visiting residencies offered a different advantage: concentrated contact with performers, non-musicians, choirs, and public audiences. Her informal mentoring added another route, retaining the close musician-to-musician transmission through which she had learned.

### What she actually did at Duke

**Her course welcomed non-musicians.** O’Brien describes a course introducing students to jazz “in all its forms,” rather than a specialist course limited to instrumental technique. Other biographical accounts identify it as *History of Jazz*. O’Brien was her teaching colleague as well as her manager and a Jesuit priest; their collaboration connected her performing experience with sustained spoken historical presentation. [1][4]

**Ensemble work made history practical.** Williams also wrote for and directed Duke’s Jazz Ensemble. Students therefore encountered her not only as a narrator of earlier achievements but as a composer, arranger, conductor, and working pianist. A classroom account could identify swing phrasing; a rehearsal could require players to produce it together. Those were complementary ways of teaching the same musical inheritance. [1]

**Students remembered an experiential teacher.** Bill Cunliffe, who studied at Duke during her residency, recalled learning through stories, playing, and observing how she thought about music rather than through an academically predetermined method. Hamida Jackson-Little similarly remembered that Williams helped students understand both the music and its history: it was not simply a class in which they listened to recordings. [11][8]

**Experiential did not mean unstructured.** Williams’s four-era historical sequence, her repeated return to spirituals and blues, and her insistence that students internalize one kind of phrasing before proceeding show a definite instructional order. The structure lay partly in repertoire and embodied demonstration rather than exclusively in written explanation. Her standards could be demanding even when the classroom atmosphere was personal.

**Duke also supplied a cultural setting.** The university’s retrospective describes her jazz program as a focal point for Black students and Black campus culture. That institutional recollection fits her insistence that jazz history involved a people’s experience, not simply a succession of fashionable sounds. It also helps explain why teaching non-musicians mattered: she was cultivating a historically informed community of listeners as well as developing performers. [8]

**Several records locate this teaching directly.** Duke’s catalogue names a lecture dated 15 September 1977. Rutgers separately catalogues a late-1970s recording titled *Mary Lou Williams in Duke University classroom*, credited as a WFMU production from Greensboro, North Carolina. The latter survives in three listed video formats, which represent copies or formats of the classroom item rather than three separately documented classes. [5][12]

### The lecture-recital: explanation, demonstration, and participation

**Speaking and playing formed one argument.** The clearest documentary model is *The History of Jazz*, recorded in Williams’s New York apartment in late 1970 and issued by Folkways in 1978. She wrote and recorded the narration herself using a Tandberg recorder, then illustrated the unfolding eras at her Hamilton, or Baldwin, piano. The recording date and release date belong to different stages of the same project. [1]

**The examples carried historical meaning.** Williams introduced her original “Lord Have Mercy” as an interpretation of the spirituals. For ragtime, she played “Who Stole the Lock Off the Henhouse Door,” identifying it as a tune her mother had taught her. That introduction did two jobs: it placed the music within a historical category and demonstrated how musical knowledge passed through family memory. [13]

**Live presentations allowed different proportions.** A solo concert could compress historical explanation into a connected musical survey, while a campus residency could expand discussion and participation over several days. Scott Yanow describes her 1970s historical recitals as moving through gospel, stride, swing, bebop, and modern jazz, sometimes including music bordering on the avant-garde and an original religious conclusion. This was a flexible performing practice, not one immutable script. [14]

| Presentation type | Principal components | Audience relationship | Historical purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrated history recording | Prepared speech and piano examples | Guided listening | Connect eras, personal memory, and musical principles |
| Solo historical recital | Connected pieces and stylistic contrasts | Hear transitions in performance | Make several historical languages audible |

The recording and residency descriptions come from O’Brien’s Folkways notes [1]; the recital model is also described by Yanow [14].

**The Montreux medley gives a concrete example.** At the 1978 festival, Williams opened with an approximately eleven-minute historical sequence. It moved from her spiritual “The Lord Is Heavy” to the fast rag/stride piece “Fandangle,” a slow blues, “For the Figs” as a Kansas City swing example, “Baby Bear Boogie,” and “Roll ’Em.” These are the selections of that particular concert, not a substitute track list for the Folkways recording. [15]

**Transitions were part of the teaching.** The Montreux account describes tremolos and sustained musical momentum connecting pieces. The implication is important: Williams could demonstrate continuity without stopping to explain every connection verbally. A listener heard changes in bass patterns, rhythmic attack, phrasing, and harmonic vocabulary while remaining inside one pianist’s unfolding performance.

**Campus residencies added conversation and collaboration.** O’Brien describes three- or five-day visits that could include a trio concert, an appearance in a music or Black-history class, and a large-hall lecture-demonstration. At the piano and through question-and-answer periods, she explained the roots, history, and development of Black American music and jazz. A college archivist might record oral history, while bands or Glee Clubs could rehearse toward a performance with her. [1]

**Each component served a different learner.** The concert established what the music could achieve; the demonstration slowed attention toward particular sounds; discussion supplied historical connections; rehearsal required participants to act on what they heard. Oral-history recording preserved the testimony of a participant in the music’s development. Together, these activities made the visiting artist both a performer and a historical witness.

### Teaching feeling before mechanical competence

**Singing was a central method.** In O’Neill’s 1979 account, Williams described having Duke students sing the successive eras, including bebop syllables such as “shoo-be-doo-be-doo.” She wanted the music to enter their ears and “saturate the entire mind” before they moved forward. Students were not merely identifying a style; they were trying to reproduce its phrasing and expressive character. [4]

**One classroom reversal explains her priorities.** Williams recalled assigning a blues that the students sang too coldly. Instead of pressing onward, she returned them to spirituals; afterward, she thought their feeling improved. This is unusually specific evidence of her teaching in action: historical return was a corrective exercise, not decorative background. [4]

**Attack and phrasing made feeling audible.** During the interview, Williams tapped a rhythm, hummed, and sang “I Cover the Waterfront” in different ways to demonstrate swing and bop phrasing. Her explanation focused on how a note was attacked and allowed to linger or move onward. “Feeling” therefore referred to perceivable musical decisions as well as an inward emotional or spiritual state. [4]

**Physical proximity supported that transmission.** Pianist Sumi Tonooka remembered sitting immediately beside Williams while Williams played, connecting with the physical movement of performance. This individual teaching recollection is distinct from the Duke course, but it illuminates a related principle: observation could communicate timing, touch, and musical intention that verbal instructions alone might leave abstract. [11]

**Technique remained necessary but insufficient.** Williams warned that a pianist could play “like a typewriter” and still fail to communicate. Yet she also praised the training of earlier musicians and described learning arranging from Andy Kirk by watching him write down her ideas. Her objection was not to knowledge, notation, or disciplined work; it was to substituting technical execution for musical understanding. [4][16]

**Historical grounding was preparation for freedom.** In the Keystone Korner interview, Williams described taking young musicians back to Fats Waller and then bringing them forward. She argued that players needed older music in order to play avant-garde music convincingly. Her teaching was therefore not simply a demand to reproduce the past: it presented the past as a resource for creating something individually new. [10]

### Education beyond the classroom

**Choir work turned audiences into participants.** *Mary Lou’s Mass* could involve professional, amateur, or children’s choirs. Oxford describes Williams refining school choirs already prepared by their teachers, while O’Brien told O’Neill that she rewrote sung parts to match the abilities of the people assembled in a church, college, or community setting. Adaptation was part of the educational work. [9][4]

**Accessibility did not require musical isolation.** Participants could enter through congregational singing while hearing a jazz pianist and bassist shape the performance. In 1975, more than 3,000 people attended *Mary Lou’s Mass* at St. Patrick’s Cathedral—the first jazz Mass celebrated there. Its educational importance lay partly in challenging the assumption that jazz and sacred communal life were incompatible. [4]

**Children were a deliberate long-term concern.** Rutgers records that Williams established her foundation in 1980 with O’Brien and Joyce Breach to advance public knowledge of jazz by teaching it, in all its forms, to children aged six to twelve. This stated mission complements the college course for non-musicians: both sought to build receptive listeners, not merely recruit future virtuosos. [6]

**Her final work retained the historical mission.** At Duke, she also worked on the unfinished composition now discussed as *History…: A Wind Symphony*. Anthony M. Kelley’s account describes 1979 rehearsal footage in which Williams listened and commented from hospital. Historical presentation had expanded from the solo piano into a substantial university ensemble project. [17]

**Her educational aim was cultural continuity.** The connecting thread was the formation of people able to hear, feel, perform, and value jazz’s inheritance; Duke supplied a sustained base for that work, while recitals, residencies, choirs, and children’s education carried it outward.

**Scope and central finding**

For this report, Mary Lou Williams’s later-years window is **1970 through 28 May 1981**. Rutgers archival and biographical records give her death date as **28 May 1981** [33][34], while a five-page Routledge reference entry, first published in 2004, prints **13 May 1981** [32]. That conflict should remain visible; the multiple archival and biographical records support 28 May as the working endpoint. The same Routledge entry dates Williams’s return to music-making to the **1970 recording *From the Heart*** and places the creation of her **Mary** label in the mid-1970s; these are recording activities, not evidence of teaching or lecture-recital work [32].

The strongest evidence presents Williams as both educator and historian. A Rutgers finding aid reports that, in the **late 1970s**, she created musical lectures on the history of jazz, developed a tree of jazz with artist David Sloan Wilson, and made history-inflected recordings; in **1977**, she accepted a position at Duke University and spent her final years educating young students on jazz [33]. A separate dated artifact is titled *The History of Jazz According to Mary Lou* and is identified as recorded live on **8 May 1977** [40].

An Oxford event description further says that during the last decade of her life Williams promoted her version of jazz history through **live performances, lectures, and recordings** [36]. **Teaching philosophy and evidence limits**

The defensible synthesis—marked as analysis rather than a direct Williams quotation—is that her late teaching joined **historical narration with active musical practice**: she taught jazz history, directed an ensemble, presented musical lectures, performed, recorded, and continued writing about jazz [33][34][36][41]. Her personal papers are described as containing a continuing narrative, revisions, a handwritten index, and essays from the late 1960s onward about the state and history of jazz; this supports her role as a self-documenting historian but does not identify a teaching venue or lecture sequence [41].

Williams’s attributed teaching philosophy—partly preserved through earlier context—was oral and historically grounded: she learned by ear; her mother rejected formal lessons to preserve improvisation; and Williams advised students to imitate recordings of great masters before forming their own style. She opposed conservatory exercises that, in her view, destroyed jazz’s natural feeling. She believed younger people should learn from elders so they could add to the future, and used jazz-history teaching, including with New York City street kids, to pass on tradition—not simply to oppose avant-garde music; she had experimented with avant-garde playing in 1940s rehearsals. [54] She described jazz as spiritual, loving, communal, and potentially healing in both club and church; in the related Mass account, accessibility involved simple forms, catchy riffs, and accessible lyrics. [54] Her 1978 Montreux *Solo Recital* combined spiritual themes, ragtime, blues, and swing. [55][56]

**Duke and the evidence-bounded venue roster**

Mary Lou Williams’s clearest documented late-career university teaching site was **Duke University in Durham, North Carolina**: she went there as Artist in Residence in **1977**, taught a course in jazz history, wrote for and conducted a jazz orchestra, and continued teaching until February; the obituary does not specify the year of that February endpoint [60]. A later Duke account reports that she was hired in **1977** as the university’s first artist-in-residence and director of its jazz ensemble, remained at Duke for **four years (1977–1981)**, mentored students, and promoted Black culture [58]. It is a historical performance with spoken framing, not proof of a standardized lecture-recital [131]. *The History of Jazz* is catalogued as a 1978 release [125].

Rutgers lists the Jazz Tree artwork and circa 1977–1978 notes but no labels or teaching use [122]. The supplied St Patrick’s record adds only a New York church/venue, not Williams activity [132]; Tonooka records here do not verify a Williams teaching link [120][130].

Duke University’s documented framework was a university artist-in-residence appointment in Durham, North Carolina: Mary Lou Williams co-taught a jazz-history course and taught there; one account dates Duke teaching to 1976, while other documentation places the artist-in-residence period at 1977–1981 [64][44][118]. This classifies Duke as formal historical instruction joined to musical practice, making it central to Williams’s educator-historian role rather than merely a performance setting [116][44].

During the 1970s and through May 28, 1981, Mary Lou Williams’s later educational work—including teaching at the University of Massachusetts in Amherst in 1975–1977—treated teaching as preservation of African American jazz heritage: her approach emphasized oral learning, listening, memorization, improvisation, and learning by ear, and her purpose was to teach younger generations that heritage [65][34][54][117]. In the Fall of 1977, she carried the inspirational exchange of ideas from salons and bandstands into formal education, directly serving the purpose of treating her as both educator and historian [84][113].

Duke University’s connection to Mary Lou Williams was institutional and educational: she became Duke’s first artist-in-residence in 1977, taught jazz history, and directed the jazz ensemble through 1981 in Durham, North Carolina [47][67][98]. This linked historical instruction and ensemble practice to her educator-historian role, and Duke later credited the appointment with helping establish jazz as a serious part of its musical life [116].

Mary Lou Williams’s lecture-recitals are documented as a late-1970s educational-historical activity, linking her performance practice to her presentation of jazz history and therefore to her educator-historian role [33]. The supplied evidence does not establish the exact format, audience, or date of an individual lecture-recital, so those particulars should remain unresolved.

The additional universities and teaching venues cannot be treated as a verified teaching roster: the evidence includes a 1973 venue/date entry, but explicitly says that the source does not document Williams teaching at a university [62][77]. Their relation to Williams’s educator-historian role is therefore unestablished, and they should not be counted as confirmed later university instruction.

Sumi Tonooka’s private study with Mary Lou Williams illustrates a practice-based, historical method: at nineteen, Tonooka traveled from Philadelphia to Williams’s Harlem apartment, was required to understand jazz history before playing, and learned through listening and a spiritual relationship with the music [70][114][102][92][113]. The study was brief and pivotal, but its date is not supplied, so it illustrates Williams’s transmission of practice without proving that the lessons fell within the requested period [115][97].

The non-Duke university-and-venue listing is anchored in 1977, with entries dated September 6–29, December 10, and 1978, but the available evidence does not establish that these activities were university teaching [61][33][77]. It therefore cannot be used as confirmed additional university teaching for Mary Lou Williams’s educator-historian role.

## Jazz Tree Analysis

### The central lineage and the diagram’s architecture

**The Tree’s developmental line is clear.** The closest source-grounded reconstruction is:

**Suffering of slavery → spirituals → ragtime → K.C. swing → bop.**

Givan transcribes the four trunk terms as “spirituals,” “ragtime,” “K.C. [Kansas City] swing,” and “bop.” O’Neill’s contemporary description explains the underlying suffering as that of enslaved people producing the first spirituals. [2][4]

**Four eras rise from a foundation.** “Suffering” is not a fifth musical style. It is the historical and human condition from which the roots grow. Spirituals begin the sequence of musical eras; ragtime, Kansas City swing, and bop follow. This distinction preserves both the Tree’s architecture and Williams’s terminology.

**Blues provides continuity across the eras.** O’Brien’s Smithsonian essay gives the most detailed prose description of its movement: blues originates in the roots and runs up both sides of the trunk. On the right it ascends into the leafy branches; on the left it is interrupted by chopped-off branches. Givan likewise notes the repeated word “blues” along the trunk’s edges. [18][2]

| Component | Reported wording or content | Position | Historical function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground and roots | “SUFFERING” | Below and at the tree’s base | Foundational African American experience |
| First trunk era | “spirituals” | Lowest named era | Immediate musical origin |
| Second trunk era | “ragtime” | Above spirituals | Subsequent developmental stage |
| Third trunk era | “K.C. swing” | Above ragtime | Kansas City’s creative era |
| Fourth trunk era | “bop” | Upper trunk | Latest central era in this scheme |
| Trunk edges | Repeated “blues” | Rising from the roots | Continuing expressive foundation |
| Living branches and leaves | Musicians’ names | Flourishing upper growth | Individual creative exemplars |
| Bare or cut branches | Rejected styles and practices | Side growth | Williams’s judgments of failed continuation |

The structural details combine [18][2], and O’Neill’s contemporary account [4].

**This is more than a timeline.** A timeline places events beside dates. Williams’s Tree assigns different historical jobs to suffering, musical eras, expressive continuity, individual musicians, and rejected directions. Its form implies that creative growth requires nourishment from a shared root system.

**Its omissions are also selective.** Gospel, boogie-woogie, stride, and avant-garde examples appear in her historical performances, but they do not therefore become additional trunk stages. The four-era diagram is a compressed historical thesis, not a list of everything she played or taught. Her broader performing vocabulary should be read alongside it, not silently inserted into it. [13][15]

### Provenance, dates, and source-specific descriptions

**The reproduced poster has a strong credit.** O’Brien’s 2010 Smithsonian Folkways essay identifies the poster as a drawing made to Williams’s specifications by David Stone Martin. Its accompanying caption explicitly calls it the “Jazz Tree” and credits Williams and Martin, 1977. This is stronger identification than a caption that merely names the two collaborators without describing the object. [18]

**The documentary references nevertheless differ.** Givan also identifies Martin and 1977, while the Rutgers collection’s biographical narrative names artist David Sloan Wilson as Williams’s collaborator in developing a jazz tree during the late 1970s. These should remain separate source claims, not be converted into an invented joint collaboration or an alternative name for Martin. [2][6]

|---|---|---|---|

**The archival citation deserves precise handling.** Shirk identifies a *History of Jazz Tree Drawing* in the Mary Lou Williams Collection, Series 5, Box 2, in an unnumbered folder labelled “History of Jazz Tree 1977.” Rutgers also catalogues *History of Jazz Handwritten notes, circa 1977–1978*, currently described as Box 502, Folder 24. A dated drawing reference and a notes file are different documentary objects; the shared subject does not make them interchangeable. [19][21]

**Source dates are not version counts.** A 1977 drawing, a description published in 1979, and a reproduction captioned 1979 do not automatically prove three designs. They establish a sequence of references to the Tree. Its central argument remains remarkably consistent across the descriptions, while the grouping and wording of rejected branches vary.

### Roots, living branches, and named leaves

**The roots place suffering before style.** O’Brien describes them descending into African American human experience, named “SUFFERING.” O’Neill specifies the suffering of slaves. The diagram consequently makes jazz’s history inseparable from the conditions under which Black Americans created and transmitted music; its starting point is not simply an instrumental innovation or an entertainment market. [18][4]

**The leaves represent people rather than periods.** O’Neill names six musicians among the Tree’s leaves. Their function differs from that of “ragtime” or “bop”: they embody achievement within the living inheritance instead of adding another step to the trunk.

| Named leaf | Identification | Meaning within the description |
|---|---|---|
| Ellington | Duke Ellington | A jazz great within the living growth |
| Fats Waller | Pianist and composer | A named creative exemplar |
| J. P. Johnson | James P. Johnson | A named creative exemplar |
| John Coltrane | Saxophonist | An experimental musician included in the inheritance |
| Charlie Parker | Saxophonist | A named creative exemplar |
| Miles Davis | Trumpeter | A modern musician included in the inheritance |

All six are individually named in O’Neill’s 1979 description [4]; Ellington, Parker, and Waller also appear in the later Angelus account [22].

**Leaf placement is recognition, not blanket endorsement.** Coltrane’s inclusion is especially important because Williams criticized “avant-garde” music elsewhere on the Tree. Davis’s inclusion matters because she also criticized aspects of his contemporary presentation. The diagram therefore allows a musician to belong to jazz’s creative inheritance without making every stylistic association or career decision exemplary.

**The flourishing blues explains their connection.** O’Brien describes the leaves as musicians in whose music the blues flourishes. That interpretation makes the Tree’s living side an account of continued nourishment rather than a mere collection of celebrities. Historical belonging involves an expressive relationship to the roots, not simply chronological placement near the top. [18]

### The dead-end labels and their stated rationale

**Givan transcribes five rejected labels.** His account of the 1977 drawing names “commercial rock,” “black magic,” “avant-garde,” “cults,” and “exercises-classical books.” These are five reported labels on bare branches; the count should not be mistaken for a precise botanical count of every limb and twig in the drawing. [2]

| Label in Givan’s transcription | Williams’s related explanation | How to understand the judgment |
|---|---|---|
| “commercial rock” | Commercial dominance and loss of feeling | A criticism of cultural displacement and musical influence |
| “black magic” | Grouped among forces threatening jazz | Her spiritual and polemical vocabulary |
| “avant-garde” | “Cold” playing; schooling displacing feeling | Rejection of particular musical and educational tendencies |
| “cults” | Included in the collective warning | A broad danger category, not a named musical school |
| “exercises-classical books” | Technique without feeling becomes mechanical | A criticism of instruction treated as sufficient for creativity |

The labels and collective explanation come from Givan [2]; the contextual explanations of commercialism and mechanical technique appear in the Keystone interview [10] and O’Neill’s profile [4].

**Her explicit explanation was collective.** Asked about the negative symbolism, Williams said that these things had “almost destroyed jazz” and that something should have grown out of bebop but had not. That statement gives the branches a common rationale: they represented, for her, failed or damaging alternatives to the next organically developing jazz era. [2]

**Commercial rock received a developed criticism.** Williams believed that its commercial saturation displaced jazz and shaped children’s musical expectations before their own creativity could develop. In the Keystone interview, however, she explicitly allowed that some rock was good. Her sharpest target was the “slop” dominating television and radio, not every recording that could be classified as rock. [10]

**Avant-garde criticism combined several objections.** She attacked coldness, formalized learning without feeling, and the assumption that playing “far out” made a musician superior. These objections concern sound, educational formation, and personal attitude respectively. They overlap, but they are not identical—and none should be treated as a complete description of every experimental player. [2][10]

**Exercises and books threatened substitution.** Williams’s typewriter analogy explains why instructional materials could appear on a dead branch: a player might acquire patterns while bypassing the ear, historical experience, and expressive judgment that made those patterns meaningful. Connecting the label with that teaching criticism is a contextual interpretation; her quoted explanation of the Tree itself groups the dangers together. [4]

**“Cults” and “black magic” remain her terms.** They belong to Williams’s religiously charged criticism of musical practices she associated with confusion, empty exercises, or loss of jazz feeling. Her statements do not justify assigning those labels to a particular named religion or assuming that she had supplied a separate doctrinal argument for each branch. The historically responsible reading preserves the collective rationale without inventing individual targets. [2]

### Differences among the branch descriptions

**Other accounts preserve different groupings.** O’Brien describes chopped-off sticks bearing “cults and black magic,” “mere exercises,” and “classical books.” O’Neill describes avant-garde music, cult music, and performance-technique books on one dead limb, with commercial rock occupying a bare branch of its own. These formulations should remain visible alongside Givan’s transcription.

| Account | Reported wording | Reported grouping |
|---|---|---|
| O’Brien | “cults and black magic” | Words on chopped-off growth |
| O’Brien | “mere exercises” | Words on chopped-off growth |
| O’Brien | “classical books” | Words on chopped-off growth |
| O’Neill | Avant-garde music | Part of one dead limb |
| O’Neill | Cult music | Part of the same dead limb |
| O’Neill | Books teaching performance technique | Part of the same dead limb |
| O’Neill | Commercial rock | A separate bare branch |

Sources: O’Brien’s Smithsonian essay [18] and O’Neill’s 1979 profile [4].

**“Free music” appears in the explanation.** O’Neill quotes Williams saying that commercial rock and what musicians called “free music” had taken feeling out of the music after bop. In that account, “free music” belongs to her spoken rationale; it should not automatically be added as another separately transcribed inscription on the drawing. [4]

**The central issue was interrupted creation.** Williams believed that creative musicians needed to hear great music in order to create something new. Her fear was not simply that audiences would prefer a different genre. It was that a broken chain of listening and learning would prevent the emergence of new creators with the depth of earlier generations. [4]

**“Dead end” is an evaluative diagnosis.** It expresses what Williams thought deserved to continue the central jazz lineage, not a neutral finding that the condemned music ceased developing or attracting audiences. The Tree is consequently most useful as a compact statement of her historical convictions—and of the boundaries she drew around legitimate renewal.

## Jazz Historiography

### Jazz’s origin: suffering, spirituals, and Black American experience

**Williams located jazz’s immediate origin in spirituals.** In the narration recorded in late 1970, she stated: “From suffering came the spirituals. The main origin of American Jazz is a spiritual.” She connected Black Americans’ religious background with rhythms capable of expressing inward experience. Her history therefore began with the transformation of suffering into expressive life. [13]

**This was a sacred historical explanation.** Williams was not merely saying that musicians sometimes borrowed church melodies. Spirituals supplied a foundational relationship among suffering, religious feeling, rhythm, and expression. Jazz inherited that relationship even when its repertoire was secular and its performance setting was a nightclub rather than a church.

**Her Catholicism did not erase earlier roots.** Her late-career sacred compositions arose from her own religious commitments, but the origin story reached back to enslaved Black Americans and the spirituals. It would reverse her chronology to make Catholic liturgy the source of jazz. The Mass was a later setting in which she believed jazz’s already sacred inheritance could be recognized. [23][24]

**Suffering did not mean permanent sadness.** Her historical vocabulary included joy, sorrow, love, charity, and survival. In the 1972 interview published in *Coda*, she explained that Black Americans had been badly mistreated but that God had given jazz to everybody as an expression of love. She joined a particular historical origin to a universal human purpose. [16]

**That distinction prevents a false contradiction.** Asserting jazz’s Black American origins did not require Williams to say that only Black people could play it. Conversely, welcoming players and listeners of different backgrounds did not require erasing the people whose experience generated the music. Her position held historical specificity and universal participation together.

### Spirituals and blues: origin and continuing expressive foundation

**Spirituals and blues performed different historical jobs.** Spirituals occupied the explicit origin position in her narration and Tree. Blues supplied a persistent expressive and musical continuity. The Tree’s blues labels running from the roots up the trunk make this distinction unusually clear: blues is not exhausted by one historical period. [13][18]

**Blues was more than unhappiness.** In album notes quoted in O’Neill’s profile, Williams called it a special music expressing everyday experience, rising above racism and bitterness, and showing heroism. That formulation makes the blues an achievement in responding to life rather than a passive record of suffering. [4]

**Healing and love belonged inside it.** O’Brien recalled Williams identifying blues with healing, love, and “the spiritual feeling” in jazz. This interpretation refused a rigid separation between sacred feeling and secular musical subjects. A blues performance could carry spiritual depth without being a hymn or setting a religious text. [18]

**Musical development could enrich that foundation.** In *The History of Jazz*, Williams explained that older blues acquired a new appearance through bop-era chords, richness, and technique. She therefore recognized real change in musical resources without treating change as the abandonment of blues. This is the mechanism her organic historical model most readily approves: new vocabulary deepens a continuing expressive foundation. [13]

**Her teaching applied the same distinction.** Returning students from a coldly sung blues to spirituals was not necessarily a claim that the two genres were technically identical. It was a way of recovering what she thought connected them: inward feeling, vocal expression, and an understanding of the historical experience behind the sound. The pedagogical connection was expressive before it was taxonomic. [4]

### Good jazz: feeling, conversation, improvisation, and service

**Her definition emphasized relationships in performance.** Williams described spiritual feeling, deep conversation, and “mental telepathy” among bass, drums, and soloists as permanent characteristics of good jazz. The language points toward responsive ensemble playing: musicians hear one another, answer, and shape the unfolding performance together. [13]

**Improvisation required mind and heart together.** She described ideas passing from the mind through the heart to the fingertips. When the mind stopped, a player fell back on patterns; when the heart failed to participate, feeling disappeared. Her distinction was not simply between prepared and spontaneous notes. It was between living musical thought and execution that continued after thought or feeling had become disengaged. [13]

**Accompaniment carried an ethical obligation.** In the Keystone interview, she said that if she failed to accompany another musician properly, that player would struggle to create—and she would not feel like taking a solo herself. This connects musical generosity with artistic quality. Good jazz was not a sequence of individuals displaying themselves over one another. [10]

**Listeners also had an active role.** Williams asked audiences to listen with their ears and hearts. The therapeutic or healing language in her teaching concerned this attentive encounter with feeling and musical exchange. It was a statement of artistic and spiritual purpose, not simply an advertising description of relaxing music. [18]

**Her historical standard was consequently demanding.** Technical complexity, stylistic novelty, and public success could all coexist with expressive failure. Equally, a familiar blues or an older style could remain creative if the performance contained thought, responsiveness, and feeling. Chronological newness alone did not determine historical value.

### Ragtime, Kansas City swing, and bop

**Ragtime belonged to her valued inheritance.** Williams repeatedly placed it among the four great eras and taught it through music learned from her mother. Its historical standing was affirmative: it belonged inside the main developmental trunk and deserved to be transmitted. That conclusion concerns its place in her history, rather than assigning it the superlative aesthetic status she explicitly gave Kansas City swing. [19][13]

**Kansas City received unmistakable direct praise.** In her recorded narration, she called the Kansas City swing era associated with 1929 “the greatest Jazz era of them all.” In the 1972 *Coda* interview, she called Kansas City “the most important era in the music.” These were personal historical judgments, rooted in her own experience as a pianist, composer, and arranger. [13][16]

| Style or period | Place in her history | Clearest evaluative emphasis | Reason or function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ragtime | Core trunk era | Part of the “four great eras” | Family transmission and historical foundation |
| Kansas City swing | Core trunk era | “Greatest” and “most important” era | Creativity, trained musicians, sharing, and listening |
| Bop | Uppermost central era | Major creative achievement | New techniques growing from earlier musicianship |
| Post-bop succession | Contested continuation | An expected development had failed | Perceived loss of roots and feeling |

Sources: Williams’s narration [13], *Coda*[16], Givan [2], and Shirk [19].

**Her Kansas City praise was social as well as musical.** She remembered Depression-era musicians sharing what they had, staying out late, and jamming through the night. One recollection described returning after changing clothes to find musicians still developing “Sweet Georgia Brown,” with their collective playing sounding like a substantial arrangement. The scene’s value lay in sustained participation and exchange. [16]

**Training meant apprenticeship through musical life.** Williams praised earlier musicians as well trained, even while criticizing later musicians for relying too heavily on school. These positions become intelligible when “training” includes bandstand discipline, close listening, arranging, repetition, and older musicians correcting younger ones. She was distinguishing educational environments and priorities, not knowledge from ignorance.

**Bop grew through intergenerational contact.** Williams’s narration names Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, Bud Powell, Art Blakey, Max Roach, and Charlie Parker as great musicians emerging from the Kansas City swing era. Her account treats an era as a formative musical environment; the list should not be read as a claim that every named musician was born in Kansas City or followed the same geographical route. [13]

**Her historical mechanism was listening forward.** Younger musicians listened to older musicians and developed new techniques and ideas. Bop thus exemplified the process she wanted education to renew: innovation arising from deep contact with predecessors. The Tree’s impasse after bop meant, in her judgment, that this process had been interrupted—not that creative change itself was undesirable.

### A practitioner’s history and its internal tensions

**Her authority combined inheritance and participation.** Williams’s statement that she had “played through” jazz’s eras joined childhood knowledge of older music with direct participation in swing, bop, and later developments. It should not be taken literally to mean that she witnessed the historical birth of spirituals. Her claim concerned command of musical languages and involvement in their transmission and transformation. [25]

**Her own compositions made the case audible.** Instead of telling history only through other people’s celebrated recordings, she could move among historical languages through her own repertoire. This placed a woman composer and arranger inside the account of how jazz changed, rather than leaving her outside as a commentator on a predominantly male succession.

**Kernodle emphasizes that revisionist achievement.** Tammy L. Kernodle interprets Williams’s late-career performances, lectures, and recordings as challenges to exclusionary jazz narratives. In the NPR account, Kernodle connects Williams’s assertion of jazz’s Blackness with the contemporary formation of Black studies, women’s studies, and gender studies in universities. This is Kernodle’s interpretation of Williams’s historical significance, not terminology Williams herself necessarily used. [26][24]

**Givan emphasizes her restrictive boundaries.** His analysis places considerable weight on the formal and expressive practices Williams expected jazz to retain, contrasting her position with Cecil Taylor’s broader cultural definition. This perspective explains how a historically adventurous musician could reject substantial portions of subsequent musical development. [2]

**The interpretations address different questions.** Williams could be progressive in restoring Black history and women’s creative authority while remaining restrictive about which new musical directions deserved recognition as jazz’s next central era. Neither description alone captures the whole position. Her history challenged exclusions even as it established boundaries of its own.

**Her final composition complicates a rigid reading.** Kelley describes *History…: A Wind Symphony* as a “sonic tableau” connecting styles, not necessarily in chronological order. Its surviving section labels and sketches show history becoming an expressive composition rather than remaining only a linear chart. These are headings in an unfinished score, not additional inscriptions on the Jazz Tree. [17]

| Score section | Detail described by Kelley | Kelley’s interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| “Suffering” | Dense, dissonant polyphonic opening | Collective human trauma |
| “Spiritual” | Direct musical language | Collective reconstitution |
| “Blues” | Blues references and reconstruction concerns | Bonding, healing, and love |
| “Ragtime” | Sixteen bars for clarinet melody and harmony | Recreational joy amid adversity |
| “Gospel” | Uplifting finale | Catharsis and unity |

**The musical history remained larger than the chart.** The Tree concentrated her developmental argument, but her performances and final score could juxtapose, revisit, and transform historical materials. Her historiography is best understood as an effort to make renewal accountable to memory—not as a claim that the past should simply be repeated.

Several other retained pages add only evidentiary limits: a June 27, 2019 public post calls Williams a jazz pianist and composer and says only “Williams taught…” [35]; March 20 and April 14, 2024 Duke Chapel items document later presentations rather than Williams’s own activities [134][138]; a May 8, 2020 DC Jazz Festival page names a performance but supplies no transcript [135]; and a July 26, 2024 interview records Sumi Tonooka’s account of her own improvisation, not Williams’s historical views [170].

## Evaluations of Specific Styles and Musicians

### The comparative picture

**Her judgments combined openness and exclusion.** Williams could praise an individual experimental musician while criticizing the movement associated with that musician. She could also perform unfamiliar music while doubting that it represented jazz’s healthy historical continuation. The useful comparison therefore asks what she valued in each case, rather than assigning every name a permanent place in an approving or disapproving camp.

| Subject | Documented position | Principal criterion | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avant-garde jazz | Strong criticism alongside selective openness | Feeling, roots, communication, and humility | Interviews and Givan’s synthesis |
| John Coltrane | Admiration and historical recognition | Innovation grounded in older music | Givan, Dahl, and teaching remarks |
| Eric Dolphy | Admiration | An accepted experimental musician | Givan’s scholarly account |
| Cecil Taylor | Admiration, collaboration, and sharp criticism | Talent versus incompatible musical expectations | Statements, letters, and concert analysis |
| Miles Davis | Criticism of contemporary presentation and conduct | Substance, lineage, and responsibility to colleagues | 4 July 1972 interview |
| Stan Getz | Qualified praise within a critical comparison | Tone and harmonic playing | Same 1972 interview |
| Dave Brubeck | Criteria-based comparison, not an attributed personal verdict | Classical influence alone decides nothing | Brubeck’s profile and Williams’s stated principles |

The principal sources are Givan [2], Linda Dahl’s biographical excerpt [27], the Keystone interview [10], *Coda*[16], and the Smithsonian’s Brubeck profile [28].

### Avant-garde jazz: real hostility, selective acceptance

**Her hostility should not be softened away.** Williams described the avant-garde as cold, placed “avant-garde” among the Tree’s dead labels, and sometimes questioned whether Taylor was playing jazz at all. She also attacked empty exercises and the displacement of feeling by schooling. These were substantive aesthetic objections, not merely a preference for familiar repertoire. [2]

**Her rhetoric could become intensely polemical.** Statements associated with the 1977 Taylor encounter linked avant-garde practices with hate, confusion, “black magic,” and exercises derived from European composers. Such language reveals the force of her religious and cultural anxieties; it does not itself establish the artistic worth or motivations of the musicians she criticized. [2]

**She also explicitly defended stylistic breadth.** In the Keystone interview, she said, “I play all styles, everybody should. It’s all great.” Her next objection was to avant-garde musicians who assumed that being farther out made them greater than other players. Here the immediate target was superiority and self-display rather than the mere existence of experimental music. [10]

**These positions form a genuine tension.** The generous statement does not cancel the dead branches, and the dead branches do not erase her experimental practice. Williams appears to have distinguished the possibility of valuable music in many styles from her judgment that particular post-bop tendencies had failed to sustain jazz’s central creative inheritance.

**Her own music supplies the counterexample.** Givan describes the early recorded “A Fungus A Mungus” as beginning with an eleven-note unmetered phrase containing nine pitch classes, followed by dense dissonant playing. Although that recording belongs to the early 1960s, the composition remained relevant to her later historical presentation. Dissonance and rhythmic freedom were resources she could use herself. [2]

**The composition also carried a warning.** In her late-1970 narration, Williams introduced it as music anticipating sounds that would threaten Black roots and heritage. O’Neill’s account identifies it as an avant-garde or free-jazz example in *The History of Jazz*. The piece could therefore demonstrate a sound world while commenting critically on its historical implications. [13][4]

**Late performance continued that exploration.** The Montreux review describes “Concerto Alone at Montreux,” approximately six and a half minutes long, as a personal exploration of freer expression. The reviewer hears atmosphere and blues-related suggestions rather than straightforward harmonic progression, while distinguishing the music from complete atonality. This is the reviewer’s musical description, not a newly attributed statement by Williams. [15]

**The deciding standard was expressive continuity.** Her practice supports a narrower conclusion than “she opposed modern music”: she opposed experimentation when she heard it as cold, rootless, mechanically acquired, or indifferent to other musicians. Nevertheless, her broad condemnations sometimes reached beyond that conditional standard, which is part of the complexity of her late-career position.

### John Coltrane and Eric Dolphy

**Coltrane was an admired experimental musician.** Givan explicitly identifies Williams as a genuine admirer of Coltrane and Dolphy. Coltrane also appears among the named leaves in O’Neill’s description of the Tree. These two forms of recognition—scholarly testimony about admiration and inclusion in her teaching image—place him within her valued inheritance. [2][4]

**Dahl supplies a useful explanatory distinction.** Her biographical account interprets Williams’s acceptance of late Coltrane and aspects of Taylor’s work in terms of their ability to play “within the tradition.” In this reading, their experimental language did not result simply from an inability to command earlier musical practices. That is Dahl’s explanation of Williams’s position, rather than an extended direct review by Williams of a particular Coltrane recording. [27]

**Williams’s teaching remarks reinforce that interpretation.** She warned that young players patterned after Coltrane would not become “a Coltrane” without knowing older music such as Basie. The point was not that they should abandon Coltrane. It was that copying the sound of a historically developed artist could not substitute for developing comparable depth of musicianship. [10]

**Hilton Ruiz was her practical example.** Williams said she took Ruiz back to Fats Waller and worked on getting his left hand to swing; she then declared that he could play anything. The progression joined an older pianistic resource to modern freedom. Coltrane functioned in the same argument as an example of achievement that imitators could misunderstand by hearing only its most recent surface. [10]

**Her harmonic curiosity was not narrow.** In the 1972 interview, Williams recalled learning advanced sounds from Milton Orent during the 1940s and retrospectively compared those harmonies with Coltrane’s. The comparison demonstrates her interest in sophisticated harmonic resources. It should not be converted into a claim that Orent taught Coltrane or directly caused Coltrane’s later musical language. [16]

**Dolphy strengthens the case for selectivity.** Givan’s inclusion of him among musicians Williams admired is a significant counterexample to a blanket anti-avant-garde interpretation. The strongest judgment is recognition of an experimental artist she valued; assigning her a detailed preference among his instruments, compositions, or recordings would add a different kind of claim. [2]

**Coltrane’s importance was therefore double.** He was both a respected musician and a pedagogical example of why innovation requires historical depth; the comparison with his imitators explains Williams’s standards more clearly than a simple traditional-versus-modern classification.

### Dave Brubeck and the meaning of classical influence

**Brubeck calls for a narrower comparison.** His jazz-classical work provides a relevant point of contact with Williams’s criticism of “classical books,” but that contact is not itself her personal appraisal. The Smithsonian describes Brubeck’s sustained efforts to integrate jazz and classical music, including *Dialogues for Jazz Combo and Orchestra*, recorded with the New York Philharmonic under Leonard Bernstein in 1959. [28]

**Classical influence alone does not decide the issue.** Williams herself composed for larger concert forces, pursued advanced harmony, and valued what she learned from Orent. Her unfinished Duke work also brought a jazz-historical project into the wind-symphony setting. It would therefore misrepresent her criteria to turn the Tree’s criticism of books and exercises into automatic opposition to every musician associated with classical composition. [16][17]

**The supported comparison concerns musical priorities.** Applied analytically—not attributed as a Brubeck verdict—Williams’s questions would concern feeling, swing, responsiveness, historical grounding, and whether technique served expression. A composition’s concert-hall setting, written score, or classical connection would answer none of those questions by itself.

**Nor does race supply an answer.** Williams’s 1972 interview explicitly welcomed jazz as an art for everyone and praised Zoot Sims’s soulfulness. Her insistence on Black American origins therefore cannot be used to manufacture an unfavorable judgment of Brubeck as a white musician. The distinction is between her stated standards and an individual opinion requiring its own attribution. [16]

**The resulting judgment is deliberately bounded.** Brubeck’s classical crossover offers a useful comparison with Williams’s aesthetic principles, but neither that crossover nor her general complaints about schooling should be presented as something she specifically praised or condemned in him.

### Cecil Taylor: admiration, historical ambition, and conflict

**Taylor produced her most revealing encounter.** The Carnegie Hall performance took place on 17 April 1977 and appeared as the album *Embraced* in 1978. Oxford places an initial approach in 1976, while Givan documents planning during that year and reconstructs Williams’s eventual proposal following Taylor’s visits to hear her. The preparation and performance dates describe different stages of the project. [2][9]

**She began with genuine positive expectations.** Before the concert, Williams called Taylor honest and sincere and described answering what he played during rehearsals. Her willingness to organize the event is itself evidence of engagement: she did not treat him merely as an opponent whose music should remain outside her experience. [2]

**Her purpose was explicitly historical.** In the concert program, she argued that a new jazz era was overdue and hoped that returning to the music’s history could produce something unexpected from the modern era. The concert was consequently an attempt to address the very impasse pictured by the Tree. Tradition was supposed to generate a future, not simply defeat an avant-garde rival. [2]

**The musicians understood that return differently.** Williams’s history depended substantially on recognizable musical languages: spirituals, blues, swing, boogie, and bop. Taylor could understand the inheritance as a source of cultural energy and creative principles without reproducing its metrical or harmonic conventions. Both could claim continuity while disagreeing over what continuity had to sound like. [2]

**Their preparations exposed the difference.** Williams wrote parts, bass lines, chords, and short figures for Taylor. He was reluctant to treat notation as a directive controlling the performance; his later prose about the project emphasized a generative core within the inherited material. For Williams, the historical examples supplied a shared musical basis. For Taylor, they could stimulate a response that remained recognizably his own. [2]

**The conflict also qualifies her anti-school rhetoric.** Here Williams herself used notation and organized rehearsal material to communicate historical knowledge. Taylor, despite conservatory training, resisted dependence on written instructions. Their encounter cannot be adequately explained as an intuitive elder confronting a musician who merely followed academic exercises.

**The principal musical contrast was audible.** Givan describes Williams generally playing swing-based tonal compositions while Taylor contributed unmetered figures, modal or post-tonal clusters, and different textural densities. John S. Wilson’s contemporary review described them as separated by different musical concepts. This was not simply a disagreement over which tunes to play; it concerned how musical events were organized and how partners should respond. [2]

**Interaction nevertheless occurred within the friction.** Givan identifies passages where Taylor followed aspects of Williams’s pitch material, including bass movement in “Fandangle.” In “Good Ole Boogie,” he briefly adopted a more concordant rhythmic and harmonic approach; Williams can be heard laughing appreciatively. These moments complicate accounts that describe the entire concert as two wholly unrelated performances. [2]

**Other passages frustrated her expectations sharply.** During “The Blues Never Left Me,” Taylor’s intense clusters contrasted with Williams’s slower, more introspective playing. The bassist Bob Cranshaw and drummer Mickey Roker, added late in the preparation, introduced further differences over structure and responsiveness. Givan also documents limited attempts by both pianists to adapt on compositions written by the other. [2]

**Williams’s own criticism was explicit.** In a letter Givan dates 29 July 1977, she accused Taylor of producing “monotony, corruption, and noise,” while asking why he would damage his great talent through clowning. The simultaneous recognition of talent and rejection of the performance is essential: her complaint was not simply that he lacked ability. [2]

**Her later public comments were more varied.** She sometimes spoke diplomatically about the encounter and told Val Wilmer that repeated listening made the music seem to add and flow together. Elsewhere, she recalled being shocked and feeling that Taylor was not allowing her room. These statements show changing emphases and contexts, rather than one final verdict neatly replacing every earlier response. [2]

**Taylor also claimed the historical inheritance.** He argued that succeeding generations possessed the organizing principles left by earlier musicians and could honor them on their own terms. That offers a serious alternative to Williams’s expectations: inherited culture might support radical transformation without preserving a stable set of audible conventions. [2]

**The concert tested her educational model.** Williams expected historical grounding to make a productive encounter possible. The result showed that knowledge of predecessors and mutual admiration did not guarantee agreement about meter, harmony, accompaniment, or creative authority. The bounded conclusion is not that historical education failed, but that shared history alone could not settle how two strong improvisers should use it.

### Miles Davis and Stan Getz

**The Davis criticism has a specific setting.** Williams spoke on 4 July 1972 in an interview later published in the June–July 1974 issue of *Coda*. Her remarks responded partly to press comments attributed to Davis about other performers. She acknowledged that she had heard about those comments rather than read them herself, making her response evidence of her own values rather than a secure quotation of Davis’s position. [16]

**She challenged the appearance of musical novelty.** Williams said Davis still played substantially the style he had previously played, while modern “guys who make the noise” stood behind him. Her distinction separated the soloist’s continuing language from the contemporary backing around it. The comment criticizes presentation and accompaniment; it should not be enlarged into a comprehensive assessment of every phase of his work. [16]

**Her Getz comparison preserved a compliment.** She described Getz in a similar modern setting but said he still played beautifully, directing attention to his tone and chord changes. For her, a player’s musical substance could survive within surroundings she disliked. This is a more discriminating judgment than rejecting an entire performance solely because of its contemporary instrumentation or association. [16]

**Responsibility to colleagues also mattered.** Williams objected to public attacks that might cost musicians work and defended Gillespie and Sarah Vaughan. She connected selfishness and bitterness with failures of musical character. In the same conversation, she remembered Davis more warmly in earlier years and discussed the damaging experience of his being beaten by police. Her criticism included personal history and concern, not just stylistic dislike. [16]

**Davis’s Tree leaf supplies a counterweight.** His inclusion in the living inheritance in O’Neill’s 1979 account shows why the 1972 remarks should not become a total exclusion from her history. Canonical recognition, criticism of a current sound, and objection to professional behavior could coexist. [4]

**Waller received especially vivid praise.** Williams recalled his ability to create a composition immediately in response to an idea and called him “the most terrific composer in the world.” The superlative expresses her admiration, while the anecdote identifies its reason: spontaneous invention supported by comprehensive musicianship. Her repeated recommendation that students return to Waller was therefore more than nostalgia. [16]

**Gillespie exemplified her ethical ideal.** Williams praised him not only for musical greatness but for helping poor people, getting young people into school, and teaching in his basement. She also admired James Moody’s breadth and generosity. These remarks show that her preferred musician was someone who could play widely and sustain the community around the music. [16]

**Parker complicated any simple moral formula.** When asked whether Parker was always a sympathetic person, Williams defended his character while acknowledging his self-destructive difficulties and the obstacles those created for teaching. Her response distinguished personal kindness, artistic achievement, and the capacity to mentor. Even her strongly ethical aesthetic did not collapse every aspect of a musician’s life into one judgment. [16]

**Aretha Franklin and James Brown supplied exceptions.** Daphne A. Brooks reports a letter to Phyl Garland, associated with Garland’s work on a 1977 feature, in which Williams complained that youngsters had lost their heritage through rock, avant-garde music, and “black magic exercises.” In the same passage, she called Aretha and James Brown “the only real mccoys.” The letter’s praise and criticism belong together. [29]

**Those exceptions resist a genre-only reading.** Williams could recognize authenticity in prominent popular musicians while condemning commercial saturation. Popular success was therefore not by itself disqualifying, just as experimental association was not by itself disqualifying for Coltrane or Dolphy. Her recurring concern was whether feeling, creative individuality, and a meaningful relationship to Black musical inheritance remained active.

**The evidence requires several distinctions.** Event dates differ from release and publication dates; Tree labels above retain their source-specific wording, and its named leaves are identified examples rather than an estimated total. The exact September 1977 lecture content and a comprehensive campus chronology turn on the lecture recording, appointment records, and dated itineraries; further leaf placement and the relationship between 1977 and 1979 Tree references turn on the drawings themselves. A personal Brubeck verdict rests on a dated Williams statement naming him. The Keystone excerpt is headed 1978, although one reproduction’s URL contains 1977, so it is best treated as late-1970s testimony. No numerical estimates or measured claims about educational or therapeutic outcomes are introduced here. [5][30][31]

**Her lasting principle joined memory and invention.** Williams’s late-career educational work sought neither a museum of finished styles nor novelty detached from history. She wanted musicians and listeners to recover the feeling, discipline, responsiveness, and communal purpose through which another creative era might become possible. Her judgments could be severe and contradictory, but the educational ambition remained consistent: knowing the roots should enlarge what musicians could create.

**Attribution limits and competing explanations**

The surrounding evidence demonstrates why a contextual mention must not be converted into Williams’s opinion. In a **June 18, 1964** *Down Beat* Blindfold Test, it was **Miles Davis**, not Williams, who made “derisive and dismissive remarks” about Eric Dolphy and Cecil Taylor while offering favorable but cautious comments about Ornette Coleman [253]. **Context and evidence limits**

- **Avant-garde/free jazz.** “Free jazz” denotes avant-garde jazz of the **1960s** and experimental practices before and after it. Definitions describe departures from tonal harmony, meter, form, instrumentation, timbre, technique, and ensemble roles, or emphasize experimentation, collaboration, improvisation, and spirituality; performance still requires skill and communication to choose constraints and develop form and content. [236] Ornette Coleman’s *Free Jazz* appeared in **1961** (37 minutes; piano-less double quartet; high-energy, dissonant group improvisation). Bill Dixon’s October Revolution (**1964**, West 91st Street, New York) lasted **four days**, featured **around 75 musicians**, drew critical attention, and demonstrated a formal-arts audience. In the **1970s**, Manhattan lofts including Studio Rivbea supported presentation; some musicians found university jobs, and European institutions were more supportive. Reported legacies include dissonance, atonality, extended techniques, noise, open-ended collective improvisation, artist-run cooperatives, openness, self-determination, and community. None is a Williams evaluation. [236]

- **John Coltrane.** *Both Directions at Once* documents a **1963** classic-quartet session released later; *A Love Supreme* is a **1965** spiritual-jazz masterpiece, with a private suite recording captured in **October 1965**. [242] His **1965** *Ascension* included Archie Shepp, Pharoah Sanders, Marion Brown, and Tchicai; the account says Coltrane’s avant-garde turn gave the movement credibility and broadened its socio-aesthetic-spiritual terrain. A 1998 biography, “Moment’s Notice,” and a **March 7, 2024** item on Davis, Coltrane, and Bill Evans are context or research leads, not Williams evidence. [236][242][244]

- **Bebop, Dizzy Gillespie, and Thelonious Monk.** Gillespie’s Parker combos date to **1945**, while his big bands popularized bebop from **1947**. In a **1 January 1973, London** interview, he called the music evolutionary, “not necessarily revolution,” and said, “We’re dealing in spirit now in jazz.” He described clinics and spending “a week or so” on a college campus, assigning Minton’s the rhythmic and Monroe’s Uptown House the harmonic aspect. [254] The same retrospective account records Monk and Kenny Clarke at Minton’s and Monk exchanging ideas with Gillespie, but no Williams assessment; Gillespie’s educator role is not Williams evidence. [254]

- **Miles Davis.** A specialist study treats Davis’s late-1960s/early-1970s fusion studio and mid-1970s concerts as real-time composition and improvisation; rhythm-section cuts respond to ensemble improvisation and can push soloists toward new material, including movement from blues phrases toward tonally ambiguous, sonically experimental material. [244] Drum-machine use is qualified as likely beginning in the first half of **1973**, possibly influenced by Sly Stone, with an early recording on **12 April 1973** during a tour begun in March. The study’s technology analysis, including its warning that recordings can flatten or distort history, is not Williams’s reasoning; no Williams-Davis evaluation is documented. [244]

- **Bud Powell and Elmo Hope.** Hope and Powell were childhood friends who played and listened to jazz and classical music; Powell’s horn-like right hand over simple left-hand chords was reportedly worked on with Hope. Hope also worked with Clifford Brown, Coltrane, Lou Donaldson, Jackie McLean, Sonny Rollins, and Harold Land. Hope was chiefly associated with bebop and hard bop and died **19 May 1967**, outside the window. [255] His playing is described as individual, complex, and subtle, favoring variation over bebop virtuosity; Hope, Powell, and Monk were considered mutually influential early in their careers. An unnamed **1980** *Down Beat* writer called Hope and Herbie Nichols “practically categorized out of existence” but “prolifically creative and highly original.” The **2010** *Penguin Jazz Guide*, Hasaan Ibn Ali, and Johnny Griffin later praised Hope. These are external assessments, not Williams’s. [255]

The 1970 reissue and recordings dated 1953–1966 therefore provide historical comparison material, not evidence of a Williams evaluation. [256][257] |

The open roster is not exhaustive: the allowed evidence also names Cecil Taylor, Herbie Nichols, Sonny Rollins, and other musicians in performance or recording contexts, but does not thereby establish Williams-attributed evaluations. The deciding condition is direct attribution to Williams in a dated statement, artifact, or clearly identified report. General jazz-pedagogy criteria—individuality, freedom, experiential, exploratory, and collective learning—remain comparison frameworks rather than Williams’s documented criteria. [222] The Rutgers archive separately records Duke classroom activities, concerts, publications, and ephemera during Williams’s last years, but does not connect those materials to any particular evaluation. [63]

**Evidence status:** Coltrane—admired in 1960s context; no separate 1970–81 statement or reason [221][249]. Brubeck—no Williams stance; 1961–67/1990 records are by others [249]. Monk/Gillespie—mid-1940s mentoring and Harlem gathering, not late evaluations [221][258]; Gillespie’s 1976 report is not hers [249]. Miles—no Williams stance; Taylor’s criticism is Taylor’s [221]. Bud—gathering context only [221]. Elmo Hope (1923–1967)—outside the window, with no Williams link [258]. Bebop—mid-1940s affinity; “bop” on the trunk, but no growth after its era [221][258]. Additional supported subjects are Taylor, Dolphy, “commercial rock,” “black magic,” “cults,” and “exercises-classical books”; spirituals, ragtime, and K.C. swing are tree categories, not separate evaluations here [221]. The roster is evidence-bounded, not exhaustive; contextual reviews are not Williams statements [249].

| **Avant-garde/free jazz** | Williams criticized some musicians for not hearing chords or learning basic elements; although the music could be exciting, she said she did not really call it music.
