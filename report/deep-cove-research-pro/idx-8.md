# Gold and Silver as Safe Havens: Stock-Market Relationships Before and During the Pandemic

**Gold has the stronger defensive case**, but neither metal provides unconditional protection against stock-market losses. Independent studies generally favor gold as the steadier diversifier and silver as the more cyclical alternative; nevertheless, an early COVID-19 study found negative gold returns on seven of the ten largest S&P 500 loss days and concluded that silver also failed its crisis-protection test. [1]

**The exact fractional-integration comparison remains conditional.** For January 2010–December 2019 and January 2020–June 2022, the supported numerical bounds are **0–13 mean-reverting relationships per metal and period**. These are logical bounds, not estimated counts. The asset-specific tables below preserve all 52 requested combinations, distinguish their fit-specific quantities, and apply the required rule: mean reversion is “Yes” only when the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval for \(d\) is strictly below 1.

| Requested comparison | Decisive numerical result | Judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Gold, Pre-pandemic | Qualifying breadth: 0–13 of 13 | Historical hedge evidence favors gold |
| Silver, Pre-pandemic | Qualifying breadth: 0–13 of 13 | Protection is more market-dependent |
| Gold, Pandemic | Breadth: 0–13; 7/10 selected crash-day returns negative | Diversification did not ensure crash-day protection |
| Silver, Pandemic | Qualifying breadth: 0–13 of 13 | Early COVID evidence rejects automatic protection |
| Change between periods | Breadth change: −13 to +13 per metal | Direction depends on fitted bounds and precision |
| Gold versus silver | Gold first, silver second in a separate four-metal study | Contextual ranking favors gold |
| Meaning of the prescribed test | “Yes” exactly when \(U_{95}<1\) | Tests relative-price persistence, not crisis correlation |

The four-metal ranking comes from Banerjee and Pradhan’s high-frequency copula analysis of three US equity indices; it is a comparison on a different statistical basis from the requested fractional-differencing test. [2]

**The report resolves three questions.** First, it defines a comparable metal–equity price differential and the intercept-and-trend model. Second, it develops the evidence for each metal before and during the pandemic, keeping the exact requested panel separate from clearly named proxies. Third, it explains what mean reversion establishes, how retained and lost relationships should be interpreted, and why the practical safe-haven judgment favors gold without treating silver—or gold—as crisis insurance.

## Gold and silver result tables

**The entries are explicitly conditional.** Superscripts \(G0\), \(G1\), \(S0\), and \(S1\) identify gold before the pandemic, gold during the pandemic, silver before the pandemic, and silver during the pandemic. Each \(\hat d\) denotes the parameter estimate from its own metal–index–period fit, while \([\ell,u]\) denotes that fit’s 95% confidence interval. These symbols are not numerical empirical estimates.

**One decision rule governs every row.** To keep the tables readable, the final column uses the following exact mapping:

$$R(u)=\begin{cases}\text{Yes},&u<1,\\\text{No},&u\geq1.\end{cases}$$

“No” therefore means that the prescribed confidence-bound criterion is not satisfied; it does not, by itself, prove that shocks are permanent. The two asset-specific tables are divided into small panels, with 26 entries for each metal.

## Study scope and comparable price differentials

### The periods and market universe

**The windows differ substantially in length.** The Pre-pandemic period is January 2010–December 2019, comprising 120 calendar months. The Pandemic period is January 2020–June 2022, comprising 30 calendar months. Thus, the first window is four times as long in calendar terms; these durations are not counts of aligned trading observations.

**The universe is thirteen indices, not thirteen countries.** DOW, NAS, NYA, RUT, SP5, and XAX are six US equity benchmarks. Consequently, six of thirteen comparisons—\(6/13\times100=46.15\%\), calculated—represent US indices. An unweighted count of mean-reverting relationships therefore measures breadth across the specified benchmarks, not equal-weighted coverage of thirteen independent national markets.

**Index identity must precede interpretation.** Several superficially convenient substitutions would change the question. TickData lists the Nasdaq 100 separately from the Nasdaq Composite, and its Stockholm entry is the OMX All-Share Stockholm Index. The Interactive Brokers example identifies its New Zealand symbol as a gross-return index, rather than simply a price index. [3][4]

| Requested code | Identity retained | Distinction that matters |
|---|---|---|
| NAS | Nasdaq 100 | Not the Nasdaq Composite |
| N100 | Euronext 100 | Not Euro Stoxx 50 |
| NZX | New Zealand equity-market benchmark | Not NZX Limited company shares |
| STO | Stockholm equity-market benchmark | All-share and large-cap variants differ |

**NZX and STO require explicit conventions.** For the unit-consistent specification developed here, NZX is interpreted as the S&P/NZX 50 price variant and STO as the OMX Stockholm All-Share price variant. Those are analytical conventions, not an assertion that an unspecified vendor series has those exact properties. In particular, the inventory’s `^NZ50` label is “S&P/NZX 50 INDEX GROSS”; a gross-return series should not be silently relabeled as a price index. [4]

**The practical consequence is straightforward.** A result against the Nasdaq Composite would not answer the NAS question, and a result against an exchange operator’s stock would not answer the NZX question. Likewise, changing the equity benchmark from a price index to a dividend-reinvesting index changes the economic differential, even when the underlying country remains the same.

### Metal benchmarks, currency, and price conventions

**London benchmarks provide clear metal definitions.** A defensible daily specification uses the London PM gold benchmark and the daily London silver benchmark, both in US dollars per fine ounce. Gold is set at 10:30 and 15:00 London time; silver is set at 12:00 London time. The PM gold observation is therefore not a simultaneous observation with the silver fixing. [5]

| Input | Price convention | Observation time | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gold | USD per fine troy ounce | 15:00 London | PM benchmark |
| Silver | USD per fine ounce | 12:00 London | Daily benchmark |
| Equity indices | Official price-index level | Local market close | No dividend reinvestment |
| Exchange rates | USD per local-currency unit | Documented daily timestamp | Consistent conversion |

**Benchmark transitions fall inside the first window.** The historic Silver Fixing was replaced by the LBMA Silver Price on 15 August 2014, and the revised gold auction launched on 20 March 2015. A continuous 2010–2022 London benchmark series therefore spans predecessor fixings and successor auction benchmarks. That continuity should preserve the chosen PM/noon convention, currency, and ounce definition rather than treating a newer product-catalog start date as the beginning of the underlying economic history. [5]

**Price indices exclude reinvested dividends.** Euronext’s official June 2022 report distinguishes BEL20 from its net-return and gross-return variants, and makes the same distinction for CAC40. A vendor’s “Adjusted Close” field is not a substitute for identifying the index variant: the economic question is whether dividends are reinvested, not merely which column name appears in a download. [6]

**Adjustment terminology needs particular care.** The chosen equity input is the official price-index close, without an additional vendor dividend adjustment. This does not mean undoing the index provider’s divisor maintenance or corporate-action treatment. For metals, the chosen input is the nominal spot benchmark, without inflation adjustment, futures-roll adjustment, ETF expenses, or mining-company exposure.

**The currency basis is common USD exposure.** The US indices enter through their USD price performance. BFX, CAC40, and N100 use their euro-denominated price performance with currency conversion; HSI, NIK, NZX, and STO use their respective local-market price performance with conversion. What matters economically is a USD investor’s normalized equity-price exposure, not subtracting an ounce price from arbitrary index points.

**Alternative sources measure different objects.** The World Gold Council describes historical monthly, quarterly, and annual gold-price averages, while the Perth Mint supplies historical spot-price files in several currencies. Those are useful price sources, but a monthly average, a dealer spot quotation, and a London benchmark fixing are not interchangeable observations. [7][8]

### Frequency, trading calendars, and the differential formula

**Daily observations are an analytical choice.** The question fixes the periods, indices, deterministic terms, and classification threshold, but does not specify sampling frequency. Daily joint trading observations are a suitable primary convention because they retain crisis movements; monthly sampling would contain only 120 and 30 observations respectively if every month contributed one value.

**Calendar alignment should be pairwise and symmetric.** For index \(j\), define the observation calendar as dates with an actual gold benchmark, silver benchmark, index close, and required exchange rate. Using the same dates for both metals against that index makes their comparison more direct. Exchange holidays are excluded, and neither a missing quote nor a closed market is converted into a new observation by interpolation or automatic forward filling.

**Matching dates does not synchronize clocks.** Asian closes, London metal auctions, European closes, and US closes occur at different times. Morningstar’s closing-price documentation explicitly uses local exchange times and describes carry-forward conventions for closures; the NYSE calendar also distinguishes full holidays and early closes. These details explain why an apparently rectangular daily file can contain stale or asynchronous values. [9][10]

**The differential must be dimensionally meaningful.** Subtracting a USD-per-ounce metal price from an equity index quoted in index points gives a number whose scale depends on arbitrary quotation conventions. A normalized log price-level differential supplies a clear interpretation while retaining the requested analysis of prices rather than replacing it with a return-correlation regression.

Let \(P^{USD}_{m,t}\) be the price of metal \(m\), \(I^c_{j,t}\) the index level in its native denomination, and \(F^c_t\) USD per unit of that currency. Let \(t_0\) be a fixed common base observation.

$$\widetilde P_{m,t}=\frac{P^{USD}_{m,t}}{P^{USD}_{m,t_0}},\qquad \widetilde I^{USD}_{j,t}=\frac{I^c_{j,t}}{I^c_{j,t_0}}\frac{F^c_t}{F^c_{t_0}}.$$

The specified price differential is:

$$y^m_{j,t}=\log\widetilde P_{m,t}-\log\widetilde I^{USD}_{j,t}=\log\left(\frac{\widetilde P_{m,t}}{\widetilde I^{USD}_{j,t}}\right).$$

**This is a relative-price level.** A rising differential means that the metal has appreciated relative to the USD equity-price exposure since the base date. Its first difference is the metal-minus-equity log-return differential, but the fractional-integration model is applied to \(y^m_{j,t}\), not automatically to that first difference.

**Normalization removes arbitrary quotation units.** Changing a metal quotation from dollars to cents or an index base from one fixed number to another adds only a constant to a log differential. The model’s intercept absorbs that constant. By contrast, changing a price index into a total-return index introduces an evolving dividend component and changes the series itself.

**Consistent currency conversion has a useful property.** A one-to-one log price ratio is invariant to changing both assets into the same alternative currency using the same exchange rate. The important error is mixing USD metal prices with local-currency equity performance without accounting for exchange-rate movements. This distinction is especially relevant when comparing investors’ local-currency protection with a common-USD study.

**The section’s conclusion is precise:** benchmark identity, price variant, observation clock, and the differential formula define the object being tested; they are not cosmetic reporting choices.

## Fractional differencing and the prescribed decision rule

### The intercept-and-trend model

**Every differential uses both deterministic terms.** For each metal, index, and period, the specified model is:

$$y^m_{j,t}=\alpha^m_j+\beta^m_jt+x^m_{j,t},\qquad (1-L)^{d^m_j}x^m_{j,t}=u^m_{j,t}.$$

Here, \(Lx_t=x_{t-1}\), \(\alpha\) is an intercept, \(\beta t\) is a linear time trend, and \(u_t\) is a short-memory process. This is the intercept-and-linear-trend fractional-integration specification documented in the 2010 DIW methodological study. [11]

**The parameter measures shock persistence.** Fractional differencing generalizes the usual choice between zero differences and one full difference. Instead of forcing prices into a binary stationary/unit-root classification, it estimates the degree of dependence carried across time.

| Fractional order | Behavior after deterministic terms | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| \(d=0\) | Short memory | Shocks decay through short-run dynamics |
| \(0<d<0.5\) | Stationary long memory | Dependence decays slowly |
| \(0.5\le d<1\) | Nonstationary but mean-reverting | Shock effects eventually disappear |
| \(d=1\) | Unit-root boundary | Shocks have permanent level effects |
| \(d>1\) | More persistent than a unit root | No mean reversion in this framework |

These interpretations follow the methodological source’s distinction between covariance stationarity and mean reversion. [11]

**Reversion is around the fitted trend.** Because the model includes \(\beta t\), a mean-reverting stochastic component does not require the observed price differential to return to one fixed historical number. It means that the effect of a shock to the deviation from the deterministic path eventually disappears.

**Slow reversion is still slow protection.** A parameter below 1 can describe very persistent deviations. Consequently, a qualifying differential need not close quickly enough to protect an investor facing a margin call or a short holding period. The value of \(d\) is not a directly interpretable number of trading days to recovery, and it does not specify a portfolio hedge ratio.

### Estimation and confidence intervals

**The error specification is consequential.** A frequency-domain Whittle fit with a documented short-memory specification, accompanied by inversion of Robinson-type tests, is consistent with the methodological evidence. White-noise, AR(1), and Bloomfield disturbances represent different ways of separating short-run dependence from long-run fractional persistence. [11]

**Deterministic terms belong inside estimation.** For each trial fractional order, the same fractional transformation applies to the dependent variable, intercept, and trend. Merely removing an ordinary least-squares trend first and then treating the residual as if the deterministic components were known need not reproduce the joint fractional model. The deterministic-trend methodology explicitly formulates the transformed regressors. [12]

**The 95% interval records estimation uncertainty.** Under the test-inversion approach, it consists of fractional orders not rejected by the specified test at the chosen significance level. It can be asymmetric. It is not a range selected by taking the smallest and largest point estimates from different studies, different frequencies, or different error models.

**A point estimate below one is insufficient.** A useful numerical demonstration appears in the allowed 2010 methodological study, using the one-minute GBP/USD High series with an intercept and linear trend:

| Short-memory specification | Estimated \(d\) | 95% interval | Mean reversion under the requested rule |
|---|---:|---|---|
| White noise | 1.101 | [1.066, 1.141] | No |
| AR(1) | 0.936 | [0.883, 0.996] | Yes |
| Bloomfield | 0.962 | [0.915, 1.006] | No |

The Bloomfield point estimate is below 1, yet its upper bound exceeds 1. Only the AR(1) row satisfies the requested criterion. These are methodological examples from exchange-rate data, not entries in the gold or silver tables. [11]

**The strict inequality must be preserved.** An upper bound exactly equal to 1 produces “No.” Classification should use the underlying numerical precision, not a rounded printed value that happens to display as 1.00.

**Frequency also changes the comparison.** The same methodological study finds lower estimated integration orders at lower intraday frequencies. Its ten-minute results include mean-reverting cases that are not identified at the highest frequency. This establishes a reason to keep frequency constant across metals and periods, rather than treating daily, weekly, and monthly estimates as interchangeable. [11]

**The model section’s takeaway is simple:** all 52 decisions must come from comparable intercept-and-trend fits, and the upper confidence bound—not the point estimate alone—determines “Yes.”

## What mean reversion does—and does not—say about safe havens

### Relative-price stability is not crisis protection

**The two concepts answer different questions.** A hedge is commonly defined as an asset that is uncorrelated or negatively correlated with another asset on average. A safe haven concerns that relationship during severe market stress. Mean reversion instead asks whether shocks to a specified relative-price process eventually dissipate. [13]

**A mean-reverting spread can indicate co-movement.** Suppose two price series share a common upward or downward stochastic trend, while their difference fluctuates around an equilibrium. Their spread can mean-revert precisely because they remain tied together over time. That is not automatically evidence that one protects against losses in the other.

The following are analytical counterexamples, not simulated or observed market results. Let \(s_t\) be a random-walk equity log price, \(z_t\) an independent random walk, and \(v_t\) an independent stationary short-memory process.

| Hypothetical metal log price | Metal–equity spread | Spread order | Return relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(m_t=s_t+v_t\) | \(v_t\) | \(d=0\) | Positive covariance |
| \(m_t=z_t\) | \(z_t-s_t\) | \(d=1\) | Zero correlation under independence |
| \(m_t=c-s_t\) | \(c-2s_t\) | \(d=1\) | Perfect negative correlation |

**The first case passes persistence logic.** Because \(m_t-s_t=v_t\), the spread is short-memory and mean-reverting. Yet:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta m_t,\Delta s_t)=\operatorname{Var}(\Delta s_t)>0.$$

The metal shares the equity shock. A low spread \(d\) therefore does not establish protection during an equity collapse.

**The third case reverses the conclusion.** Here, \(\Delta m_t=-\Delta s_t\), so log returns move perfectly in opposite directions. Nevertheless, the spread contains the equity random walk and has order 1. A highly protective return relationship can therefore coexist with a non-mean-reverting price differential.

**This changes the economic interpretation.** The prescribed test should be respected as a test of mean reversion. Its positive results identify relative-price relationships whose stochastic disturbances are less persistent than a unit root with the required confidence. Calling them safe havens requires an additional economic argument about behavior when equities fall, not simply a change of label.

### Portfolio protection adds volatility and timing

**Diversification can help without negative correlation.** For a portfolio with metal weight \(w\) and equity weight \(1-w\), return variance is:

$$\sigma_p^2=w^2\sigma_m^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_e^2+2w(1-w)\rho_{me}\sigma_m\sigma_e.$$

A sufficiently low-volatility asset can reduce portfolio variance even when its correlation with equities is slightly positive. Conversely, a volatile metal with modestly negative correlation may offer less risk reduction than its correlation alone suggests.

**Crash-day protection is a stricter demand.** Investors may need the metal to hold its value during the same days that equities collapse. A subsequent rebound does not undo a forced sale, funding shortfall, or realized loss during the initial shock. Long-run persistence, full-period diversification, and contemporaneous crisis protection therefore need separate judgments.

**The interpretation section’s conclusion is decisive:** mean-reversion breadth is a relative-price-stability measure; safe-haven reliability also depends on downside returns, volatility, liquidity, and the investor’s horizon.

## Pre-pandemic findings: January 2010–December 2019

### Gold: the strongest supported case is diversification

**Gold’s pre-crisis evidence favors a hedge role.** He, O’Connor, and Thijssen’s 2018 study finds gold consistently useful as a hedge or diversifier against US and UK equity portfolios, but does not identify a distinct safe-haven regime that reliably maps onto severe market falls. Its historical US equity samples run through 29 September 2017, providing a longer-period benchmark that overlaps—but does not equal—the requested Pre-pandemic window. [13]

**Two requested index names appear directly.** The study’s daily CAPM estimates against the S&P 500 and Dow Jones show small negative gold betas and very low explanatory power:

| Requested index | Gold beta | Regression \(R^2\) | Economic interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| SP5 (S&P 500) | −0.0288 | 0.000594 | Small inverse average exposure |
| DOW (Dow Jones) | −0.0371 | 0.0010 | Small inverse average exposure |

These are the published CAPM results, not estimates of fractional \(d\). [13]

**The economic magnitudes are modest.** Using the reported betas, a 10% equity decline corresponds to an average gold-return association of approximately \(−0.0288\times−10\%=+0.288\%\) for SP5 and \(−0.0371\times−10\%=+0.371\%\) for DOW. These calculated associations are not predictions of gold’s realized return on a particular crash day.

**Low explanatory power is informative here.** The small \(R^2\) values mean that equity returns explain very little of gold’s variation in those linear regressions. That supports diversification, although it does not prove statistical independence or eliminate nonlinear tail dependence. The result is most naturally read as evidence that gold is not simply another equity position.

**The qualifying fractional set has an exact definition.** For the requested Pre-pandemic gold comparison:

$$\mathcal M_{G0}=\{j\in\mathcal J:u^{G0}_j<1\},\qquad N_{G0}=|\mathcal M_{G0}|\in\{0,1,\ldots,13\}.$$

Here, \(\mathcal J\) contains BFX, CAC40, DOW, HSI, N100, NAS, NIK, NYA, NZX, RUT, SP5, STO, and XAX. The US beta results support an economic hedge interpretation for DOW and SP5 on their own measurement basis; they do not assign either index to \(\mathcal M_{G0}\).

**“Pre-pandemic” does not mean uniformly calm.** The period includes the 2011 US credit-rating downgrade. Bond University’s discussion of international evidence identifies that episode as one in which gold and silver separated from falling equities and provided safe-haven benefits, particularly in developed economies. Thus, the first window already contains stress episodes rather than serving as a pure tranquil-market control. [14]

**Gold’s resulting judgment is qualified but favorable.** Its strongest supported pre-pandemic claim is low average equity exposure and useful diversification, with episodic crisis protection. A mean-reverting gold–equity differential would add evidence about the durability of relative-price deviations, but would not independently establish the crisis-specific part of that claim.

**Gold’s Pre-pandemic takeaway:** the historical evidence supports a defensive diversifier more strongly than an asset whose protection switches on predictably during every stock-market crash.

### Silver: metal-price persistence and equity protection diverge

**Silver’s defensive interpretation is more conditional.** Its precious-metal investment role can attract flight-to-quality demand, while its industrial exposure can make it respond to the same economic deterioration that depresses equities. These forces need not dominate in the same direction during every episode. [14]

**Published fractional estimates illustrate a key distinction.** Yaya, Vo, and Olayinka analyze daily gold and silver prices over 4 May 2011–31 July 2020. Their sample overlaps much of the Pre-pandemic window and the opening pandemic months, and their Table 3 reports the following standalone metal-price estimates at three bandwidths:

| Bandwidth | Gold: Whittle | Silver: Whittle | Gold: log-periodogram | Silver: log-periodogram |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 107 | 0.9785 | 0.8868 | 0.9623 | 0.8985 |
| 233 | 0.9405 | 0.9814 | 0.9439 | 1.0282 |
| 508 | 0.8831 | 0.9720 | 0.8836 | 0.9953 |

These are estimates for the individual bullion-price series, not silver-minus-stock-index differentials with the requested confidence-bound classification. [15]

**The metal ordering changes with specification.** At bandwidth 107, silver’s Whittle estimate is below gold’s; at bandwidths 233 and 508, silver’s is above gold’s. The log-periodogram estimates display a similar reversal. Therefore, even a broad statement that one metal is “less persistent” needs a clearly identified estimator and bandwidth.

**Volatility does not determine fractional order.** In the same study, the gold and silver volatility gauges have mean levels of 16.84 and 29.14 index points. Their reported maxima are 48.98 and 100.66 respectively. The numerical descriptive statistics point to greater silver-market volatility, despite a conflicting qualitative comment in the paper’s plot discussion. [15]

**Those gauges are not confidence intervals.** A higher volatility-gauge level concerns the size or anticipated intensity of fluctuations. Fractional \(d\) concerns their persistence. Neither the gauge comparison nor silver’s standalone \(d\) estimates can determine whether a particular silver–CAC40 or silver–NIK differential has an upper 95% bound below 1.

**Gold–silver cointegration makes the distinction concrete.** Yaya and colleagues report a joint price-pair order \(d=0.980\) and cointegrating order \(b=0.837\). The implied equilibrium-error order is \(d-b=0.980-0.837=0.143\), calculated from their estimates. This shows how two individually persistent metal prices can have a much less persistent equilibrium relationship. It concerns gold relative to silver, not either metal relative to the thirteen equity indices. [15]

**The qualifying silver set uses identical logic.**

$$\mathcal M_{S0}=\{j\in\mathcal J:u^{S0}_j<1\},\qquad N_{S0}=|\mathcal M_{S0}|\in\{0,1,\ldots,13\}.$$

A broader qualifying set would indicate more widespread evidence of relative-price reversion under this specification. It would not establish that silver’s losses are smaller, that its liquidity is better, or that its returns become negative-correlated with equities during a crash.

**The normal-period comparison favors different roles.** Gold’s low equity beta supports a strategic diversification interpretation. Silver’s dual demand structure supports a more contingent role: it may benefit from monetary or investment demand, but may also behave like a cyclical commodity. Evidence that silver protected during the 2011 downgrade is consistent with this conditionality rather than proof of universal protection. [14]

**Silver’s Pre-pandemic takeaway:** mean reversion, volatility, and safe-haven behavior are separate properties, and the independent evidence gives silver a less uniform defensive role than gold.

## Pandemic findings: January 2020–June 2022

### Gold: crash-day failure can coexist with portfolio benefits

**Early COVID evidence challenges automatic protection.** Cheema, Faff, and Szulczuk report that gold returns were negative on seven of the ten largest S&P 500 loss days in their pandemic analysis. The selected-day negative-return frequency is therefore \(7/10\times100=70\%\), calculated. This is an event-conditioned descriptive statistic, not an estimate of failure frequency across all pandemic days or all thirteen indices. [1]

**The 12 March example is consequential.** On 12 March 2020, gold lost 4.90% while the S&P 500 lost 10%. Gold therefore failed the strict expectation that it should hold its value precisely when equities suffered a very large daily loss. [1]

**Diversification still reduced that hypothetical loss.** Consider an explicitly hypothetical portfolio beginning that day with 50% in gold and 50% in the S&P 500, ignoring fees and rebalancing within the day. Its return would have been \(0.5\times−4.90\%+0.5\times−10\%=−7.45\%\). The loss is 2.55 percentage points smaller than the equity-only loss, yet the portfolio plainly did not preserve capital.

**This separates two defensible judgments.** Gold helped relative to holding only equities in that example, but it did not provide positive crash-day insurance. The distinction matters when describing gold as a “safe haven”: an investor seeking lower portfolio volatility and an investor seeking cash-like protection during a liquidation event are asking different questions.

**Longer-horizon pandemic evidence is more favorable.** Esparcia, Jareño, and Umar study gold combined with eight MSCI country indices, using an out-of-sample 2020 portfolio experiment and a 2019 comparison. Their framework models time-varying dependence and constructs monthly rebalanced minimum-Value-at-Risk portfolios. It therefore evaluates an investment strategy, rather than simply asking whether gold rose on the worst equity days. [16]

**A named correlation proxy shows mixed protection.** For four developed-market MSCI indices, the study’s cross-period table gives these average dynamic correlations between undecomposed USD log returns and gold:

| MSCI country proxy | 2019 correlation | 2020 correlation | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| France | −0.0184 | 0.0166 | +0.0350 |
| Italy | 0.0058 | 0.0241 | +0.0183 |
| United Kingdom | 0.0057 | 0.0133 | +0.0076 |
| United States | −0.1098 | 0.0087 | +0.1185 |

The changes are calculated as the 2020 value minus the 2019 value, using the paper’s cross-period table. These are MSCI country proxies, not substitutions for CAC40, DOW, SP5, or the other requested index definitions. [16]

**Average correlation became less favorable.** All four changes are positive, and the US proxy moves from a negative mean correlation to a slightly positive one. On this particular measure, gold’s average return relationship with these equity markets became less defensive in 2020 than in 2019.

**Portfolio risk reduction nevertheless improved.** The same paper reports annualized volatility for its equity-only positions and monthly rebalanced equity–gold strategies. The following calculations use its undecomposed-return strategy, with relative volatility reduction defined as \(100(1-\sigma_{\text{combined}}/\sigma_{\text{equity}})\).

| MSCI country proxy | 2019 volatility: equity / combined | 2019 reduction | 2020 volatility: equity / combined | 2020 reduction |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|
| France | 13.56% / 8.26% | 39.09% | 34.21% / 19.68% | 42.47% |
| Italy | 14.82% / 8.72% | 41.16% | 37.41% / 20.08% | 46.32% |
| United Kingdom | 12.90% / 8.08% | 37.36% | 33.65% / 19.22% | 42.88% |
| United States | 12.51% / 7.62% | 39.09% | 34.82% / 19.42% | 44.23% |

Volatility inputs come from the study’s 2019 and 2020 performance tables; reductions are calculations, not separately estimated parameters. [16]

**The US arithmetic illustrates the mechanism.** The 2020 reduction is \(100(1-19.42/34.82)=44.23\%\), compared with \(100(1-7.62/12.51)=39.09\%\) in 2019. The improvement is 5.14 percentage points. France, Italy, and the UK also show greater proportional volatility reduction in 2020.

**There is no contradiction in those findings.** Portfolio variance depends on both individual volatilities and covariance, while the portfolio weights in this study also change. A small positive mean correlation can coexist with substantial risk reduction when the equity position becomes much more volatile and the combination allocates risk differently.

**The empirical horizons also differ.** Cheema and colleagues emphasize the days of extreme equity losses. Esparcia and colleagues assess an actively managed strategy over the year. A metal can fail the first test yet contribute materially to the second, particularly if its behavior changes between the initial market shock and subsequent months.

**Mean-reversion strengthening has a narrower meaning.** For gold, the requested Pandemic set is \(\mathcal M_{G1}=\{j:u^{G1}_j<1\}\). A larger set than \(\mathcal M_{G0}\), combined with lower persistence estimates and more favorable upper bounds, would support stronger relative-price reversion. That conclusion should remain distinct from the crash-day and portfolio-volatility results above.

**Gold’s Pandemic takeaway:** the evidence is unfavorable to guaranteed crash-day protection but favorable to portfolio diversification; “strengthened” or “weakened” depends on which property is being measured.

### Silver: cyclical exposure makes crisis protection less dependable

**Early pandemic evidence is unfavorable to silver.** Cheema, Faff, and Szulczuk conclude that silver did not function as a safe haven during either the 2008 financial crisis or their COVID-19 episode. Their conclusion directly challenges treating silver as defensive merely because it belongs to the precious-metals category. [1]

**A rebound answers a different question.** Yaya and colleagues describe a sharp March–April 2020 decline in the metal prices and a quick subsequent rebound in silver. That recovery is relevant to investors able to remain invested, but it does not establish protection during the initial fall or a qualifying fractional confidence bound for a silver–equity differential. [15]

**Industrial demand provides a plausible mechanism.** Silver’s uses in electronics, electrical systems, and solar cells tie part of its demand to production and investment activity. Morgan Stanley’s structural comparison reports that more than half of silver demand comes from heavy industry and high technology. A deterioration in economic expectations can therefore depress silver demand at the same time that it depresses expected corporate earnings. [17]

**That mechanism competes with investment demand.** A crisis may also increase demand for precious metals as stores of value. Silver’s outcome depends on whether investment flows, industrial expectations, supply disruptions, and broader commodity selling offset or reinforce one another. The dual role explains why it can behave defensively in some markets while behaving cyclically in others.

**Relative rankings do not establish absolute safety.** Banerjee and Pradhan rank silver second to gold among four precious metals under their high-frequency copula analysis. That is compatible with silver being the next-best alternative in a particular comparison while still failing a stricter requirement to preserve wealth on extreme loss days. [2]

**Higher volatility changes allocation economics.** The World Gold Council’s longer-history comparison describes silver volatility as roughly twice gold’s. Under an illustrative standalone-volatility-matching calculation, \(w_S\sigma_S=w_G\sigma_G\) and \(\sigma_S\approx2\sigma_G\) imply \(w_S\approx w_G/2\). This is a calculated risk-scaling relationship, not an optimal portfolio allocation; actual portfolio risk contributions also depend on covariance. [18]

**The fractional comparison remains asset-specific.** Silver’s qualifying Pandemic set is \(\mathcal M_{S1}=\{j:u^{S1}_j<1\}\). Greater volatility does not logically force \(d\) upward, and a quick rebound does not force its upper confidence bound below 1. The direction of the persistence comparison must follow the silver differential itself.

**The safe-haven judgment is therefore conditional.** The independent COVID evidence makes silver a less dependable candidate for immediate equity-crash protection. A stronger result under the requested mean-reversion criterion would establish improved relative-price reversion, but would not erase the distinction between eventual recovery and protection during the initial drawdown.

**Silver’s Pandemic takeaway:** silver can participate in recovery and sometimes provide crisis diversification, but its industrial and volatility exposures make it a less consistent defensive substitute for gold.

## Comparing retained, lost, and new relationships

### Relationship counts and their interpretation

**The transition categories are mutually exclusive.** For either metal \(m\), let \(\mathcal M_{m0}\) and \(\mathcal M_{m1}\) be its qualifying Pre-pandemic and Pandemic sets. Then retained relationships belong to both sets, lost relationships qualify only before the pandemic, and new relationships qualify only during it.

| Relationship category | Pre-pandemic | Pandemic | Gold count bound | Silver count bound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Retained | Yes | Yes | 0–13 | 0–13 |
| Lost | Yes | No | 0–13 | 0–13 |
| New | No | Yes | 0–13 | 0–13 |
| Neither period | No | No | 0–13 | 0–13 |

**The counts must add consistently.** If \(r_m\), \(l_m\), \(n_m\), and \(q_m\) denote retained, lost, new, and neither-period counts, respectively:

$$r_m+l_m+n_m+q_m=13,\qquad N_{m0}=r_m+l_m,\qquad N_{m1}=r_m+n_m.$$

Consequently:

$$\Delta N_m=N_{m1}-N_{m0}=n_m-l_m,\qquad -13\leq\Delta N_m\leq13.$$

**Equal breadth can conceal turnover.** If new and lost relationships have equal counts, total breadth is unchanged even though the metal’s market-specific relationships have shifted. For an investor exposed to CAC40 rather than SP5, the identity of the retained relationship matters more than the overall number of “Yes” outcomes.

**Counts alone also conceal intensity.** Two differentials may both qualify, but one upper bound may lie only just below 1 while the other lies materially farther below it. Conversely, two “No” results can differ because one interval crosses the boundary narrowly while another is concentrated above it. Reporting \(\hat d\) and the complete interval is therefore essential.

**Additional documented evidence**

The evidence available here supports a **conditional safe-haven interpretation**, not an unconditional ranking of gold over silver. For the relationships covered in this part, the contextual sources do not report the requested fractional-integration \(d\) estimates, 95% confidence intervals, upper endpoints, or Mean Reversion classifications for the two exact windows—2010-01-01–2019-12-31 and 2020-01-01–2022-06-30. The reported relationships therefore must not be substituted for the study’s row-level results or used to construct its qualifying sets and counts [66][122][121].

A Mean Reversion = Yes result under the study’s rule concerns persistence and return toward stable behavior in the specified metal–index differential. It is not, by itself, proof of contemporaneous inverse correlation, a hedge, diversification, causality, profitability, or universal crisis protection. A high correlation can coexist with a relationship that does not revert to a stable mean [63]. The distinction is also important economically: a hedge is described as an inverse relationship under normal conditions, whereas a safe haven refers to inverse correlation during an extreme downturn [66]. Safe-haven assets can nevertheless remain risky and may not display the safe-haven feature in every episode [121].

The proposed mechanism is investor reallocation: during financial crises or periods of uncertainty, investors may sell stocks and move funds into gold as a store of value; when equity markets stabilize, they may reverse that allocation. This mechanism could produce a negative stock–gold relationship after a negative stock-market shock, but it is a hypothesis about behavior rather than a causal implication of a Mean Reversion classification [66].

### Distinguishing persistence changes from precision changes

**A lost classification need not mean higher persistence.** The Pandemic window is shorter. A confidence interval can widen sufficiently to reach 1 even if the point estimate falls. Under the requested rule, that relationship changes from “Yes” to “No,” but the evidence has become less decisive rather than necessarily showing a larger underlying \(d\).

**An exact decomposition clarifies this issue.** Write the upper uncertainty distance as \(h=u-\hat d\). Then:

$$u_{m1,j}-u_{m0,j}=(\hat d_{m1,j}-\hat d_{m0,j})+(h_{m1,j}-h_{m0,j}).$$

This identity separates movement in the point estimate from movement in the upper interval distance. It requires no assumption that the confidence interval is symmetric.

**The strongest weakening evidence is aligned.** A decline in qualifying breadth accompanied by higher point estimates and higher upper bounds would provide a coherent weakening pattern in relative-price reversion. A decline in breadth with lower point estimates but wider intervals warrants a different judgment: less statistically decisive evidence, rather than an unqualified increase in persistence.

**The strongest strengthening evidence is also aligned.** More qualifying relationships, lower estimated \(d\), and lower upper bounds jointly support broader and stronger reversion. If breadth improves while some previously strong relationships deteriorate, the result is a trade-off rather than uniform improvement.

**Classification changes are not causal estimates.** Even a clean difference between the two windows describes a change across periods, not an isolated causal effect of COVID-19. The window labels organize the comparison; they do not hold constant every other market influence or the amount of statistical information in each sample.

**Benchmark dependence also affects aggregation.** Six US indices share substantial market exposure, and every gold differential contains the same gold series. The thirteen classifications should therefore not be interpreted as thirteen independent experiments. Similarly, fifty-two individual 95% intervals are not automatically a simultaneous 95% confidence statement about the entire panel.

**The transition section’s takeaway:** compare named relationships, point estimates, upper bounds, and sample precision together; a single count is an incomplete description of change.

## Why the independent studies reach different conclusions

### They measure different forms of protection

**The disagreement is economically meaningful.** The studies differ not merely in technical implementation but in the outcome they call protection. Average equity exposure, extreme-day returns, tail dependence, and the performance of a rebalanced portfolio are related but distinct objects.

| Study | Measurement basis | Principal finding | Question answered |
|---|---|---|---|
| He, O’Connor and Thijssen, 2018 | CAPM and Markov switching | Gold is a hedge/diversifier | Average and regime-dependent equity exposure |
| Cheema, Faff and Szulczuk, 2020 | Extreme-loss days and GJR-GARCH | Gold and silver fail early COVID protection tests | Protection when equities fall hardest |
| Banerjee and Pradhan, 2024 | High-frequency copula dependence | Gold first; silver second among four metals | Relative hedge and safe-haven ranking |
| Esparcia, Jareño and Umar | Dynamic dependence and minimum-VaR portfolios | Gold improves pandemic portfolio diversification | Risk reduction through an investment strategy |

The findings are documented in the respective studies. [13][1][2][16]

**A low beta is not insurance.** Gold can have almost no average linear equity exposure while still falling sharply during selected episodes. A low-beta estimate averages across many observations and does not impose a nonnegative return during every crisis day.

**A relative winner can still lose.** Ranking gold above silver says that gold performs better on the study’s chosen criteria. It does not say that gold eliminates losses or that silver provides no diversification benefit. This distinction reconciles a favorable precious-metal ranking with unfavorable evidence on particular extreme days.

**An investment strategy is more than an asset.** Portfolio studies incorporate allocation weights, rebalancing, volatility differences, and sometimes short positions. Their findings demonstrate what a specified strategy achieved under its assumptions, not an intrinsic guarantee attached to each ounce of metal.

**Integration is another separate property.** Farinha and colleagues report 53 integrated relationships out of 90 possible directed market pairs and 67 Granger-causal relationships in their January 2019–September 2020 study. Those findings concern market linkage and predictive transmission, not the confidence-bound rule for the requested metal–index spreads. [19]

**The resulting judgment is not agnosticism.** The evidence supports gold’s diversification value while rejecting an unconditional interpretation of its safe-haven label. For silver, both its relative standing behind gold and its occasional protective episodes can be true. The disagreement becomes manageable once the outcome, horizon, and market are stated explicitly.

### Market structure explains the gold–silver contrast

**Gold has a broader demand base.** The World Gold Council describes gold demand as distributed across investment, consumer uses, and official reserves, while silver demand is more industrial-heavy. This gives gold more scope to attract investment demand when economic growth deteriorates, whereas a larger part of silver demand remains exposed to that deterioration. [18]

**Silver supply adds another cyclical channel.** The same source reports that 70–80% of silver production comes as a by-product of copper, lead, and zinc. Its supply response is therefore connected to decisions and disruptions in other mining sectors, rather than responding only to silver’s own investment demand. [18]

**Liquidity affects usable protection.** The World Gold Council’s 2026 structural comparison describes gold as deeper and more liquid, with tighter spreads and lower volatility. Such characteristics can matter during stress because an investor must be able to buy, sell, or rebalance the defensive asset at an acceptable cost. They provide an economic rationale for favoring gold, rather than a retrospective estimate for every observation in 2010–2022. [18]

**Depth does not prevent forced selling.** A liquid asset can be sold to obtain cash when investors face losses elsewhere. Thus, gold’s liquidity may support its long-run portfolio role while not preventing a short-run decline during a broad liquidation. This is a plausible reconciliation of its defensive reputation with the observed March 2020 co-decline, not a causal estimate of that episode.

**Industrial exposure does not rule out protection.** Silver can still attract substantial investment demand or benefit from a supply constraint during an equity downturn. Bond University’s discussion explicitly notes stronger silver safe-haven behavior in some markets during extreme stress. The appropriate contrast is therefore greater conditionality, not an absolute claim that silver never protects. [14]

**The structural takeaway favors gold:** its demand mix, liquidity, and lower volatility support a steadier defensive role, while silver combines precious-metal protection with more cyclical exposure.

## Overall judgment and practical meaning

### Which metal is more reliable?

**Gold is the better-supported defensive baseline.** The independent evidence combines small average equity betas, favorable relative rankings, and demonstrated portfolio-volatility reduction. Silver has credible protective episodes and ranks next to gold in the cited copula study, but its industrial exposure and higher volatility make its defensive role less consistent.

**Reliability still depends on the objective.** For an investor seeking lower portfolio volatility over months, gold has a stronger evidentiary case. For an investor demanding preservation of capital on the worst equity days, the early COVID results reject a blanket promise for either metal. For an investor seeking cyclical upside alongside precious-metal exposure, silver may be attractive for reasons that are different from safe-haven reliability.

**The requested persistence ranking is conditional.** On the specified mean-reversion measure, gold would lead in both periods if \(N_{G0}>N_{S0}\) and \(N_{G1}>N_{S1}\), with the \(d\) estimates and upper-bound margins supporting that breadth advantage. Silver would lead under the reverse pattern; a reversal of leadership across periods would indicate market-condition dependence. Those conditions decide the exact-panel ranking, while crisis-return evidence decides whether the resulting relative-price stability also provides protection when equities collapse.

**A broader qualifying set is not enough by itself.** Because a mean-reverting spread can reflect common long-run movement, the practical safe-haven conclusion should remain anchored to downside behavior. The most convincing defensive asset would combine robust relative-price results with tolerable losses, low crisis equity dependence, and liquidity that remains usable during stress.

**The normal-versus-crisis distinction is therefore substantive.** Before the pandemic, gold’s strongest supported role is persistent diversification rather than a uniquely activated crisis regime. During the pandemic, it offered portfolio risk reduction even while failing on several of the worst equity days. Silver’s behavior is more sensitive to the balance between investment demand and industrial conditions in both settings.

**Evidence and measurement basis.** The supplied material contains benchmark documentation and studies with different samples, markets, or estimators, rather than the 52 fitted differential estimates and confidence intervals required for numerical versions of Tables 1 and 2. Those tables are therefore conditional registers, not estimated results; the 0–13 counts are logical bounds, not confidence intervals. Fractional \(d\) is dimensionless, the displayed portfolio reductions are calculations from cited annualized volatility inputs, and later structural commentary is not treated as a measurement of the requested historical panel.

**The final judgment favors gold, conditionally.** Gold is the more defensible choice for steady equity diversification; silver is better understood as a more volatile, market-dependent complement. Neither should be described as guaranteed crisis insurance, and mean reversion of a metal–equity price differential should not be mistaken for proof that the metal protects investors precisely when stock markets fall.

**Additional documented evidence**

Cross-market persistence can reflect measurement and market-structure conditions as well as economic substitution. Financial data may contain irregular observation times, intraday patterns, price discreteness, and long-lived dependence. When series trade at different rates, interpolation used to align them can create spurious correlations; temporal aggregation generally reduces dependence but negative first-order autocorrelation can remain [48]. Volatility, trading frequency, volume, and spreads commonly follow a U-shaped intraday pattern, with activity and volatility particularly high near the open and close [48]. These features make time-zone, holiday, closing-price, and alignment conventions material to comparisons across global indices.

Regularization choices can also change the measured relationship. Common alternatives include using the most recent price at the end of an interval, carrying forward the previous price, or interpolating between observations [48]. Different discretization schemes trade off temporal-aggregation bias and variance; averaging reduces variability but blurs event timing, and interpolation can induce positive autocorrelation or even create apparently causal relationships in stylized settings [48]. The source gives a one-hour versus five-second intertrade duration example to show why irregular spacing matters for volatility measurement [48]. In a separate high-frequency example, more than 98% of observations took only five values and sample kurtosis was 66; those figures concern Airgas data, not the present study, but illustrate the potential effect of discreteness and heavy tails [48].

Market frictions provide further plausible mechanisms. Orderly-market requirements can smooth a large price move through intervening transactions, large orders may be split into smaller orders, and trade timing can contain information about asymmetric trading and uncertainty [48]. Tick-size rounding and market-making costs can alter observed-price variance and first-order serial correlation [48]. These are plausible explanations for differences in measured persistence across markets or periods, not causal findings identified by the requested fractional-integration estimates.

Finally, mean-reversion evidence is vulnerable to regime changes. Stationary behavior has time-invariant statistical properties, whereas unit-root behavior produces long memory; differencing can remove unit roots, but a regime shift can change the underlying process [63]. Mean-reversion strategies are specifically described as vulnerable to fundamental market-regime changes and prolonged drawdowns, with leverage potentially amplifying losses [63]. Thus, even a broad set of Pre-pandemic or Pandemic Yes classifications would support a bounded, sample-specific conclusion rather than a permanent safe-haven guarantee.

The NZX label requires an additional qualification. The available NZX material exposes data for the **S&P/NZX 50, S&P/NZX 20, and S&P/NZX All**, with NZX as the upstream source, but it does not resolve which of those benchmarks the generic “NZX” label denotes [124]. NZX market data are also described as delayed by 20 minutes and potentially cached [124]. Any NZX comparison must therefore retain the benchmark identity condition and must not silently treat one of these series as the requested index without explicit verification.

Under the requested decision rule, a supported **Mean Reversion = Yes** means that the unrounded upper endpoint of the two-sided 95% confidence interval for the estimated fractional-differencing parameter \(d\) is strictly below 1 in the intercept-plus-linear-trend model; it is not enough for the point estimate alone to be below 1. [127] Fractional-integration evidence should therefore be read as persistence evidence: \(d\in[0.5,1)\) permits shocks with long-lasting but ultimately fading effects, whereas \(d\geq1\) implies permanent shocks in the cited interpretation. The same source distinguishes anti-persistence (\(d<0\)), short memory (\(d=0\)), long memory (\(d>0\)), stationarity (\(d<0.5\)), nonstationarity (\(d\geq0.5\)), and a unit root (\(d=1\)). [127]

[128] Thus, a “Yes” result here supports only mean reversion of the specified metal–stock differential. Long-memory evidence can be distorted by level shifts or low-frequency contamination: apparent fractional persistence may be spurious rather than a stable economic property. [129] A relevant robustness approach is to exclude or truncate very low-frequency components when testing structural stability; the cited discussion describes a truncation band shrinking at the rate \(\log(T)/T\) and reports good simulated size across a wide range of truncations, provided the selected band contains the relevant changes. [129] However, structural-break tests can have non-monotonic power when multiple breaks occur, and inference based on long-run variance estimates can also suffer from non-monotonic power. [129] A change in persistence between the two requested windows should therefore not automatically be interpreted as a change in intrinsic metal quality.

State dependence is another plausible explanation. Predictive or mean-reverting behavior can differ between recessions and expansions, for which a Markov regime-switching formulation is a possible alternative rather than proof that one regime is economically causal. [129] Similarly, a forecast failure does not necessarily mean that a model should be abandoned, because losses can change when shock variance changes; the source recommends assessing the source of the change. [129]

A cited trading discussion warns that parameters may require updating and that a mean-reversion approach may need to be suspended or replaced by trend following after a regime change. The contextual evidence supports a **conditional and market-specific** interpretation of gold and silver as safe havens; it does not replace the study’s primary rule, under which Mean Reversion is determined only by whether the unrounded upper 95% confidence bound for \(d\) is strictly below 1. A conventional hedge is defined through an average negative or zero correlation, whereas a safe haven is defined through a negative or zero relationship during extreme market conditions; linear definitions also miss rare tail events [219]. A separate copula formulation distinguishes a hedge using Kendall’s tau, a weak safe haven using zero lower-tail dependence, and a strong safe haven using both zero lower-tail dependence and negative tail correlation [219]. The alternative safe-haven procedure in the cited historical study estimates dynamic OLS relations and identifies stress using the 5%, 2.5%, and 1% quantiles of stock returns.
