# Gut Microbiota and Colorectal Cancer Treatment: Benefits, Risks, and Evidence Through September 2024

**The effect is treatment-specific.** Gut microbiota—the microorganisms living in the intestine—can influence both the effectiveness and complications of colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment. They affect anastomotic healing after surgery, chemotherapy metabolism and resistance, immune responses to checkpoint inhibitors, and intestinal injury during pelvic radiotherapy. The clearest human intervention evidence concerns selected postoperative infection and treatment-related diarrhea outcomes; many effects on tumor response remain supported principally by laboratory experiments and observational studies. [1][2]

**Microbiota manipulation is an adjunct.** Through 30 September 2024, the evidence supported carefully selected microbiota-directed approaches as potential additions to CRC care, not replacements for established treatment. Probiotic effects depended on the product and clinical setting, while fecal microbiota transplantation remained investigational for improving cancer-treatment outcomes. Microbial signatures were promising research biomarkers, but did not justify routine microbiome-based selection of chemotherapy or immunotherapy. [1][3]

**The report follows the clinical questions.** The interaction tables first compare advantages and complications across the requested treatments. The subsequent sections explain the mechanisms, distinguish human intervention results from associations and animal findings, examine conflicting results, and assess the clinical readiness and safety of probiotics, prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation.

**The scope is global.** This synthesis concerns publicly available findings through **30 September 2024**, without geographic restriction. ### Surgery

**The principal outcomes are healing and infection.** An anastomosis is the surgical connection between two bowel ends; anastomotic leakage means that connection loses integrity. Surgical-site infection includes infections involving the incision or operative site.

**Additional documented evidence**

**Evidence and mechanisms**

- In **156 patients**, probiotics given **3–15 days before surgery** were associated with less postoperative-site infection; in **60 patients**, dosing **5 days before and 7 days after surgery** was associated with faster bowel recovery [79].
- A randomized trial of **164** open elective colonic-cancer patients (**80 probiotic; 84 placebo**) gave treatment from **1 day before to 15 days after surgery** and assessed complications within **30 days**. It reported fewer major complications, pneumonia, surgical-site infection and anastomotic leak, plus shorter hospitalization; TNF/IL-6 and SOCS3 regulation was proposed [259].
- Among **78** CRC-resection patients receiving probiotics for **one year**, ileus decreased and hospital stay and deaths were lower; the infection trend was nonsignificant, with the greatest apparent benefit in rectal tumors [259].
- The MIRACLe pilot combined oral antibiotics, bowel preparation and eight-strain probiotics in **60** laparoscopic tumor-surgery patients versus **500** ERAS controls: anastomotic leak was **1.7% vs 6.4%**, surgical-site infection **1.7% vs 3.6%**, and reoperation **1.7% vs 4.2%**. Recovery and stay were shorter, but differences were not statistically significant and exposures were bundled [259].
- A **100-patient**, approximately **six-week** post-resection/chemotherapy study reduced abdominal pain, distension, constipation and diarrhea and increased fecal SCFAs, but did not significantly change chemotherapy efficacy [259].

Results were not uniform: in **54** CRC-surgery patients, synbiotics increased mucosal lactic-acid bacteria without changing postoperative course or complications [79]. A **140-patient** preoperative study gave **30 g** mixed prebiotics for **7 days**, increasing commensals and identifying *Bacteroides* for mechanism research, without establishing a surgical outcome [79]. A 2024 **40-patient** postoperative crossover study associated a modified microbiota-accessible-carbohydrate diet with improved diarrhea (**28 patients**) and constipation (**27**), although symptoms worsened in **10** and **13** patients; adjuvant chemotherapy confounded attribution [260].

Direct evidence is therefore clinical and complication-focused; FAA-diet and FMT animal findings are indirect, and no surgery-specific in-vitro outcome is reported [259][256]. A registry protocol planned postoperative synbiotics for **15 days**, with bowel and quality-of-life outcomes at **1, 3 and 6 months**, but reports no results [261]. **Row answer:** Treatment Area = Surgery; Specific Modality = colorectal surgical resection; positive impacts = recovery and infection signals; negative or limiting evidence = dysbiosis, antibiotic disruption, null and nonsignificant findings. Priorities are standardized trials and validated biomarkers [246]; FMT remains investigational pending donor, engraftment, delivery and long-term-safety evidence [251].

Surgical resection (Surgery) has mixed evidence through 2024-09-30: dysbiosis/barrier loss and *E. faecalis*/*F. nucleatum* may promote MMP9-mediated anastomotic leakage; fibre prehabilitation (mice) and microbiota modulation show healing signals [252][263][53][255]. In 4,999 patients, oral antibiotics+MBP reduced leakage 5.7%→2.8% [253]; either alone did not. Probiotic/synbiotic effects were mixed [249]; FMT remains investigational; Phase I NCT06370884 assessed post-surgical healing [247].

CRC resection/Surgery: 2024 fecal n98 (10 AL; matched 10/10): controls had more Streptococcus/Citrobacter/Klebsiella; assoc only [202]. Review 2016 n3023: intra-op leaks 6.4%; testing 4.3% vs 6.8% postop (p=.051) [245]. LAR: 5.5% stapling; leak 15% vs .9% [262].

CRC resection—Surgery: AL 13/77 post-op [258]; SSI 3–26%/30d [203]; microbes aid healing, but dysbiosis/E. faecalis–MMP-9 associate with leakage; FMT (18 patients→mice) raised cytokines [258]; sparse evidence.

CRC surgery: A. onderdonkii→leak, P. goldsteinii→healing [189]; 144 mice (2024): bowel-prep microbiota changed, no leak effect [257]; future: targeted therapy/marker.

CRC resection: a 2024 review of 24 RCTs (17 surgery studies) found mixed pre-/pro-/synbiotic effects—often fewer infections, translocation, hospital days and antibiotic use; barrier/colonization resistance is proposed [264]. A 2023 Italian cohort (4,529 patients) reported 26.8% morbidity, 7.5% major morbidity, 4.5% leak, 1.4% mortality; no microbiota measures [254].

Colorectal resection is a Surgery modality. By 2024-09-30, a review of 24 CRC RCTs (17 surgical) found perioperative probiotics/synbiotics associated with fewer infections, faster gut recovery, shorter stay and less antibiotic use (infection RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.54–0.74; surgical-site infection OR 0.53, 0.36–0.78) [77]. Results were mixed; strain, dose and duration need larger trials, and barrier mechanisms are described [77][157]. *E. faecalis* collagen degradation/MMP9 activation may promote anastomotic leak [265].

CRC resection: probiotics reduced major complications (28.6% vs 48.8%; 17 RCTs, n=1,242), but leak/LOS/QOL effects remained mixed in 2020 [80].

CRC surgery: in a 2023, 16-study non-microbiota comparison, compression anastomosis had 3.64% vs 9.49% reoperation (handsewn); leaks, infection, abscess, mortality, and hospital stay did not differ [250].

Colorectal-surgery RCTs: 88 analyzed (2007): synbiotics+neomycin+MBP reduced translocation (2/20 vs 11/24; P<0.001), not septic morbidity (3/20 vs 5/24; P=0.928) [266]. PEG vs meal (250, 2005) did not change infection/leak [267]; cited antibiotic-associated C. difficile risk [268]; complications use 30-day endpoints [248].

For Surgery, perioperative probiotics/synbiotics are associated with faster bowel recovery and fewer infections; a review covered 17 surgery studies, but effects were mixed and optimal strain, dose, and duration remained uncertain [570,403]. Fusobacterium nucleatum positivity was higher in leak than non-leak patients (87.5% vs 48.28%), but human associations and rat/epithelial findings do not establish causality [557,402].

For Surgery, surgical-site infection occurs in 3–26% within 30 days; the proposed Trojan-horse mechanism has intestinal pathogens such as MRSA travel inside macrophages or neutrophils to remote wounds [203]. Postoperative infection is associated with increased Actinomycetaceae, Actinomyces, Sutterella, and Enterococcus faecalis, while probiotics/synbiotics often reduce infectious complications, although results remain mixed [420,570].

Mechanistically, Surgery links microbiota to healing through the bacterial depletion sought by mechanical bowel preparation plus oral nonabsorbable antibiotics [253], and through species-level effects: Fusobacterium nucleatum exposure in rats increased epithelial MMP9, depleted collagen, and impaired anastomotic healing [263]. In mice, inulin/5-ASA activated gut PPAR-γ, reinforced the barrier, and prevented anastomotic tumors and metastatic spread; however, human leak signatures are associative rather than causal, with functional support including a murine model (n = 20) [349,402,404].

Colorectal surgical resection disrupts microbiota through perioperative preparation, antibiotics, and reconstruction; loss of colonization resistance/barrier permits pathogen expansion and translocation, while altered permeability, transit, digestion, metabolism, and immune function can drive infection, anastomotic leak, dysmotility, and malabsorption [213]. The anastomotic-leak-associated microbiome reduced anti-inflammatory PPAR-γ activation in mice, and Enterococcus faecalis collagenase/MMP9 activity degrades collagen and impairs healing; anastomotic leak can extend inpatient stay from 7 to 19 days, so these preclinical/associative mechanisms need clinical validation [219,97].

### Chemotherapy

**The regimens contain overlapping drugs.** FOLFOX combines 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin. CAPEOX, also called XELOX, combines capecitabine with oxaliplatin; capecitabine is an oral fluoropyrimidine that produces 5-fluorouracil. FOLFIRI combines leucovorin, 5-fluorouracil, and irinotecan, so microbiota associations observed during these regimens do not automatically identify the contribution of one drug. [8]

### Immunotherapy

**Checkpoint inhibition releases immune brakes.** Immune checkpoint inhibitors target programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), its ligand PD-L1, or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4). Microbiota can influence immune priming and intestinal immune tolerance, potentially affecting both antitumor activity and inflammatory toxicity. [16]

### Radiotherapy

**The CRC relevance is principally rectal cancer.** Pelvic radiotherapy can damage normal intestinal tissue as well as treating the tumor. Radiation enteritis or enteropathy describes radiation-related bowel injury; diarrhea is an important symptom, whereas dysbiosis describes microbial compositional or functional disruption rather than a clinical diagnosis. [24]

| Treatment Area | Specific Modality | Positive Impacts/Advantages | Negative Impacts/Complications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radiotherapy | Pelvic radiotherapy | Human randomized synthesis, mixed pelvic cancers: probiotic and synbiotic subgroups had lower acute-diarrhea risk, with risk ratios of 0.71 and 0.45, respectively. Proposed protection includes barrier support and altered microbial metabolites. [25] | Human observational: treatment alters microbial diversity and composition, often alongside diarrhea. Approximately 30–50% of pelvic-radiotherapy patients develop diarrhea. Proposed mechanisms include epithelial inflammation and disturbed microbial bile-salt processing; a 2024 rectal study found increased richness with enteritis. [24][26] |
| Radiotherapy | Pelvic chemoradiotherapy with 5-FU or capecitabine | Human observational, CRC-specific: a 39-patient rectal-cancer cohort linked preserved diversity to favorable response. An integrated microbial–immune model distinguished good from poor response with an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve of 0.821. [27] | Human randomized toxicity data: concurrent 5-FU increased grade ≥2 diarrhea from 17% with radiotherapy alone to 34%. A small rectal-cancer synbiotic trial showed no statistically significant improvement in its principal between-group symptom or quality-of-life comparisons. [24][1] |

### Future Outlook

**These interventions alter different biological inputs.** Probiotics are live microorganisms administered for a health benefit; prebiotics are substrates selectively used by host microorganisms, commonly fermentable fibers. Synbiotics combine microorganisms and supporting substrates. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) transfers a screened donor-derived fecal microbial community, rather than a small set of selected strains. [1][5]

| Treatment Area | Specific Modality | Positive Impacts/Advantages | Negative Impacts/Complications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Future Outlook | probiotics/prebiotics | Human randomized synthesis: 24 CRC RCTs involving 2,204 participants supported benefits for selected surgical and diarrhea outcomes. Prebiotic fermentation and probiotic-supported microbial networks may increase short-chain fatty acids and strengthen epithelial barriers. [1][28] | Effects are strain-, substrate-, dose-, and setting-dependent. The 24-RCT review contained no immunotherapy supplementation trials. Live products carry infection concerns in vulnerable patients, and prebiotic protection against checkpoint colitis remains a mechanistic hypothesis. [1][22] |
| Future Outlook | fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) | Preclinical CRC: reduced FOLFOX mucositis and enhanced checkpoint responses. Early human development included the CRC-specific RENMIN-215 combination trial; a separate 12-patient, mixed-cancer series reported an 83% clinical response in refractory checkpoint colitis. [11][29][20] | Human evidence is exploratory and often uncontrolled or combination-based. Donor-derived infection, transfer of undesirable microbial functions, and bacterial translocation require careful assessment; short-term animal protection does not establish safety in immunocompromised patients. [11][20] |

- **Surgery—colorectal resection:** **Positive:** Probiotics reduced incision infection, pneumonia, urinary infection and anastomotic leak in 19 controlled studies involving 1,975 CRC patients. **Negative:** Complications occurred in 23–34.6% and included diarrhea, leak, obstruction and infection; the broad FMT claim was unquantified [78].
- **Systemic chemotherapy:** **Positive:** A balanced microbiota may enhance response, while probiotics reduced gastrointestinal toxicity. **Negative:** Dysbiosis may hinder response or cause diarrhea, and *F. nucleatum* is linked to resistance (associative evidence) [419,202]. In 23 first-line fluoropyrimidine patients, diarrhea tracked lower Firmicutes/Bifidobacterium and higher Bacteroidetes, without alpha- or beta-diversity change; CAPOX diarrhea groups differed in 75 strains after eight cycles [222,202].
- **Oxaliplatin:** In mice, only half survived high-dose treatment long term; oxaliplatin-exposed FMT and Lactobacillus/Bifidobacterium depletion worsened toxicity, whereas probiotics mitigated it without reducing efficacy; CRC patients had reduced CD45+IL-10+ cells [95].
- **Irinotecan:** Diarrhea occurred in 60–90% of treated CRC patients and was severe in 20–40%; microbial β-glucuronidase reactivates SN-38G to toxic SN-38, injuring mucosa. Three β-glucuronidases marked high-metabolizer fecal samples from 20 healthy people, but cancer risk remained hypothetical. Antibiotics may reduce toxicity while removing beneficial bacteria; a completed phase-3 probiotic study of 46 participants reported rationale, not results [338,74].
- **ICB, anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4:** Nine studies of 734 metastatic CRC patients (2010–2023) found any-grade/severe colitis of 3.9%/2.5%; PD-1 monotherapy yielded 3.5%/2.3%, PD-L1 1.1%, CTLA-4 6.4%/2.1%, and CTLA-4+PD-1 4.9%/3.2% [150]. **Additional documented evidence**

**Future Outlook: scope and maturity**

Through 30 September 2024, microbiota-directed interventions remained investigational adjuncts rather than established CRC treatments. The clearest clinical signal in the evidence summarized here is reduced postoperative infection after perioperative probiotic or synbiotic use, not proven tumor-control benefit. “Probiotics” are live microorganisms administered in adequate amounts; “prebiotics” are indigestible substrates intended to stimulate selected bacteria; synbiotics combine both, whereas FMT transfers donor stool-derived communities. Their effects should therefore be reported as direct CRC evidence, indirect oncology evidence, mechanistic evidence, or unresolved—not as interchangeable efficacy claims. [114][105][106]

**Probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics**

In elective colorectal surgery, a 2022 systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of **21 randomized trials**—15 probiotic and 6 synbiotic trials—found fewer overall infectious complications with probiotics/synbiotics than control (**RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.47–0.75; 12 trials**), fewer surgical-site infections (**RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.52–0.95; 11 trials**), pulmonary infections (**RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.20–0.63; 10 trials**), and urinary infections (**RR 0.41, 95% CI 0.19–0.87; 6 trials**). Anastomotic leakage (**RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.47–1.48; 11 trials**) and wound infection (**RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.53–1.03; 11 trials**) were not significantly reduced. [114] These outcomes support a perioperative infection advantage, but not a general CRC-treatment response advantage.

The proposed clinical mechanism is reduced overgrowth of potentially pathogenic bacteria, preservation of the mucosal barrier, and modulation of local immunity; it remains difficult to attribute these effects to a single strain or metabolite. The trials were heterogeneous: 8 used preoperative treatment, 10 used both pre- and postoperative treatment, and 3 used postoperative treatment; preoperative exposure lasted **3–14 days** and postoperative exposure **2–21 days**, with one trial continuing for one year. One-third of trials used one strain, one-third two strains, and one-third three strains. [114] A four-probiotic randomized study reported fewer postoperative complications, whereas a 2012 colon-resection study found that *Lactobacillus plantarum* 299v did not reduce enteric bacteria or bacterial translocation, illustrating mixed strain-specific results. [114]

For the requested Future Outlook classification, combined **probiotics/prebiotics**, separate **probiotics**, separate **prebiotics**, and adjacent **synbiotics** should remain distinct modalities. Prebiotic-only CRC evidence is presently sparse in the cited material: a rectal-cancer prebiotic study (**NCT05516641**) and a prebiotic-mixture study during abdominal radiotherapy (**NCT01549782**) are listed without outcome results. Similarly, listed studies of oral probiotics for pelvic-radiation enteritis (**NCT05032027**), probiotics for irinotecan-induced diarrhea (**NCT01410955**), synbiotics for irradiated rectal-cancer mucosa (**NCT03420443**), and dietary microbiota modification around CRC surgery (**NCT04869956**) do not supply results in the cited review. [105] A separate meta-analysis of **26 randomized trials involving 1,720 people** reported metabolic effects of probiotics, including reductions in body weight and BMI, but the population was not identified as CRC patients and long-term leptin and adiponectin effects remained unknown; this is indirect evidence, not CRC-treatment efficacy evidence. [106]

The main development priorities are standardized strain or substrate selection, dose, timing, duration, microbiota endpoints, and prespecified clinical outcomes. Baseline microbial variation makes identical products biologically inconsistent between patients, so adequately powered trials should test personalization rather than assume a universal formulation. Probiotics or synbiotics should not be incorporated into enhanced-recovery pathways as a uniform CRC standard until formulation and timing are resolved. [114][105]

## Surgery: preventing infection and supporting anastomotic healing

### Anastomotic leakage has a plausible microbial component

**Leakage risk varies by operation.** A 2023 systematic review reported anastomotic leakage in approximately 2–15% of colorectal operations overall, with ranges of 2–5% for right-colon anastomoses and 10–15% for rectal anastomoses. Its 15 observational human studies included 52,945 patients, of whom 1,747 experienced leakage; collagenase-producing bacteria were identified in patients with leakage in 10 studies. [6]

The distinction between colon and rectal surgery matters clinically. A microbial intervention with the same relative effect would produce a larger absolute benefit in a higher-risk rectal population. However, the observed differences in baseline leakage also reflect operative anatomy, tissue perfusion, surgical technique, and prior treatment; they should not be attributed to microbiota alone.

**Bacterial function matters more than presence.** Experimental work implicates *E. faecalis* through two complementary activities: direct degradation of collagen and activation of host matrix metalloproteinase-9, or MMP9, an enzyme capable of breaking down extracellular matrix. Both processes can weaken the tissue supporting a new bowel connection. A preliminary survey of 64 bacterial strains isolated from human anastomotic tissue found the combined collagen-degrading/MMP9 phenotype in *E. faecalis* and *P. aeruginosa*. [4]

This provides a more useful explanation than simply labeling a species “harmful.” A strain’s capacity to express tissue-damaging functions, and the conditions that activate those functions, may determine its effect. The same organism can behave differently in an intact bowel and in tissue exposed to surgical stress, ischemia, or radiation-associated signals.

**Standard antibiotics do not cover every mechanism.** In the rat experiments summarized in the colorectal-surgery review, parenteral cefoxitin neither eliminated *E. faecalis* nor prevented leakage. That observation supports the possibility that particular microbial functions escape otherwise appropriate prophylaxis. It does not establish that additional or prolonged antibiotics prevent human anastomotic failure. [4]

**C. difficile adds a clinical association.** The 2023 review summarized a large case–control study in which leakage occurred in 3.52% of patients with *Clostridioides difficile* infection, versus 1.44% without it; the review’s table reported an odds ratio of 2.39, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.70–3.36. This connects a clinically recognizable intestinal infection with impaired postoperative outcomes, but remains an observational association. [6]

The competing explanations are important: infection could impair healing, while the clinical circumstances accompanying a leak could also promote infection or its detection. The resulting judgment is that microbial activity belongs in the biology of leakage, alongside technical and host factors, rather than replacing them.

### Beneficial microbial metabolism may improve healing

**Butyrate supports repair in experimental models.** Butyrate is a short-chain fatty acid produced by microbial fermentation of dietary substrates. In rat colonic-resection models, postoperative butyrate enemas increased anastomotic strength, with increased collagen synthesis and maturation proposed as explanations. Short-chain fatty acids also inhibited *P. aeruginosa* growth in vitro. [4]

These experiments connect microbial metabolism to a concrete surgical property: the strength of the healing bowel wall. Their practical implication is that preserving repair-supporting functions may be as relevant as suppressing pathogens. Increased bursting strength in an animal anastomosis, however, is a different endpoint from reducing clinically diagnosed leakage in patients.

**Diet changed the postoperative microbial phenotype.** In a mouse model summarized in the 2023 surgical-bioresilience review, six weeks of a high-fat, low-fiber Western diet increased leakage and favored *Enterococcus*. During the first postoperative week, collagenase-producing *Enterococcus* represented 65–90% of the community in Western-diet mice, compared with 4–15% in standard-chow mice. Switching to standard chow two days before surgery reduced leakage. [5]

The contrast suggests that microbial ecology can change the probability of a tissue-damaging phenotype rather than merely changing total bacterial numbers. It supports investigation of nutrition and fermentable substrates around surgery, but the mouse timing should not be translated into a two-day human dietary prescription.

### Bowel preparation illustrates a genuine benefit–cost trade-off

**Adding oral antibiotics reduced infections.** In the seven-RCT meta-analysis summarized in the 2016 surgical review, oral antibiotics added to mechanical bowel preparation and systemic prophylaxis reduced total surgical-site infection from 16.0% to 7.2%. The calculated absolute reduction was 8.8 percentage points, from 16.0 − 7.2, and the calculated relative reduction was 55%, from 1 − 7.2/16.0. [4]

That is clinically meaningful human intervention evidence. Nevertheless, the comparison tested the addition of oral antibiotics to a preparation strategy that already included mechanical cleansing and systemic antibiotics. It does not establish that every component independently contributes the same benefit, or that mechanical cleansing alone is protective.

**Mechanical cleansing alone is a different intervention.** Randomized trials repeatedly failed to demonstrate an independent reduction in infection or leakage from mechanical bowel preparation alone. One proposed explanation for the success of combined preparation is that reducing fecal bulk improves delivery of oral antibiotics to the mucosal surface. [4]

This distinction prevents two opposite misinterpretations: that bowel preparation is universally ineffective, or that physically removing bowel contents fully explains infection prevention. The evidence instead supports a treatment-specific interaction between luminal contents, microbial exposure, and antimicrobial prophylaxis.

**Microbial disruption can persist beyond surgery.** A separate human experiment involving 22 participants found that five days of neomycin, vancomycin, and metronidazole reduced bacterial load approximately 10,000-fold and produced diversity changes lasting more than six months. That experiment was not a trial of a standard short colorectal-surgery regimen, so its magnitude should not be assigned directly to routine prophylaxis. [5]

A longitudinal pilot also found that community recovery began much later after colorectal surgery with surgical preparation and antibiotics than after colonoscopy preparation alone: mean 31 days, versus three days. Because the surgical group experienced an operation and several accompanying interventions, this comparison describes the overall perioperative disturbance rather than an isolated antibiotic effect. [30]

**Resistance changes the prevention problem.** Registry data covering 288,458 colorectal procedures indicated that 43% of surgical-site infections involved pathogens resistant to standard cefazolin/metronidazole prophylaxis. The denominator is infections, not all operations, and the finding does not show that oral preparation caused that resistance. [5]

The practical judgment is to preserve effective infection prevention while avoiding unnecessary antimicrobial exposure. Microbiome concerns do not justify withholding indicated prophylaxis or treatment of an infection; they strengthen the case for appropriate regimen selection, timing, and stewardship.

### Perioperative probiotics show benefits, but not a uniform class effect

**The aggregate infection signal is favorable.** A meta-analysis of 14 RCTs involving 1,566 patients reported a 37% relative reduction in postoperative infection with perioperative probiotics, attenuating to 18% after adjustment for publication bias. The latter estimate suggests that the direction of benefit persisted while its apparent magnitude became smaller. [5]

The 2024 CRC supplementation review provides a useful counterbalance: of 14 studies assessing infectious complications, nine reported reductions, but two of those reductions were not statistically significant, and five studies found no difference. Thus, a favorable average does not mean that all products, operations, or patients benefit. [1]

**Barrier measurements are informative but intermediate.** Two Liu trials reported bacterial translocation to mesenteric lymph nodes of 18% versus 28%, and 13% versus 28%, favoring probiotics. Bacterial translocation means that organisms cross the intestinal barrier into tissues or lymphatic compartments. Reducing it is biologically attractive, but improved permeability or microbial engraftment does not automatically translate into fewer leaks, shorter hospitalization, or lower mortality. The strongest surgical product would therefore demonstrate both a plausible barrier effect and a reproducible improvement in patient outcomes.

**The surgical takeaway is selective use.** Infection prevention has stronger human intervention support than microbiota-directed prevention of anastomotic leakage. A defined probiotic or synbiotic regimen would gain clinical importance if replicated trials demonstrated fewer meaningful complications without excess infection or other harm; the current evidence does not support treating all supplements as interchangeable.

## Chemotherapy: resistance, drug metabolism, and intestinal toxicity

### 5-FU and oxaliplatin resistance can involve bacterial signaling

**F. nucleatum has experimentally supported resistance mechanisms.** Yu and colleagues’ 2017 study showed that *F. nucleatum* reduced apoptosis—the programmed death of damaged cells—after 5-FU or oxaliplatin exposure in CRC cell models. Its effects involved TLR4–MYD88 signaling, reduced expression of the regulatory microRNAs miR-18a-star and miR-4802, and increased ULK1/ATG7-dependent autophagy. [10]

Autophagy is a cellular recycling process that can help stressed tumor cells survive. The proposed sequence was therefore not simply bacterial destruction of the drug: microbial signals changed how cancer cells responded to treatment-induced damage. Knockdown of TLR4 or MYD88, interference with autophagy, or restoration of the relevant microRNAs reversed resistance in experimental systems.

**Intervention experiments strengthen the mechanistic case.** In one SW480 cell comparison, an oxaliplatin concentration of 109.9 micromoles per liter produced 50% apoptosis in parental cells, versus 35.5% in parental cells cocultured with *F. nucleatum*. The corresponding half-maximal effective concentration increased from 109.9 to 177.2 micromoles per liter. [10]

These are laboratory measurements of altered drug sensitivity, not patient resistance rates. Their importance lies in the accompanying rescue experiments: blocking the proposed pathway restored chemotherapy sensitivity, which is stronger mechanistic evidence than observing bacterial enrichment alone.

**Patient recurrence associations support clinical relevance.** In an additional 173-patient cohort within the same study, the high-*F. nucleatum* group had a recurrence rate of 73.4%, compared with 30.9% in the low-abundance group. Higher bacterial abundance was also associated with shorter recurrence-free survival. [10]

That result makes the mechanism clinically plausible, but it does not establish that removing the organism would reduce recurrence by the observed difference. Bacterial abundance could partly mark aggressive tumor biology or a favorable niche created by the tumor. This is the distinction between a prognostic association and an effective therapeutic target.

**Several host pathways may contribute.** Separate work summarized in the chemotherapy literature linked *F. nucleatum* to increased BIRC3 expression, an antiapoptotic pathway that promotes 5-FU resistance. This complements, rather than replaces, the autophagy mechanism: microbial exposure may protect tumor cells through more than one survival program. [2]

**A 2024 study added intercellular transfer.** In a 40-patient cohort receiving first-line FOLFOX- or XELOX-based therapy, plasma hsa_circ_0004085 was higher in the 18 patients with progressive disease than in the 22 without progression. Higher pretreatment expression was associated with shorter progression-free survival—the time before disease worsened. [32]

The experimental component showed that *F. nucleatum* induced this circular RNA and promoted its packaging into exosomes, small vesicles that transfer material between cells. In recipient CRC cells, the RNA stabilized GRP78 messenger RNA through RRBP1 and promoted ATF6-related signaling, relieving chemotherapy-induced endoplasmic-reticulum stress. This transmitted oxaliplatin/5-FU resistance in cells and xenografts.

**The clinical implication is narrower than eradication.** These findings support studying *F. nucleatum*-associated functions as resistance markers and therapeutic targets. They do not support indiscriminate antibiotic treatment of patients with positive stool or tumor tests: bacteria supporting treatment-related immunity could also be removed, and tumor-associated organisms are not equivalent to the entire fecal community.

### 5-FU can damage the microbial–epithelial barrier

**Treatment can create a damaging feedback loop.** Preclinical studies describe 5-FU-associated shifts away from some commensal organisms toward Enterobacteriaceae and other opportunistic groups. Mucosal injury, inflammatory cytokines, and increased permeability can then facilitate further microbial contact with damaged tissue, amplifying mucositis and potentially allowing bacterial translocation. [2]

Mucositis means inflammation and injury of the mucosal lining. In this setting, it is important not to assign the entire process to bacteria: 5-FU directly injures susceptible host tissues, while microbial changes can amplify or modify that injury. The relationship runs in both directions.

**Restoring composition does not guarantee restored efficacy.** In mouse CRC experiments, an antibiotic cocktail reduced 5-FU antitumor activity, but probiotic supplementation after 5-FU did not significantly improve its efficacy. That counterexample separates two claims often conflated in discussions of microbiome restoration: improving intestinal ecology and improving cancer control. [2]

By contrast, *L. plantarum* combined with 5-FU induced caspase-mediated apoptosis and inhibited Wnt/β-catenin signaling in resistant CRC cells. This demonstrates a possible sensitizing mechanism in vitro, but should not be presented as clinical reversal of resistance by a commercially available probiotic. [9]

**A historical interaction demonstrates metabolic consequences.** The sorivudine–5-FU interaction provides an unusually concrete example of microbial metabolism increasing systemic toxicity. Gut bacterial enzymes, including those expressed by several *Bacteroides* species, generated bromovinyluracil, or BVU, from sorivudine. BVU inactivated hepatic dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase, the enzyme responsible for much 5-FU clearance. [15]

Coadministration was associated with 16 deaths in Japan in the historical clinical reports reviewed by Alexander and colleagues. This illustrates how a microbial transformation can alter host drug handling dramatically; it is a specific drug–drug interaction, not an estimate of the danger of ordinary 5-FU regimens.

### Oxaliplatin benefits from some microbial immune signals

**Microbiota can support drug-induced tumor damage.** In germ-free or antibiotic-treated mouse models, oxaliplatin activity decreased alongside reduced production of reactive oxygen species by tumor-associated myeloid cells. Reactive oxygen species are chemically reactive molecules that can increase cellular damage; here, microbial immune priming helped those host cells support chemotherapy cytotoxicity. [2]

The mechanistic contrast with *F. nucleatum* is central. One microbial signal can help a cancer cell survive treatment, while another can help an immune cell intensify tumor damage. Consequently, “microbiota present” and “microbiota depleted” are biologically coarse categories, not opposing treatment recommendations.

**The timing suggests an early effect.** Alexander and colleagues noted that antibiotic-associated loss of oxaliplatin activity appeared within two days in the cited mouse experiments. That timing was consistent with suppression of early cytotoxicity, rather than depending entirely on later adaptive immune responses. [15]

Broader experimental literature also connects microbiota to T-helper-cell and other adaptive immune programs. These pathways provide plausible routes for treatment modification, but the clearest oxaliplatin example in this evidence base is microbiota-supported host-cell oxidative activity.

**Human toxicity studies identify candidate communities.** A small CAPEOX study summarized in the clinical chemotherapy review compared four patients with diarrhea against 13 without diarrhea after eight treatment cycles. Diarrhea was associated with lower within-sample diversity and enrichment of Proteobacteria, Enterobacteriaceae, and *Klebsiella*; *K. pneumoniae* represented 31.22% of the diarrhea-associated community. [12]

That percentage is relative microbial abundance, not the percentage of patients infected with *K. pneumoniae*. Moreover, the study excluded grade 3–4 diarrhea. Its value is identifying a possible ecological pattern accompanying milder toxicity, not establishing a threshold for infection or severe-diarrhea prediction.

**Toxicity protection must preserve tumor control.** In the FOLFOX/FMT mouse experiment, microbial restoration improved mucosal injury and diarrhea without a detected short-term reduction in antitumor activity. This is encouraging because a supportive intervention would be less useful if it also protected the tumor from treatment. [11]

The appropriate interpretation is “no detected loss in that short experiment,” rather than proven equivalence or long-term safety. The same dual-outcome requirement should apply to human probiotic and FMT studies.

### Irinotecan has a particularly clear microbial toxicity pathway

**Bacterial enzymes reactivate an excreted metabolite.** Irinotecan is converted to the active metabolite SN-38. Host glucuronidation then produces the less active conjugate SN-38G, which is excreted into the intestine. Bacterial β-D-glucuronidases remove the glucuronide group, releasing active SN-38 again in the bowel lumen, where it can damage intestinal tissue. [14]

This is more specific than a general association between dysbiosis and diarrhea: the microbial enzyme, substrate, product, and toxic consequence are identifiable. It also explains why intestinal toxicity can depend on microbial function even when the anticancer drug is administered systemically.

**Clinical severity gives the mechanism practical importance.** Severe irinotecan-associated diarrhea was reported in up to 40% of patients in the clinical literature summarized by Alexander and colleagues, sometimes requiring dose reduction or treatment termination. That upper-end figure describes the reviewed treatment contexts, not a universal contemporary incidence or the fraction caused exclusively by bacterial enzymes. [15]

Irinotecan can also change the organisms producing these enzymes. Experimental studies describe increases in β-glucuronidase-producing groups, including *E. coli* and other Enterobacteriaceae, potentially creating a feedback loop between treatment-related ecological disruption and toxic metabolite reactivation. [2]

**Human composition studies remain small.** A 2023 study compared stools from five healthy individuals, five patients with colon cancer, and five irinotecan-treated patients. The treated group had lower richness and diversity, with differences involving *Veillonella*, *Clostridium*, *Prevotella*, *Bifidobacterium*, and other taxa. [14]

Because these were separate groups rather than a large randomized longitudinal experiment, the findings describe an associated community pattern. Measuring β-glucuronidase activity is mechanistically closer to the proposed toxic process than assuming that the abundance of one genus captures the entire pathway.

**Selective enzyme inhibition offers a focused alternative.** The experimental literature identified four potent bacterial β-glucuronidase inhibitors, with mouse studies demonstrating protection from irinotecan-associated diarrhea. The compounds described did not broadly kill bacteria or mammalian epithelial cells in the tested systems. [2]

This approach targets an undesirable microbial function while potentially preserving beneficial community functions. Its challenge is that β-glucuronidases from different organisms vary in susceptibility, so enzyme selectivity and coverage matter as much as a drug’s ability to inhibit one laboratory enzyme. [8]

**Defined probiotic mixtures also have experimental support.** In a 60-mouse study, a mixture of *L. plantarum*, *L. acidophilus*, and *L. rhamnosus* reduced irinotecan-associated diarrhea alongside lower β-glucuronidase expression and oxidative stress, and improved epithelial protection. The mixture produced stronger inhibition of the tested bacterial enzyme-expression signal than the individual strains. [14]

The mechanistic result is promising, but the mouse regimen is not a human dosing recommendation. Importantly, oxidative activity can have different consequences by location: reducing damaging intestinal oxidative stress is not the same as suppressing the tumor-associated oxidative activity supporting oxaliplatin.

### Human chemotherapy studies distinguish promising signals from consistent benefit

**FOLFIRI response associations are regimen-specific.** A metastatic-CRC cohort enrolled 110 patients receiving FOLFIRI plus cetuximab or bevacizumab and analyzed pretreatment microbial profiles in 55 patients with partial response or progressive disease: 31 and 24 patients, respectively. Greater diversity was associated with favorable response, and *F. nucleatum* was approximately 32-fold more abundant in progressive disease than partial response. [13]

However, the diversity contrast was clearer in the bevacizumab subgroup, while the cetuximab subgroup showed no significant alpha- or beta-diversity difference. Stable-disease patients were excluded from the main response comparison. The study therefore supports a candidate pretreatment association, not a universal irinotecan response test or proof that increasing diversity improves outcomes.

**Randomized supplementation results differ substantially.**

| Study and participants | Treatment context | Supplement | Diarrhea finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Österlund: 150 patients | 5-FU-based therapy | *L. rhamnosus* GG | Grade 3–4: 22% versus 37% | Favorable randomized result; unblinded |
| Mego: 46 patients | Irinotecan-containing therapy | Ten-strain mixture with inulin | Grade 3–4: 0% versus 17.4% | Difference not statistically significant |
| Zaharuddin: 14 patients | Capecitabine plus oxaliplatin | Six-strain mixture | Grade 3–4: 13% versus 17% | No significant reduction |
| Huang: 100 patients | Postoperative chemotherapy | Four-species preparation | Overall diarrhea: 16% versus 40% | Favorable product-specific result |

The trial findings are summarized in the CRC supplementation review [1] and the probiotic gastrointestinal-toxicity meta-analysis [33].

**Trial design affects the apparent contrast.** The Österlund study reported less severe diarrhea, hospitalization, and bowel-toxicity-related dose reduction, but lacked blinding and a placebo control; some participants also received pelvic radiotherapy. The Mego trial was double-blind but stopped after recruiting 46 of 220 planned patients, limiting precision. [9]

A nonsignificant 0% versus 17.4% comparison should therefore not be described as proof of either strong protection or no biological effect. It is a favorable but imprecise result. Conversely, positive results from one formulation should not be generalized to another: the CRC review also described kefir studies without protection against gastrointestinal complaints or mucositis.

**Pooled estimates require endpoint-specific interpretation.** In an eight-RCT meta-analysis involving 697 participants, the low-risk-of-bias subset contained 114 participants and estimated a risk ratio of 0.22 for grade 3–4 diarrhea, but the 95% confidence interval was 0.05–1.08 and the result did not reach conventional statistical significance. The overall-diarrhea analysis across studies was also nonsignificant, with a risk ratio of 0.93. [33]

The CRC subgroup was more favorable, with a reported risk ratio of 0.56 and 95% confidence interval of 0.34–0.92. Differences between populations, endpoints, and included studies explain why a positive subgroup and an inconclusive overall estimate can coexist. They do not establish a single class-wide probiotic effect.

**The chemotherapy takeaway is function-specific modulation.** Irinotecan’s β-glucuronidase pathway is the most concrete microbial drug-toxicity mechanism here, while *F. nucleatum* resistance and microbiota-supported oxaliplatin activity offer strong experimental explanations. Clinical benefit should be judged separately for tumor control, diarrhea, mucositis, and treatment completion; improvement in one does not establish improvement in all.

## Immunotherapy: microbial immune priming and immune-related toxicity

### Tumor biology remains the established clinical starting point

**Mismatch-repair status is not a microbial marker.** Approximately 15% of CRCs overall are microsatellite-instability-high or mismatch-repair-deficient, abbreviated MSI-H/dMMR. These tumors have shown substantial sensitivity to checkpoint blockade, whereas mismatch-repair-proficient or microsatellite-stable tumors—pMMR/MSS—have generally been much less responsive to checkpoint monotherapy. [34]

Microbiota may help explain variation within these groups, but should not displace their established clinical importance. A favorable microbial profile does not by itself show that an MSS tumor has become sensitive to a checkpoint inhibitor.

**High pathological response is not microbiota attribution.** The early NICHE-2 results summarized by Sillo and colleagues reported a 95% major pathological response and 67% pathological complete response in 112 patients with dMMR disease receiving neoadjuvant checkpoint treatment. These findings concern a combination checkpoint approach, not probiotic treatment or anti-PD-1 monotherapy. [34]

A pathological response measures what remains in the surgically assessed tumor after treatment. It differs from an imaging response in metastatic disease. Neither endpoint identifies the proportion of benefit caused by gut microbes unless microbiota are themselves evaluated as a treatment determinant.

### Anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 response signals are promising but heterogeneous

**The CRC human evidence is relatively small.** Ajab and colleagues’ systematic review, published in July 2024, included four records: three studies and one conference abstract. Some were mixed-cancer cohorts with CRC subgroups as small as five or 19 patients, rather than four large CRC-only microbiome trials. [3]

The recurring candidates included *A. muciniphila*, *E. rectale*, and members of Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Prevotellaceae. Their recurrence across reports makes them reasonable candidates for validation, but the study designs support associations with response rather than clinical benefit from administering those organisms.

**Functional convergence may be more informative.** In responder-associated microbial profiles, computational pathway analyses identified functions related to short-chain fatty acids, vitamins, and other metabolic processes. Poor-response profiles showed more predicted activity involving lipopolysaccharide production and sugar catabolism. [3]

Short-chain fatty acids, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are microbial metabolites that can affect epithelial and immune-cell behavior. However, a pathway predicted from microbial sequencing is not the same measurement as the concentration of a metabolite reaching the tumor or a T cell. Functional predictions identify plausible mechanisms, not measured metabolite delivery.

**The pooled gastrointestinal cohort supports the association.** In a 74-patient gastrointestinal-cancer study, response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment was associated with a higher *Prevotella/Bacteroides* ratio and enrichment of selected Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae. Metagenomic analyses also associated response with short-chain-fatty-acid-capable organisms, including *Eubacterium*, *Lactobacillus*, and *Streptococcus*. [18]

This is relevant to anti-PD-L1 as well as anti-PD-1 research, but the pooled treatment and cancer categories must remain visible. It supports a biological hypothesis across gastrointestinal cancers, not an agent-specific CRC response percentage.

**A “beneficial genus” is not universal.** Although bifidobacteria enhanced checkpoint activity in several experimental settings, one CRC responder analysis found more *Bifidobacterium* in nonresponders. Some Ruminococcaceae and *Bacteroides* groups also appeared on both sides of response comparisons. [3]

The implication is not that all microbial findings cancel each other out. It is that strain-level functions, ecological partners, treatment context, and host biology can be more important than a genus-level label. A supplement marketed around a broadly favorable genus does not reproduce the community or function associated with response in a study.

**CRC mouse experiments support causality within models.** In CT26 tumor-bearing mice, an ampicillin–streptomycin–colistin background produced poor anti-PD-1 activity compared with intact-community controls. Different antibiotic backgrounds produced different response patterns, consistent with the importance of community composition rather than total bacterial depletion alone. [17]

Responder-derived FMT also enhanced checkpoint activity in CRC models, with proposed changes in effector-memory CD8 T cells and immune signaling. These experiments strengthen the hypothesis that microbial communities can modify treatment response, but an MSS-labeled mouse model is not proof that FMT overcomes checkpoint resistance in human MSS CRC. [16]

### Anti-CTLA-4 and combination treatment require separate interpretation

**Microbial dependence extends beyond PD-1.** In cited sarcoma models, CTLA-4-specific antibodies controlled tumors in mice with intact microbiota but not in germ-free or broad-spectrum-antibiotic-treated mice. In melanoma models, selected microbiota and bifidobacterial supplementation enhanced anti-PD-L1-associated antitumor immunity, with live bacteria and CD8 T cells required for the observed effect. [15]

These are informative demonstrations of microbial immune priming, but their populations matter: sarcoma and melanoma experiments are indirect evidence for CRC. They support a mechanism to test in CRC, not a colorectal clinical effect size.

**Combination-treatment microbiomes have their own signals.** In the CheckMate 142 substudy summarized by Ajab and colleagues, baseline *Rothia* abundance was 2.7-fold lower, and *R. mucilaginosa* threefold lower, in partial responders than in patients with progressive disease receiving nivolumab plus ipilimumab. [3]

That is directly relevant to a CRC checkpoint combination. Nevertheless, it does not show that selectively reducing *Rothia* improves response. The organism could be a marker of a broader ecological state, medication exposure, or host condition that influences outcomes.

**Microbial priming has a plausible cellular route.** Experimental work with *L. rhamnosus* GG linked enhanced anti-PD-1 activity to dendritic-cell activation, type I interferon production, and improved cross-priming of antitumor CD8 T cells. Dendritic cells help present antigens to T cells; cross-priming enables tumor-killing cells to respond to material acquired from other cells. [22]

This is a mechanistic rationale for defined microbial adjuvants, not evidence that increasing immune activity everywhere is desirable. The intestinal compartment must retain enough tolerance to avoid colitis while the tumor compartment develops effective immunity.

### Treatment response and intestinal inflammation can move together

**CTLA-4-containing therapy carries greater colitis risk.** A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of nine metastatic-CRC studies found more colitis with anti-CTLA-4 and combination treatment than with PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy. That comparison concerns treatment toxicity; it does not quantify how much of the difference is caused by microbiota. [21]

The distinction is clinically consequential. Even if a microbial intervention enhances checkpoint response, its value depends partly on whether it also changes the risk of treatment interruption, hospitalization, or severe bowel inflammation.

**Non-CRC studies reveal an efficacy–toxicity tension.** In metastatic melanoma, CTLA-4-related colitis was reported in approximately one-third of ipilimumab-treated patients in the literature reviewed by Alexander and colleagues. Greater Bacteroidetes representation was associated with protection in some studies, whereas selected Firmicutes, including *Faecalibacterium*, were associated with colitis in another cohort. [15][22]

Some organisms associated with strong antitumor responses may therefore also mark greater inflammatory susceptibility. This argues against a single “optimal immunotherapy microbiome” defined only by response, without evaluating adverse effects.

**CRC-inclusive cohorts provide a more relevant toxicity proxy.** A prospective study of 95 gastrointestinal-cancer patients included 33 patients with CRC and examined PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition, with or without CTLA-4 blockade. Forty-four developed an immune-related adverse event: a calculated mixed-cohort proportion of 46.3%, from 44 ÷ 95. This measures any immune-related adverse event across pooled treatments, not CRC-specific anti-PD-L1 colitis. [19]

Median time to the first event was 27 days, and 35 of the 44 affected patients developed events within 12 weeks. These findings establish the relevance of early toxicity monitoring, while the microbial analysis addresses candidate predictors rather than a clinically validated screening test.

**Overall diversity did not explain toxicity.** The same cohort found no significant alpha- or beta-diversity difference between patients with and without immune-related adverse events. Individual taxa and functions differed instead; *Ruminococcus callidus* and *Bacteroides xylanisolvens* were enriched among patients without severe events, while *B. stercoris* was enriched in the high-grade-event CRC subgroup. [19]

This is a counterexample to the idea that simply increasing diversity prevents immune toxicity. It also shows why all-cancer signatures should not be transferred automatically into a CRC subgroup: the relevant associations can differ by cancer and toxicity severity.

**Integrated models show progress beyond single taxa.** A 2024 *Genome Medicine* analysis combined published data from 317 patients with in-house data from 115 patients and developed a 14-feature classifier distinguishing immune-related adverse events from none, with a reported area under the curve of 0.88. Discrimination was also evaluated in independent cohorts. [35]

Functional work linked the non-adverse-event profiles to increased menaquinone biosynthesis and higher serum menaquinone, a vitamin K2-related compound. These findings strengthen the case for combined microbial and metabolite measurements, but do not constitute evidence that menaquinone supplementation safely prevents CRC checkpoint toxicity.

**The immunotherapy takeaway is dual optimization.** Microbiota are plausible modifiers of checkpoint response and colitis, but a successful intervention must improve their balance. The decisive evidence would be a reproducible CRC-specific intervention benefit that preserves tumor control while reducing clinically important toxicity, rather than reproducing a favorable taxonomic signature alone.

## Radiotherapy: tumor-response signals and gastrointestinal injury

### Clinical bowel injury is clear; microbial attribution is more complex

**Concurrent chemotherapy increases the clinical burden.** In the EORTC 22921 rectal-cancer trial summarized in the pelvic-radiotherapy review, grade 2 or higher diarrhea occurred in 17% of patients receiving preoperative radiotherapy at 45 gray in 25 fractions, versus 34% with concurrent 5-FU. The calculated absolute increase was 17 percentage points, and the observed proportion doubled. [24]

Those are clinical treatment-toxicity findings, not estimates of a microbiota-mediated effect. They show why microbial protection is worth investigating, but also why studies must distinguish the effects of radiation, chemotherapy, and their combination.

**Acute and late injury are distinct endpoints.** Acute diarrhea often develops during pelvic treatment, whereas late enteropathy can emerge months to years later and include persistent diarrhea, bleeding, ulceration, or narrowing of the bowel. The pelvic-radiotherapy literature commonly reports diarrhea in approximately 30–50% of patients, with higher burdens in some treatment contexts. [24]

Consequently, an intervention that reduces diarrhea during treatment has not necessarily prevented chronic radiation injury. It is equally important to distinguish symptom improvement from improved tumor response: less diarrhea may help patients complete therapy, but it is not itself evidence of greater tumor radiosensitivity.

### Diversity findings do not support one universal radiation signature

**Earlier reviews often found declining diversity.** The 2021 *Radiation Oncology* review included eight studies, mostly involving gynecological or prostate cancer, and reported reduced microbial diversity during or after pelvic radiotherapy, especially among patients with diarrhea. A second 2021 review covering 13 studies found more divergent diversity and taxonomic patterns. [24][36]

**The 2024 rectal study complicates that pattern.** Sixteen postoperative stage II/III rectal-cancer patients were compared, eight with acute radiation enteritis and eight without it. After 15 radiotherapy sessions, at a cumulative dose of 27–30 gray, the enteritis group showed greater richness by several indices and greater between-community differences, while Shannon diversity did not differ. [26]

| Evidence | Population | Within-sample finding | Community differences | Clinical relationship |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 eight-study review | Mainly non-CRC pelvic cancers | Diversity generally decreased | Composition varied | Greater decline with diarrhea |
| 2021 thirteen-study review | Mixed pelvic cancers | Inconsistent directions | Taxonomic findings diverged | Changes associated with diarrhea |
| Wang, 2019 | 18 cervical-cancer patients | Lower diversity with enteritis | Greater heterogeneity | More severe injury linked to disruption |
| 2024 rectal study | 16 rectal-cancer patients | Higher richness; unchanged Shannon index | Greater differences with enteritis | Acute enteritis association |

The comparisons come from the pelvic-radiotherapy reviews [24], Wang’s cervical-cancer study [7], and the 2024 rectal study [26].

**Richness and diversity are not synonyms.** Richness measures how many microbial types are detected. Shannon diversity also incorporates their distribution, while beta diversity describes differences between communities. A rise in detected types can therefore coexist with no significant change in Shannon diversity.

The 2024 result challenges a simple “lower diversity means enteritis” rule, rather than proving that greater diversity is harmful. Sampling time, the specific index, and whether patients have already undergone surgery all affect the biological comparison. The practical judgment is to favor reproducible functional or composite signatures over a universal diversity cutoff.

### Barrier injury and bile metabolism provide plausible mechanisms

**Radiation and dysbiosis can amplify each other.** Radiation directly damages intestinal epithelium and provokes inflammatory responses. The resulting changes in mucus, permeability, and the local environment can favor different microbes; those communities can then intensify barrier disruption and inflammation. [24]

Wang and colleagues supplied an experimental link by exposing colonic epithelial monolayers to microbiota from patients with severe radiation enteritis. Compared with non-enteritis microbiota, these preparations reduced tight-junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin, increased permeability, activated NF-κB signaling, and stimulated TNF-α and IL-1β inflammatory responses. [7]

**The experimental direction is informative.** These epithelial experiments suggest that the microbial community accompanying enteritis can worsen barrier behavior. They do not establish whether the original patient dysbiosis preceded radiation injury or developed because of it, and the donors had cervical rather than colorectal cancer. The experiment therefore supports a transferable inflammatory mechanism without providing a CRC clinical treatment effect.

**Bile-salt processing may influence diarrhea.** The pelvic-radiotherapy review proposed that loss or alteration of bile-salt-hydrolyzing organisms, including *Bacteroides* and *Lactobacillus*, can disturb intestinal bile metabolism and contribute to diarrhea. Radiation-associated bile-acid malabsorption may interact with that microbial change. [24]

However, another pelvic review associated increased *Bacteroides* abundance with diarrhea and enteritis. That apparent contradiction underscores the distinction between a genus and its functions: total *Bacteroides* abundance does not measure the precise bile-processing activity of the whole community. [36]

**Radiation can also affect later surgical healing.** In a rat model, radiation-associated host signals induced an anastomotic-disrupting *P. aeruginosa* phenotype, involving tight-junction disruption and epithelial destruction. This connects the radiotherapy and surgery sections mechanistically, particularly for rectal cancer treated before resection. [4]

It remains distinct from the human diarrhea evidence. An organism’s capacity to damage a later anastomosis should not be treated as a measured clinical cause of radiation enteritis.

### Rectal chemoradiotherapy has an exploratory response signature

**Preserved diversity accompanied favorable response.** A prospective study followed 39 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer receiving 50 gray in 25 fractions plus capecitabine. Across 98 fecal samples collected before, during, and after treatment, poor responders showed a decline in alpha diversity from baseline to treatment, whereas good responders retained more stable Shannon and inverse-Simpson diversity. [27]

The study combined baseline *Clostridium sensu stricto* 1 abundance, change in *Intestinimonas*, blood HVEM/CD270, and lymphocyte counts into a response model. Its internally validated area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve was 0.821, and area under the precision–recall curve was 0.911.

**Prediction is not a treatment effect.** Those performance values describe discrimination between good and poor responders, not the percentage of patients whose tumors responded because of microbiota. Moreover, the signature includes microbial change during treatment, so it is not solely a pretreatment decision tool.

A changing microbiome could contribute to response, reflect treatment exposure, or follow changes in inflammation and nutrition as the tumor responds. The clinical value would depend on whether the signature adds useful information beyond established assessments and changes management beneficially.

### Probiotic protection concerns mainly acute symptoms

**Pooled acute-diarrhea results are favorable.** A publicly posted 2020 systematic review of 23 dietary-supplement RCTs, involving 1,919 patients across pelvic malignancies, reported an acute-diarrhea risk ratio of 0.71 for probiotics and 0.45 for synbiotics versus placebo. The respective 95% confidence intervals were 0.52–0.99 and 0.28–0.73. The 23 trials covered several supplement categories, not 23 trials exclusively of probiotics. [25]

These subgroup estimates do not establish that synbiotics are superior to probiotics, because they are not a direct randomized head-to-head comparison. Other reviews reached less favorable conclusions, making the identity of the product and treatment population important.

**Rectal-specific results were less decisive.** In the small Farshi Radvar trial, 19 patients received synbiotics and 19 placebo during neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Several symptoms moved favorably, but the principal between-group quality-of-life and symptom comparisons did not reach statistical significance. [1]

The broader pelvic meta-analysis also contained no included trials reporting late gastrointestinal side effects. Its quantified benefits therefore concern acute treatment symptoms, not proven prevention of chronic radiation enteropathy. [25]

**The radiotherapy takeaway is endpoint precision.** Microbiota are associated with intestinal toxicity and show exploratory links to tumor response, but acute diarrhea, chronic enteropathy, microbial disruption, and tumor regression must be assessed separately. A protective strategy becomes clinically persuasive when a defined product improves a meaningful bowel outcome without compromising treatment delivery or cancer control.

## Future Outlook: probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT

### Probiotics and prebiotics are more clinically developed for supportive care

**The largest CRC synthesis is predominantly surgical.** Moreira and colleagues’ May 2024 review included 24 RCTs and 2,204 participants: 1,139 in intervention groups and 1,065 controls. Seventeen studies concerned surgery and seven chemotherapy; one of the seven also involved chemoradiotherapy, so it should not be counted as a separate twenty-fifth study. None evaluated supplementation during CRC immunotherapy. [1]

The main human signals concerned postoperative complications, bowel recovery, antibiotic use, and diarrhea. This supports supportive-care development more directly than claims that supplementation increases cancer response or survival.

**The three approaches should remain distinct.**

| Approach | What is administered | Proposed microbial effect | Most relevant CRC evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Probiotics | Selected live microorganisms | Barrier support, pathogen competition, immune modulation | Several perioperative and diarrhea RCTs |
| Prebiotics | Selectively utilized substrates | Support resident fermentation and metabolite production | Mechanistic and smaller perioperative studies |
| Synbiotics | Microorganisms plus supporting substrates | Combine community supplementation with substrate support | Perioperative RCTs and selected toxicity trials |

Definitions and clinical applications are described in the CRC intervention review [1] and the colon-cancer drug–microbiota review [8].

**Barrier-supporting networks offer a plausible explanation.** A 2021 review of six clinical RCTs involving 457 adults with CRC described microbial shifts and improvements in permeability or tight-junction-related outcomes after selected probiotic or synbiotic interventions. In one study, *Bifidobacterium lactis* Bl-04 plus *L. acidophilus* NCFM was associated with increased butyrate-producing Clostridiales and *Faecalibacterium*. [28]

The distinction is that a preparation may support a network containing butyrate producers; it should not be assumed that every administered *Lactobacillus* or *Bifidobacterium* strain itself produces butyrate. Community interactions and available substrates can influence the downstream metabolite effect.

**Prebiotic evidence should not borrow probiotic certainty.** Inulin, fructooligosaccharides, galactooligosaccharides, pectin, and resistant starch appeared in the reviewed interventions. Their biological rationale is to supply substrates supporting particular microbial activities, including short-chain-fatty-acid production. Yet benefits observed with a combined synbiotic product do not isolate the effect of its prebiotic component. [1]

A randomized comparison summarized in the earlier clinical literature found no significant difference in postoperative course or complications among prebiotic, synbiotic, and standard bowel-preparation groups. This is useful counterevidence against treating fermentable substrates as universally protective. [9]

**Checkpoint-colitis prevention remains a hypothesis.** Dietary fiber may promote fermentation products and regulatory immune functions, but the reviewed literature did not establish prebiotics as a preventive treatment for immune-checkpoint colitis. This is a separate question from whether a fiber-rich diet supports general microbial metabolism. [22]

Likewise, supplementation findings from melanoma or lung cancer should not be converted into CRC efficacy estimates. The mechanistic rationale may transfer, but the tumor, checkpoint regimen, baseline community, and clinically relevant outcome still need to match.

### Product identity and patient vulnerability govern safety

**Live microbes are not automatically harmless.** The 2024 CRC review reported no major intervention-related adverse reactions in its included trials, while broader cancer-treatment literature described septic complications associated with probiotics. These observations are compatible: reassuring outcomes in selected trial populations do not eliminate infection risk in more vulnerable patients. [1][7]

Particular attention is warranted when intestinal barriers are damaged or immunity is suppressed. The clinically relevant assessment concerns the exact organism, strain, product quality, patient, and timing—not the reassuring general label “probiotic.”

**Organism names alone do not establish safety.** Some studied formulations included species also discussed elsewhere as opportunistic pathogens or tissue-damaging organisms. This reinforces the distinction between a species name and a defined strain with demonstrated properties. A successful trial of a particular preparation is not permission to substitute an unrelated product containing a similarly named organism.

For translation, the useful question is whether a reproducible product improves a specified outcome at an acceptable risk. “Improving the microbiome” is too broad an endpoint to establish patient benefit.

**Timing may be part of the effect.** Among the 17 surgical RCTs in Moreira’s review, six administered supplementation before surgery, eight both before and after, and three only afterward. Those schedules expose the intervention to different biological circumstances: preoperative conditioning, the immediate injury period, or postoperative recovery. [1]

This helps explain why one favorable postoperative regimen cannot define the optimal preoperative regimen. Establishing readiness requires matching the tested product and schedule to the clinical purpose, rather than recommending a broad microbial category indefinitely.

### FMT provides broader community transfer, with greater complexity

**CRC mouse results show toxicity protection.** In the 2020 CT26/FOLFOX study, donor preparations at 50 or 150 milligrams of fecal material per milliliter were administered orally at 200 microliters daily during treatment and for two days afterward. On day six, diarrhea scores were 2.0 and 2.1 with the two FMT preparations, compared with 2.8 with FOLFOX alone. [11]

Histological and barrier findings accompanied the symptom change: FMT improved villus architecture, goblet-cell-related measures, and the tight-junction marker ZO-1. It also reduced FOLFOX-associated activation of TLR–MyD88–NF-κB inflammatory signaling and interleukin-6 responses.

**Short-term tumor inhibition was retained.** The investigators detected no significant difference in FOLFOX antitumor activity with either FMT concentration, with reported comparison P values of 0.80 and 0.94. This supports the possibility of separating intestinal protection from loss of antitumor effect in that model. [11]

It is not a formal demonstration of equivalence, and it does not show improved human survival. The result nevertheless identifies the appropriate translational standard: microbiota-directed supportive care should be assessed against tumor control as well as bowel symptoms.

**The same experiment contained safety signals.** Although bloodstream cultures were negative at necropsy, higher-dose FMT and some chemotherapy-containing groups had increased culture-based bacterial translocation compared with saline controls. The apparently protective intervention therefore still required dose- and treatment-context-specific safety assessment. [11]

This is especially relevant to patients receiving cytotoxic chemotherapy, whose mucosal injury may create opportunities for donor organisms to cross the barrier. Absence of detected bacteremia in a short experiment does not establish absence of clinically important transmission risk.

**CRC clinical development had already begun.** The 2023 RENMIN-215 publication described an open-label, single-arm phase II study combining FMT, tislelizumab, and fruquintinib in refractory MSS metastatic CRC. Its existence is important: clinical FMT development in CRC was not restricted to animal models by the September 2024 cutoff. [29]

At the same time, a single-arm combination cannot isolate the contribution of FMT from the accompanying anticancer agents. The appropriate interpretation is clinical testing of a microbiota-containing strategy, not proof that donor-community transfer alone reverses MSS checkpoint resistance.

**Other human signals come from different settings.**

| Study or setting | Population | Reported finding | Design |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baruch, 2021 | 10 anti-PD-1-refractory melanoma patients | Three clinical responses after FMT plus anti-PD-1 | Early-phase, uncontrolled |
| Davar, 2021 | 15 anti-PD-1-refractory melanoma patients | Clinical benefit reported in six recipients | Small, uncontrolled |
| Refractory checkpoint colitis, 2022 | 12 patients with advanced cancers | 83% clinical response; mild reported adverse events | Retrospective case series |
| FMT plus nivolumab, August 2024 | 10 resistant gastrointestinal-cancer patients | Objective response 20%; disease control 40% | Single-arm phase I preprint |
| Radiation-enteropathy pilot, 2020 | Five gynecological-cancer patients | At least one-grade late-toxicity improvement at eight weeks | Nonrandomized before–after study |

The reports are described in the melanoma FMT synthesis [22], checkpoint-colitis case series [20], August 2024 gastrointestinal preprint [37], and pelvic-radiotherapy review [36].

**The August preprint adds a functional signal.** In the ten-patient gastrointestinal-cancer study, the 20% objective response rate represents two responders, while 40% disease control represents four patients, calculated from the reported denominator. The two responders had progression-free intervals of 15 and more than 19 months. No serious adverse reactions were observed in that small cohort. [37]

Benefit was associated with donor-derived microbial colonization and a closely interacting butyrate-functional group of organisms. This is more informative than a list of taxa alone, but remains an association within an unpeer-reviewed, uncontrolled, non-CRC-specific combination study.

**Colitis treatment and cancer sensitization are different goals.** The 12-patient refractory-colitis series involved patients who had not responded adequately to corticosteroids and selective immunosuppression. Successful FMT responses were accompanied by increased alpha diversity and enrichment of *Bifidobacterium* and *Collinsella*. [20]

These findings support a role for community restoration in some inflammatory bowel complications of immunotherapy. They do not establish that the donor community best suited to treating colitis is also the community best suited to maximizing tumor response. The investigators themselves discussed both possibilities: dysbiosis may drive disease in some patients, while in others it may be mainly a consequence of inflammation.

**Undated context remains separate.** An undated exploratory report, considered separately from the dated September 2024 assessment, described 20 patients with lower-gastrointestinal malignancies: 15 colon, three rectal, and two appendiceal cancers. It reported diarrhea resolution in 70% after one FMT cycle, improvement in an additional 15% after two cycles, and persistent chronic diarrhea in the remaining 15%. Resolution and improvement are different endpoints, so these figures should not be combined into an 85% cure rate. [38]

**FMT safety requires more than donor diversity.** Donor screening must address transmissible organisms and undesirable microbial functions, while recipient assessment must consider damaged barriers and immune suppression. Product consistency, preparation, route, dose, engraftment, infection, and long-term effects all influence whether a community transfer is beneficial. These are clinical-development requirements, not a rationale for self-administered FMT.

The 2023 surgical-bioresilience review reported zero elective colorectal-surgery FMT trials within its evidence base. Its discussion of high efficacy for recurrent *C. difficile* infection therefore supplies an example of successful FMT in another indication, not evidence that the same intervention improves elective CRC-surgery outcomes. [5]

**The future-outlook takeaway is product-defined translation.** Probiotics and synbiotics are further developed for selected supportive-care outcomes than FMT is for CRC cancer control. Progress toward routine use depends on defined interventions demonstrating reproducible patient benefit, acceptable infection risk, and preservation of anticancer activity.

## What the evidence means for CRC care

### Evidence maturity differs by clinical question

**Supportive-care evidence is more actionable.** Human randomized studies most directly support selected infection-prevention strategies around surgery and some product-specific diarrhea outcomes. Mechanistic studies provide compelling explanations for resistance and immune modulation, but a convincing mechanism is not itself a clinical treatment recommendation.

| Treatment area | Best-supported clinical focus | Principal evidence base | Judgment through September 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surgery | Infection prevention and bowel recovery | Randomized preparation and supplementation studies | Meaningful clinical effects; regimen-specific |
| Chemotherapy | Metabolite-related toxicity and diarrhea | Pharmacology, small cohorts, mixed supplementation RCTs | Function-targeted strategies are promising |
| Immunotherapy | Response variation and immune toxicity | Small CRC cohorts plus experimental models | Microbiota complement, not replace, tumor biomarkers |
| Radiotherapy | Acute diarrhea and enteritis | Pelvic-cancer cohorts and supplementation trials | Symptom evidence exceeds tumor-response evidence |
| Future Outlook | Defined microbial adjuncts and FMT | Product-specific RCTs and exploratory FMT studies | Investigational, with efficacy and safety assessed together |

This comparison follows the CRC supplementation synthesis [1], clinical chemotherapy review [12], CRC checkpoint review [3], and pelvic-radiotherapy evidence [24].

**There is no universally favorable microbial direction.** Microbial depletion weakened oxaliplatin and checkpoint activity in several models, yet germ-free animals were relatively resistant to some forms of radiation injury. Greater diversity accompanied favorable outcomes in some cohorts, but did not distinguish immune toxicity in another, and increased richness accompanied acute enteritis in the small 2024 rectal study. These are treatment-specific biological effects, not merely inconsistent versions of one universal rule.

**Function is often the better target.** Collagen degradation at an anastomosis and β-glucuronidase-mediated SN-38 reactivation identify specific activities that could be inhibited. Conversely, barrier-supporting fermentation and immune priming identify functions that might be preserved or supplemented. This favors selective manipulation over attempting to maximize diversity or eliminate broad bacterial groups.

### The most useful next developments are clinically specific

**Biomarkers must improve an actual decision.** A baseline microbial signature could help predict toxicity or response, while a changing signature during therapy might support monitoring. Those are different applications: the rectal chemoradiotherapy model includes an on-treatment microbial change, whereas the FOLFIRI study examined pretreatment profiles. External validation must match the proposed timing and clinical use.

A clinically useful test would need to add information beyond tumor biology, regimen, and routine assessment, and show that acting on the result benefits patients. Discriminating responders from nonresponders retrospectively is an earlier stage of development.

**Trials should preserve the efficacy–toxicity balance.** Reducing diarrhea is valuable, especially if it helps maintain treatment delivery, but should be evaluated alongside tumor response, treatment completion, and longer-term cancer outcomes. Likewise, a microbial adjuvant that enhances immune activation should be assessed for colitis and other immune-related adverse events, rather than assuming immune stimulation is uniformly favorable.

**Products need reproducible identities.** For probiotics, this means defined strains and viable preparations with tested schedules. For prebiotics, it means specified substrates and clinical endpoints. For FMT, it means screened, traceable donor-derived products and clear recipient-selection and safety procedures. These requirements follow directly from the variation seen between superficially similar interventions.

**Interpret the measurements in context.** The evidence spans different operations, tumor subtypes, regimens, diets, antibiotic exposures, sampling times, and sequencing methods; fecal relative abundance may not represent mucosa-associated organisms or their absolute activity. Diversity indices, predicted pathways, measured metabolites, symptom grades, and tumor responses are different measurements. Animal diarrhea scores are not human toxicity grades, and model discrimination is not a treatment response rate. Calculations above are labeled and derived from reported inputs; pooled estimates describe their study populations, while dated preprints retain their preliminary status. [39][12]

**The overall judgment is cautiously practical.** Gut microbiota are genuine participants in CRC treatment biology, with particularly persuasive examples in surgical healing, irinotecan metabolism, and experimental chemotherapy and checkpoint responses. As of September 2024, the sound clinical direction was selective, evidence-matched supportive care and controlled development of microbial interventions—not routine microbiome-guided treatment selection or unsupervised supplementation and FMT.
