# Generative AI and Copyright: A Research Report Through April 2022

**The answer depends on the stage.** Through **30 April 2022**, using copyrighted works to train generative AI could be lawful, but neither public availability nor the label “AI training” supplied automatic permission. The United States relied principally on case-specific fair use; the European Union’s 2019 Copyright Directive established two text-and-data-mining exceptions with different beneficiaries and conditions. Separately, copyright in generated art or code usually depended on identifiable human creative contribution, although British law provided a special rule for computer-generated works. [1][2][3]

**Copilot illustrates the central tension.** Its training on public code supported a substantial transformative-use argument, but its ability to reproduce existing material created distinct questions about infringement, attribution, and open-source licenses. For AI-generated art and code—including your motivating examples, Midjourney and GitHub Copilot—the crucial distinction was between learning from works, copying protected expression into an output, and claiming copyright over that output. [4]

**The report follows four questions.** First, how do the technologies produce works? Second, when may they use protected training material under U.S. and EU law? Third, which human contributions can support ownership? Finally, what did the Copilot controversy and the evidence available by April 2022 establish?

| Activity | United States | European Union | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training on images | Four-factor fair-use assessment | Articles 3 or 4, subject to conditions | Analytical learning versus expressive reuse |
| Training on articles | Strong support for non-expressive mining | Scientific-research or general TDM route | Facts and patterns versus protected wording |
| Training on source code | Fair use, permissions, and licenses | Article 4 and software-specific rights | Function versus protected implementation |
| Producing artwork | Human authorship and originality | Human intellectual creation | Human-directed expression versus autonomous output |
| Accepting Copilot suggestions | Protected expression and substantial similarity | Protected expression and applicable licenses | Common code versus distinctive reproduction |

## 1. Technical Background Introduction

### What generative deep learning learns and produces

**Generative models produce new examples.** Generative Deep Learning, or GDL, uses multilayer neural networks to learn patterns in data and produce new material resembling the kinds of examples encountered during learning. A model trained on portraits might generate another portrait; a model trained on text or code might generate another passage or function. The aim is to capture a distribution of possible examples, rather than merely classify an input or retrieve an existing file. [5]

**Training and generation are different operations.** During training, developers supply examples and adjust the network’s numerical parameters, commonly called weights, so its behavior better fits the training objective. During generation, the trained system uses those parameters together with an input—such as random noise, an image, a description, or surrounding code—to produce an output. Consequently, the protected files used to train a model and the expression appearing in a particular output are related but distinct objects of legal analysis. [6]

**Learning is not necessarily collage.** A neural network can learn general relationships without assembling its output from identifiable pieces of particular source files. Nevertheless, a system can also retain and reproduce material from its training set. The relevant technical question is therefore not simply whether a work influenced the model, but how that influence manifests itself in the model’s behavior and final output. [6][7]

### GANs: adversarial training followed by sampling

**GANs train two competing networks.** In the Generative Adversarial Network framework introduced by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues in 2014, a generator creates candidate examples while a discriminator tries to distinguish generated examples from real training examples. The generator improves by making its samples more convincing; the discriminator improves by detecting their deficiencies. Their opposing objectives form a two-player minimax game. [8]

**Feedback changes the generator’s behavior.** In the original formulation, the discriminator sees real examples and generated samples. Gradients—mathematical signals indicating how to improve the training objective—flow through the discriminator to update the generator. Goodfellow and colleagues emphasized that the generator was not updated by directly placing components of training examples into its parameters; it learned through this feedback. That describes the architecture, however, rather than establishing a legal exemption from reproduction rights. [8]

**Generation uses the trained generator.** Once trained, the generator can transform a latent input, typically a vector of random numbers, into an image through forward propagation: the input passes through the network to produce an output. The discriminator need not participate in every subsequent act of generation. This explains how a model can generate a new image without retrieving a particular training photograph at that moment. [8]

**Training quality affects output diversity.** The original paper identified a synchronization problem: if the generator advances without suitable discriminator updates, many different latent inputs can collapse onto the same or similar outputs. This problem, commonly called mode collapse, concerns diversity and successful learning. It is not itself evidence that the system has copied a particular protected work, just as diverse output does not by itself exclude occasional reproduction of source material. [8]

**An artwork can involve several contributors.** Gillotte’s 2020 discussion describes the collective Obvious using open-source GAN code written by Robbie Barrat and classical-portrait images to produce *Portrait of Edmond Belamy*. The example separates several potentially relevant contributions: the software, the input images, the organization of the experiment, and the generated painting. Ownership of one contribution does not automatically establish ownership of all the others. [9]

### DeepDream: optimizing an image using learned features

**DeepDream changes the input image.** DeepDream uses a previously trained convolutional neural network, or CNN, whose layers respond to visual features. Earlier layers may respond to edges and simple patterns; deeper layers respond to more complex shapes. Rather than asking the network only to identify an image, DeepDream modifies an image so that selected internal responses become stronger. [5]

**The optimization runs toward stronger activation.** Through gradient ascent, DeepDream repeatedly adjusts the input’s pixels to increase selected neuron or layer activations. An image containing a vaguely animal-like shape may acquire increasingly pronounced animal features. Starting from noise can also produce recognizable forms because the optimization amplifies patterns associated with the network’s learned representations. [6]

**This is not GAN training.** DeepDream’s creative operation normally optimizes an input while using an already trained network; a GAN instead trains a generator against a discriminator. Both rely on learned features, but they place the immediate source image in different roles. DeepDream may visibly preserve the structure of an uploaded photograph, whereas an ordinary GAN sampling process can begin with noise rather than an existing photograph. [5]

**Human settings influence the result.** Choices about the starting image, selected layers, parameters, and subsequent editing can shape the output. With a fixed model, input, settings, and random seed, the process can be reproducible. Reproducibility does not decide authorship: the legal issue is whether relevant human choices constitute original expression, not whether a computer follows deterministic calculations. [10]

### Why these mechanisms matter to copyright

**Training can implicate reproduction rights.** Downloading, storing, normalizing, and processing protected images, articles, or source code can involve making copies. That question exists even if a model’s final output contains no recognizable source expression. Conversely, permission to make training copies does not automatically authorize distributing an output that reproduces a protected image or code fragment. [11]

**DeepDream adds an immediate source question.** If a user uploads someone else’s photograph and the resulting image retains its protected composition or other recognizable expression, the output may raise adaptation or reproduction issues independently of the network’s original training data. Starting from a user’s own photograph or suitable public-domain material changes that part of the analysis. [6]

**The examples explain mechanisms, not brands.** Midjourney serves here as the motivating image-generation example, without assuming that it uses either of these particular architectures. A contemporaneous April 2022 discussion of VQGAN-CLIP illustrates the broader possibility of generating images from text, while GANs and DeepDream explain why training inputs, generation inputs, and human control must be distinguished. [12]

**The technical takeaway is separation.** Technology helps identify the copying acts, the expression retained, and the human contributions; none of those questions is answered merely by calling a system “generative.”

## 2. Copyright Issues of Training Data

### The starting point: protected expression and copying

**Copyright protects expression, not information generally.** Images, articles, and programs can all contain protected expression, but copyright does not give their owners a monopoly over every fact, idea, method, or functional principle they convey. Julien Cabay’s 2021 analysis connects this distinction to EU originality doctrine and emphasizes that freely accessible online works remain protected when the relevant legal conditions are satisfied. [13]

| Training material | Potentially protected expression | Material not protected merely as such |
|---|---|---|
| Images | Original composition and visual choices | General ideas or artistic techniques |
| Articles | Original wording, selection, and arrangement | Facts and information |
| Source code | Original implementation and expressive choices | Algorithms and methods of operation |

**Access is not comprehensive permission.** A photograph visible online, an article readable through a subscription, and a public code repository may each be accessible without granting unrestricted reproduction or redistribution rights. Permission can instead come from ownership, a sufficiently broad license, or an applicable copyright exception. Which route applies matters because its conditions may differ substantially. [13]

**The U.S. and EU structure permission differently.** U.S. fair use asks courts to balance circumstances under a general statutory standard. The EU’s DSM Directive identifies particular mining activities, beneficiaries, and conditions. The following comparison summarizes those structures before examining their operation. [14][2]

| Issue | U.S. fair use | EU Article 3 | EU Article 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eligible users | Any user may invoke it | Qualifying research and heritage institutions | Commercial and non-commercial users |
| Relevant purpose | Evaluated with all four factors | Scientific-research TDM | TDM generally |
| Access conditions | Access and contracts require separate analysis | Lawful access required | Lawful access required |
| Rights reservation | No automatic veto over Section 107 | No Article 4-style opt-out | Appropriate reservation excludes reliance |
| Contrary contracts | Separate contractual obligations may remain | Contrary provisions unenforceable | No equivalent Article 7 protection |
| Retention | Part of the overall assessment | Secure retention for scientific research | As long as necessary for TDM |
| Publishing source expression | Requires its own assessment | Not generally authorized | Not generally authorized |

### United States: how fair use operates

**Fair use permits some unlicensed copying.** Under 17 U.S.C. §107, fair use of a copyrighted work is not infringement. The statute lists criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research as examples of potentially qualifying purposes. These examples are illustrative rather than a closed list, and describing an activity as research does not settle the outcome. [14]

**The four factors work together.** Courts consider the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, the amount and substantiality used, and the effect on the work’s potential market or value. This is not a points system in which winning three factors guarantees success. The factors inform a judgment about the particular use in light of copyright’s purposes. [1]

**AI training presents an intermediate-copying question.** Even where training extracts patterns rather than distributing source works, the process may involve copying entire files. The developer’s argument is therefore not simply that the eventual model looks different from the inputs. It must address why those intermediate copies are permitted, whether by license or under fair use. [11]

### U.S. factor one: purpose and character, including transformative use

**A different purpose strengthens the argument.** The first factor asks what the defendant is doing with the work, including whether the activity is commercial or nonprofit educational. Courts also consider whether the use is transformative: whether it adds something new through a further purpose or different character rather than serving as a substitute for the original. Commerciality matters, but it does not automatically defeat fair use. [1]

**Pattern analysis offers a strong contrast.** An article written to inform readers can also be used by a researcher to measure grammatical patterns across a corpus. A photograph made for aesthetic viewing can be analyzed to improve object recognition. These uses draw on the works as sources of information rather than offering their expression to an audience for its original purpose. Matthew Sag’s 2019 analysis accordingly finds strong fair-use support for text mining and other non-expressive uses. [15]

**Generative purposes require closer examination.** A system designed to generate articles, paintings, or code does more than produce statistics about its inputs. Its training may still serve a different purpose from each source work, but its ultimate products occupy expressive or software markets. Benjamin Sobel’s 2017 analysis therefore warned that expressive machine learning, including natural-language generation, presented a harder fair-use problem than purely analytical mining. [16]

**Technical transformation is not legal transformation.** Converting a photograph into pixels, tokens, or numerical weights does not alone establish a transformative purpose. Otherwise, almost any digital copying could qualify simply because a computer processed it. The stronger argument explains the new function of the use and why that function differs from exploiting the source’s protected expression.

**The resulting judgment is conditional.** A model used to identify patterns without returning source expression has a stronger first-factor position than a service designed to reproduce particular expressive material. Evidence that generated outputs substitute for identifiable source works would weaken the changed-purpose argument; evidence of a genuinely analytical, non-substitutive function would strengthen it.

### U.S. factor two: the nature of the copyrighted work

**Creative expression generally receives stronger weight.** The second factor distinguishes, among other things, highly imaginative works from factual or technical material. A novel or artistic image generally presents a less favorable case for fair use than a factual report, although the classification is not decisive. Whether the work has been published also matters; unpublished status normally weighs against fair use, without categorically preventing it. [1]

**Images are not automatically factual.** A photograph records something in the world, but framing, lighting, timing, and composition may embody creative choices. A collection of contemporary artistic photographs therefore cannot be treated as unprotected factual material merely because its subjects are real. Quang’s discussion of photographic-use cases emphasizes that courts have resisted that shortcut. [11]

**Articles combine facts and expression.** Mining a technical article for scientific relationships draws on its informational aspects. Generating a replacement article that reproduces its distinctive explanation, examples, or phrasing engages its expressive aspects more directly. The relevant distinction concerns what was used and how, rather than whether the input file was labeled “news” or “science.”

**Code combines function and expression.** Computer programs perform tasks, and their functional elements can support a fair-use argument. However, source code can also contain original expressive choices. The software reverse-engineering cases discussed below show why functional purpose matters, without making all copying of programs permissible. [11]

**The practical implication is work-specific assessment.** A mixed training corpus should not be characterized as uniformly factual, uniformly creative, or uniformly unprotected. A finding about technical code or factual articles does not automatically carry the same weight when applied to artistic images or literary prose.

### U.S. factor three: amount and substantiality

**Both quantity and importance matter.** The third factor asks how much of the copyrighted work was used and how significant that portion was. Copying a whole work can sometimes be fair when necessary for a legitimate transformative function. Conversely, copying a small portion can weigh against fair use when that portion captures the work’s expressive “heart.” [1]

**Training often requires complete inputs.** Learning useful patterns may require processing whole images, articles, or programs. That supplies an argument about necessity, not an automatic exemption. A developer should be able to explain why the amount copied was reasonably connected to the asserted purpose, particularly where the system retains or exposes substantial source expression.

**Dataset size changes no denominator.** If an entire protected photograph appears in a very large training corpus, the relevant copying is not made trivial simply because the photograph constitutes a tiny share of that corpus. The comparison is ordinarily to the source work as a whole, not only to the size of the developer’s database. The Copyright Office specifically rejects fixed formulas based on percentages, words, lines, pages, or copies. [1]

**Input copying and output copying differ.** An entire article might be copied internally for a justified analytical function while only statistical information emerges. A much shorter output passage might nevertheless reproduce particularly significant expression. The same distinction applies to code: a conventional line may have little protectable content, while a distinctive block can matter even when it is short relative to a large repository.

**The resulting judgment turns on necessity.** Full-work training is most defensible under this factor when the copying is proportionate to a different legitimate purpose and does not become a route for delivering excessive protected expression to users.

### U.S. factor four: market effect and value

**Substitution is a central concern.** The fourth factor considers harm to the existing or potential market for the copyrighted work. Courts ask whether the use displaces sales, reduces the work’s value, or could cause substantial harm if the activity became widespread. Relevant licensing markets may also enter the analysis. [1]

**Non-expressive mining differs from replacement.** A study reporting linguistic trends across articles does not ordinarily supply the reading experience those articles offer. A service that delivers substantially reproduced articles can do so. Similarly, an image-analysis system and a service supplying recognizable substitutes for particular photographs present different market relationships, even if both use machine learning.

**Licensing arguments need careful boundaries.** A rightsholder may argue that unlicensed training eliminates revenue from a market for training permissions. That argument is stronger where a traditional, reasonable, or likely-to-develop licensing market is established. It is not enough simply to assert that every use would generate a fee if permission were mandatory; that would make the exception depend entirely on the rightsholder’s willingness to charge for it. [7]

**Competition is not automatically infringement.** Copyright protects expression rather than every economic advantage associated with an author’s ideas or techniques. Thus, a generated work’s commercial appeal does not alone establish unlawful copying. Nevertheless, evidence that a system reproduces protected material and competes in the market for that material can connect the infringement concern to a concrete market injury.

**The market conclusion follows the use.** Evidence of source substitution or established licensing displacement weakens fair use. Evidence that the use extracts information, improves discovery, or creates a different functional product without supplying the protected expression supports it. The question remains how those circumstances interact with the other factors.

### Transformative use: supporting cases and counterexamples

**The precedents support distinctions, not slogans.** Decisions involving search engines and software analysis provided important analogies by April 2022. Their outcomes show why “computers analyze copyrighted works” was too broad a category to determine fair use. [11]

| Case | Relevant activity | Result | Significance for training |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Perfect 10 v. Amazon* (2007) | Image-search thumbnails | Fair use | Images served an information-location purpose |
| *Authors Guild v. Google* (2015) | Book scanning, search, and snippets | Fair use | Complete internal copying supported a different function |
| *Sega v. Accolade* (1992) | Software reverse engineering | Fair use | Intermediate copying exposed functional requirements |
| *Sony v. Connectix* (2000) | Intermediate software copying | Fair use | A functional new product supported the defense |
| *VHT v. Zillow* (2019) | Searchable property photographs | Fair use rejected for the disputed use | Searchability did not sufficiently change the photographs’ purpose |
| *Fox News v. TVEyes* (2018) | Search and access to television clips | Fair use rejected for the challenged service | Transformative features did not overcome licensing-market concerns |

**Google Books is the strongest familiar analogy.** In *Authors Guild v. Google*, the Second Circuit upheld digitization, search functionality, and limited snippet display as fair use. Complete books were copied, but the service did not provide a significant market substitute for their protected expression. Its commercial character did not defeat that conclusion. This was a Second Circuit decision; the Supreme Court subsequently declined review rather than issuing the merits judgment. [13][11]

**The software cases explain intermediate copying.** *Sega* and *Connectix* demonstrate that copying a program during analysis can be justified even when the final product is commercial. Their significance lies in the relationship between the copying and a legitimate functional objective. They do not establish that any commercial system may reproduce software simply because its developer calls the process learning. [11]

**Zillow and TVEyes supply important counterexamples.** In *VHT*, making photographs searchable did not sufficiently alter their original role of depicting properties. In *TVEyes*, the challenged service had transformative aspects, but the court also found substantial concerns about exploitation of protected clips and the licensing market. These cases make it difficult to treat transformativeness as a substitute for examining the actual service and its outputs. [11]

**The scholarly disagreement concerned expressive generation.** Gillotte argued in 2020 that training AI on copyrighted artwork would likely be excused by fair use. Sag emphasized the strong position of non-expressive mining, while Sobel warned about expressive applications. These positions overlap on the importance of purpose but differ over how far analytical-use reasoning extends when the system produces competing expressive works. [9][15][16]

**The best-supported U.S. position is graduated.** Non-expressive text mining has especially strong support from the existing cases. Image generation, expressive article generation, and commercial code generation can present credible fair-use arguments, but their purposes, retained expression, and market relationships must be examined directly.

### European Union: the 2019 TDM exceptions

**The EU legislated two specific routes.** Directive (EU) 2019/790, adopted on 17 April 2019, introduced mandatory text-and-data-mining exceptions in Articles 3 and 4. Article 2(2) defines TDM as automated analysis of digital text and data to generate information including patterns, trends, and correlations. Recital 8 expressly includes text, sounds, images, and data, making the framework relevant beyond written documents. [2]

**The exceptions address specified legal acts.** Mining can involve copyright reproduction and extraction from databases, including databases protected by the EU’s separate sui generis database right. That database right protects qualifying investment in obtaining, verifying, or presenting database contents; it is distinct from copyright in an individual article or image. Consequently, permission concerning individual works and permission concerning a database can be separate issues. [17]

**National legislation remains operationally important.** Member States were required to transpose the Directive by 7 June 2021. An April 2022 assessment therefore had to connect the Directive’s framework to the relevant national law rather than treat its wording as a universal private license. Copyright’s territorial character also matters where access, training, and distribution occur in different countries. [13][15]

### Article 3: scientific research by qualifying institutions

**Article 3 protects qualifying scientific mining.** It requires an exception for reproductions and extractions made by research organizations and cultural heritage institutions to conduct TDM for scientific research on works to which they have lawful access. The important conditions concern both who performs the mining and why it is performed. A commercial company does not qualify merely because its employees describe their development work as research. [2]

**Eligibility is more precise than “academic.”** Research organizations include qualifying universities and other entities conducting scientific research or educational activities involving scientific research. The Directive covers organizations operating on a nonprofit basis, reinvesting profits in research, or acting under a recognized public-interest mission. Cultural heritage institutions include publicly accessible libraries and museums, archives, and film or audio heritage institutions. [2]

**Commercial partnerships are not automatically excluded.** Recital 11 allows qualifying institutions to benefit when research takes place through public-private partnerships and to use private partners’ technological tools. However, commercial influence becomes disqualifying where it gives an undertaking decisive control and preferential access to results. The distinction is between supporting an eligible institution’s research and turning a commercially controlled organization into an Article 3 beneficiary. [2]

**Lawful access remains a prerequisite.** Access can arise through subscriptions, open-access licenses, or lawfully accessible online content. Article 3 does not eliminate the need to obtain access in the first place. A subscription payment may therefore remain necessary even though a separate mining permission is not required for activity within the exception. [17]

**Research copies may be retained securely.** Article 3(2) requires an appropriate level of security and allows retention for scientific research, including verification of results. This is practically important: reproducibility can require access to the same underlying material after the initial analysis. Rightsholders may apply proportionate measures to protect network and database security, but those measures must not exceed what is necessary for that purpose. [2]

**Contracts cannot remove the core exception.** Article 7(1) makes contractual provisions contrary to Article 3 unenforceable. Article 3 also lacks Article 4’s rightsholder opt-out. An eligible institution with lawful access therefore receives a more robust statutory position than a general commercial miner, although the scientific purpose, security, and covered-act conditions continue to apply. [2]

### Article 4: broader mining subject to rights reservation

**Article 4 includes commercial companies.** It permits specified reproductions and extractions of lawfully accessible works for TDM without limiting the exception to particular institutions or nonprofit purposes. Startups, commercial research laboratories, and other businesses can therefore fall within its beneficiary class. Its broader reach distinguishes it from Article 3. [17]

**Rights reservation changes the result.** Article 4 applies only where the relevant use has not been expressly reserved by rightsholders in an appropriate manner. For publicly available online content, the Directive identifies machine-readable means. Recital 18 discusses metadata and website or service terms; in other settings, contractual agreements or unilateral declarations may be appropriate. [2]

**Access and reservation are separate conditions.** A company may lawfully read or download content yet encounter an effective reservation preventing reliance on Article 4 for mining. Conversely, the absence of a reservation does not justify obtaining content through an unlawful access route. The exception requires both lawful accessibility and satisfaction of the reservation condition.

**Retention depends on continuing necessity.** Article 4(2) permits reproductions and extractions to be retained for as long as necessary for TDM. This is narrower than Article 3’s express scientific-research and verification provision, but it is not a rule requiring every commercial miner to delete all material immediately after one calculation. The necessary period depends on the mining purpose. [2]

**Commercial permission can become a licensing market.** Bernt Hugenholtz’s July 2019 analysis argued that Article 4 enables rightsholders to control, license, or prohibit mining and noted publishers’ existing interest in paid TDM services. For businesses, that means technical access to a large corpus may coexist with a need to negotiate permissions for reserved material. [17]

**Article 4 lacks Article 3’s contract shield.** Article 7(1) names Articles 3, 5, and 6, but not Article 4. A commercial developer must therefore examine applicable access terms and contractual restrictions alongside rights reservations. This also shows why a statement that “the EU permits TDM” leaves out the most consequential distinction for companies. [13]

### Applying the rules to images, articles, and code

**Image training can fit the TDM framework.** The Directive expressly contemplates mining images. A qualifying institution analyzing images for scientific research may use Article 3 when its conditions are met; a commercial developer may instead rely on Article 4 for covered mining acts when access is lawful and rights have not been appropriately reserved. Neither route generally authorizes publishing recognizable protected images simply because they passed through a model. [2]

**Article mining has a straightforward analytical application.** A university studying patterns across lawfully accessed scientific articles is a clear example of the activity Article 3 addresses. A company performing similar analysis may use Article 4, but must account for reservations and access terms. Producing and distributing article text that reproduces protected wording is a further act, distinct from the mining copies.

**Source code requires software-specific attention.** Article 4(1) expressly refers to reproduction and adaptation rights in Article 4(1)(a) and (b) of the Software Directive, Directive 2009/24/EC. Article 3(1) does not list those software provisions. It would therefore overstate Article 3 to assume that institutional research status alone resolves every source-code training act: the relevant software right, license, and exception must be identified. [13]

**Open licenses supply another permission route.** A license may authorize copying or modification subject to conditions, independently of reliance on a statutory exception. Those conditions can matter when protected material persists in a model or output. Sag’s discussion of open-license compatibility illustrates why combining openly licensed sources is not always equivalent to combining material without legal conditions. [15]

### The comparative judgment on training legality

**The United States offers broader flexibility.** Fair use is open to commercial and non-commercial actors and can accommodate new technologies without a bespoke statutory exception. Its cost is that the legal assessment depends heavily on purpose, copying, expression, and market evidence. A rightsholder’s notice is not itself a statutory opt-out from fair use, although contractual and access issues remain separate.

**The EU offers differentiated statutory certainty.** Eligible scientific institutions receive a strong route under Article 3, including protection against contrary contracts and secure retention for research verification. Commercial users receive broader eligibility under Article 4 but face rights reservation and narrower retention conditions. Thus, the EU framework can be especially favorable to qualifying institutional research while more readily allowing rightsholders to control commercial mining. [18]

**The training-law judgment is conditional permission.** Copyrighted images, articles, and code could lawfully support AI training by April 2022, but the governing permission depended on the use and jurisdiction—not on public accessibility, dataset size, or the fact that a machine performed the analysis.

**Training copies and the U.S. baseline**

**Copyrighted training works.** Training adjusts model parameters using data and can cover all content used to develop a generative model, including protected expression; the evidence names text and images and gives image, text, and code examples, but not a complete category list. [86] Public availability is not authorization. [86]

**Copies and retention.** Acquisition may download, scrape, copy databases, use web-crawler text, or draw on pirate sources; curation filters, cleans, and compiles. Collection can make repeated copies; mini-batching, shuffling, and caching can create temporary reproductions, and later deletion does not itself resolve infringement. [86] Retention is disputed: developers describe weights as statistical relationships rather than copies, while critics point to verbatim, near-identical, or substantially similar outputs; highly specific learned patterns may be memorized data. [86]

**United States—Fair Use.** Section 107 makes fair use a judge-made, affirmative defense: it is not infringement, but the proponent bears the burden; courts balance four non-exclusive factors case by case. [86] (1) **Purpose and character:** further purpose or different character, commerciality, transformation, and sometimes lawful access matter; “intermediate pattern learning” is an argument, not a safe harbor. Research or closed, non-substitutive deployment is argued more transformative than generating substantially similar expressive works for the same market; effective output restrictions may strengthen that argument. [86] (2) **Nature** considers the source work; (3) **amount** compares the portion with the whole; neither gives a categorical result for images, articles, or code. (4) **Market:** rightsholders say ingestion destroys licensing markets and creates substitutes; technology interests say mandatory licensing could hinder development and competition. [86] Some commenters argued broad fair use conflicts with the treaty three-step test; that remains contested, not settled law. [86]

**Implications.** For **copyrighted code**, no code-specific U.S. training rule is established; absent a license or defense, protected-material use may implicate reproduction and derivative-work rights. [86] **Research organizations** face argued licensing and owner-contact burdens, not a settled exemption. [86] **Commercial companies** face an unresolved licensing question: owners call licensing a normal business cost, while technology commenters cite scale, unknown owners, and estimates of tens or hundreds of billions of dollars a year as barriers. Thus, by 30 April 2022, “open source” did not by itself settle Fair Use: the legality of the training copy remained unresolved [241].

Copilot, powered by Codex, drew on public GitHub and other-site code and suggested lines from current-file code, comments and cursor context; GitHub acknowledged imperfect safety mechanisms [240]. Open-source status does not erase licence conditions: licensed inputs and verbatim output without CMI are distinct; a copied “heart” may matter, so infringement is case-specific [243]. Government-hosted images are not automatically public domain; permission, restrictions or CC-BY attribution may apply [246].

For copyrighted training works, the applicable framework is the technology-development/technology-usage distinction within the copyright reproduction right: collecting and curating protected works can generate repeated copies, while storage and technological processing can create further copyright-relevant reproductions; later discarding the data does not by itself eliminate the copy issue [86][87][89]. These are possible training acts, not a claim that every system makes infringing copies [49].

Under Fair Use’s purpose-and-character factor, commercial use weighs against Fair Use, whereas AI training can be argued transformative when it gives expressive works a different, value-adding technological purpose; transformation is not conclusive [143][157][107]. The empirical figures show courts found transformative use in 68 cases where physical and purposive transformation co-occurred, versus 37 (60.7%) for purposive transformation without physical transformation and 16 (32.7%) for physical transformation without purposive transformation, so purpose-based transformation was the stronger route in that sample, not a safe harbor for commercial training [124].

Fair Use’s nature-of-the-work factor asks whether the source is published or unpublished and how creative it is: factual or nonfiction works generally weigh more favorably than highly creative or unpublished works [91][112]. Thus a highly creative copyrighted image is less favorable, a factual article is more favorable than an expressive one, and code must be separated into protected expression versus unprotectable ideas or procedures; none of these categories produces an automatic result [68][91][112].

When training copies all or many portions of copyrighted images, articles, or code, Fair Use’s amount-and-substantiality factor normally weighs against the use because it measures both quantitative amount and qualitative importance, including whether the copied part is the work’s “heart”; there is no fixed numerical threshold [53][91][60][112]. Complete or verbatim copying can nevertheless be compatible with Fair Use when copies are used only as nonexpressive input to a process and do not substitute for the source, so the result remains dependent on purpose and facts for each work [122][134].

Fair Use’s market-effect factor favors training or outputs that do not substitute for the source work and weighs against uses that substitute for, publicly redistribute, or otherwise harm the actual or potential market/value of copyrighted images, articles, or code; a negative economic effect alone is not automatically infringement [181][98][57][136][89][35]. In 579 U.S. opinions, factors one and four matched in 72.5% of decisions, while factor four aligned with the overall result in 50.0% of conflicting cases versus 36.0% for factor one, illustrating its stronger observed pull without making it dispositive [166].

Transformative-use reasoning can support Fair Use when AI training gives expressive works a new, value-adding technological purpose—learning patterns to build a system that produces new outputs rather than simply republishing source expression [137][59]. Its limits are substantial: training may use an entire database without transforming the database itself, and transformation does not replace the other Fair Use inquiries; whole-work copying remains purpose-dependent [198][91][134].

Directive (EU) 2019/790 provides the EU TDM pathway: TDM is automated analysis of digital text and data to generate information such as patterns, trends, and correlations, and Articles 3 and 4 create mandatory routes for reproductions and extractions of lawfully accessible works [225][214][220][58]. Article 3 is confined to eligible scientific research, whereas Article 4 is broader and can cover commercial TDM but is subject to rights reservations; generative-model training remains fact-sensitive where it internalizes or replicates expressive content rather than merely analyzing it [214].

Article 4 operates as an opt-out: the TDM exception covers lawfully accessible works unless the rightsholder expressly reserves the relevant use; for publicly available online content, the reservation should be made in an appropriate, particularly machine-readable, form such as metadata or robots.txt [229][76]. The reservation can block reliance on Article 4, implementation differs across Member States and signals may be unreliable, while copies made under the exception may be retained only as long as necessary for TDM [226][202].

United States copyright law supplies the report’s input-side baseline: fixed, human-created original expression is protected, while Fair Use can make some reproductions noninfringing under a flexible, fact-specific rule [203][162]. The practical implication is that training images, articles, or code must be examined through acquisition, licensing, and the four factors; public availability alone is not automatic permission [163][161][169].

Fair Use is the U.S. framework that lets the report explain when an unlicensed training copy may be noninfringing: Section 107 asks about purpose and character, nature, amount and substantiality, and market effect [135][35]. Because the four factors are flexible and weighed together, it explains both possible defenses for computational training and why no blanket answer applies to every generative-AI dataset [100][57].

Transformative use helps explain Fair Use’s purpose-and-character inquiry by asking whether the new use adds meaning or value and a further purpose or different character rather than merely superseding the source; AI training can be characterized as a new technological function [124][137]. It can support the first-factor argument but does not by itself resolve Fair Use, so the remaining factors and the facts of the training use still matter [124][57].

European Union copyright law supplies the report’s second legal framework: it addresses AI-training copies through specific TDM exceptions, with a research-oriented route and a broader Article 4 route subject to rightsholder reservation [223][226][216]. This makes the developer’s purpose, beneficiary status, lawful access, and opt-out compliance material to the analysis rather than treating all EU uses alike [224][227].

The 2019 DSM Directive is relevant because it harmonizes mandatory EU exceptions for reproductions and extractions made for TDM, including training-relevant analysis, through Articles 3 and 4 [225][228][227]. It reduces uncertainty but is not a blanket generative-AI licence: the automated-analysis concept, Article 3 eligibility, lawful access, and Article 4 reservation still control the result [214][116][221].

Article 3 directly serves the report’s research question: it requires a copyright exception for research organisations and cultural-heritage institutions to reproduce and extract lawfully accessible works for scientific TDM, giving qualifying research training a route for covered copying [228][231]. The limits are the qualifying institution, scientific-research purpose, lawful access, and secure handling, so Article 3 does not settle every AI developer’s position [231][222].

Article 4 serves the commercial-company question by allowing any user, including commercial entities, to reproduce or extract lawfully accessible works for TDM without a purpose restriction [228][229]. That permission is conditional: an express rightsholder reservation or opt-out can remove the Article 4 route, so commercial training still requires access and reservation checks [221][226].

Commercial companies cannot assume that describing a project as research qualifies it for Article 3, because commercial influence and profit-seeking purposes can exclude the organisation from the research-organisation category [142][232]. Article 4 is the broader route for companies’ commercial TDM, but lawful access and the rightsholder opt-out remain material limits, making compliance risk central [220][109].

Copyrighted images are central training inputs for generative AI: the cited dataset examples contain 14 million images and 600,000 images, and widely used datasets are described as containing images scraped from the internet [118]. Public availability does not settle permission; unlicensed copying raises Fair Use and market questions, and U.S. treatment of scraping remains unclear [83][84][57].

Copyrighted articles are direct training inputs: TDM and AI-driven creativity may copy or extract texts, and computational-research authorities have treated reproducing and mining collections as potentially fair use, but the result remains dependent on the particular use and conditions [45][77][147].

Copyrighted code is a central example because computer programs can be protected as literary works, and copies made during acquisition, preprocessing, or training can engage copyright even when the code is used to build a new system [219][134][85]. GitHub Copilot makes the issue concrete: its model was trained on publicly available source code and generates suggestions from a user’s workspace context, so code licences and Fair Use/TDM must be considered separately from whether a particular suggestion copies protected expression [51][233][110].

Copyrighted training works as a category are central because model development can copy protected material during collection, curation, and training, while public visibility does not itself authorize the use; no-derivatives terms may conflict with model training and opaque models may not retain attribution [87][88]. Because model quality follows training-data quality, replacing licensed material with inferior substitutes can degrade outputs, adding a practical quality and innovation risk to the legal analysis [48].

In plain language, U.S. law protects original expression that is fixed and human-authored; using a protected work to train a model is therefore potentially a reproduction requiring permission unless a licence or Fair Use applies [203][177]. Fair Use is not automatic permission for public or online material: courts balance purpose, nature, amount, and market effect case by case [161][169][174].

Plainly, Fair Use can make an otherwise unlicensed use noninfringing, but it is a case-specific balance of purpose and character, nature, amount and substantiality, and market effect—not automatic permission [136][111][100]. For AI training, the factors are weighed together rather than counted as a formula, so the answer can change with the use and its market consequences [100][111].

Transformative use means giving existing material a substantially different purpose or function—such as making it searchable or useful for a new task—rather than merely presenting the original [124][85]. It can strengthen a Fair Use argument but is neither required nor conclusive; all the other factors and the facts of the training use still matter [130][124].

In plain language, European Union copyright law does not make AI training automatically free: the DSM framework introduced specific TDM exceptions, while Article 4 supplies a broader route subject to rightsholder reservation [223][226]. The practical answer depends on which exception applies and on its conditions, rather than treating all EU training as permitted [223][226].

The 2019 DSM Directive is, in plain language, an EU rulebook for making some TDM reproductions and extractions lawful: TDM is automated analysis of digital text and data, with Article 3 for qualifying scientific research and Article 4 for broader and commercial uses [225][214][221]. It improves consistency but is not a blanket licence for generative-AI training; the research-purpose and beneficiary limits and Article 4’s rightsholder opt-out still control the result [156][214][222].

Article 3 is the mandatory research exception: research organisations and cultural-heritage institutions may reproduce and extract lawfully accessible works for scientific TDM, and the rule is intended to be implemented EU-wide [231][116]. It does not itself entitle researchers to obtain access, excludes non-qualifying users, and requires copies to be kept under proportionate security arrangements for scientific research or verification; the exception is limited to non-commercial TDM [116][93].

Article 4 is the broad route: any person or organization, including a commercial company, may reproduce or extract lawfully accessible works for TDM unless the rightsholder expressly reserves the use [229][226]. For online material, the reservation should be made in an appropriate machine-readable form such as metadata or robots.txt; because no fallback obligation is guaranteed, users may have to abandon important datasets or use pre-cleared material when a reservation is encountered [76].

## 3. Copyright Ownership of Generated Works

### Human authorship comes before ownership allocation

**Ownership presupposes a protected work.** Before deciding whether a programmer or user should own an AI-generated painting or piece of code, one must establish that copyright exists in the relevant expression. An attractive picture, a useful function, and a commercially valuable file do not acquire copyright merely because someone possesses or sells them. Copyrightability, authorship, and ownership are connected but distinct questions. [6]

**Many systems require human-created expression.** Burylo’s 2022 comparative analysis concludes that most countries’ existing copyright legislation protects original works created by human authors. This creates a central obstacle for autonomous output: an image or program may appear new, yet no human may have made the expressive choices necessary to qualify as its author. The obstacle concerns the legal source of the expression rather than its aesthetic or technical quality. [19]

**U.S. registration practice states this explicitly.** The Copyright Office’s Compendium says that a registrable work must be human-created and excludes work produced by a machine or merely mechanical process operating randomly or automatically without human creative input or intervention. This does not exclude works made with computers; it asks whether a human’s creative contribution supports the claim. [9]

**The February 2022 refusal provides a concrete example.** On 14 February 2022, the Copyright Office’s Review Board again refused registration of Stephen Thaler’s *A Recent Entrance to Paradise*, for which the claimed author was the “Creativity Machine.” The Board’s reason was insufficient human authorship: Thaler had not supplied evidence of adequate human creative input or intervention. The decision addressed the autonomous work presented in that application, not every artwork involving AI. [20]

### Originality is distinct from novelty

**Originality asks a different question.** A work can be unprecedented without being an original human work of authorship. Conversely, a human work need not be unprecedented in subject or style to qualify. In U.S. law, *Feist* requires independent creation and at least a minimal degree of creativity; originality is not simply a measure of how unusual a result looks. [6]

**EU originality emphasizes creative choice.** Under *Infopaq* and *Painer*, protection depends on the author’s own intellectual creation, reflected through free and creative choices. *Football Dataco* explains that choices dictated by technical considerations, rules, or constraints leaving no room for creative freedom do not satisfy that standard. Applied to AI, the question is which human choices, if any, are expressed in the resulting work. [13]

**Human involvement alone is insufficient.** A person may pay for a service, operate a computer, select a broad subject, and press a button without necessarily supplying the output’s protected expression. Those acts show causation or investment, but neither is automatically authorship. By contrast, a person who determines expressive features and substantially shapes the result presents a stronger claim under ordinary copyright principles.

**Effort and originality must not be conflated.** Extensive engineering can support copyright in the program itself without establishing copyright in every output. Similarly, considerable effort collecting data may not make the collector the author of each generated work. The legal link must concern the claimed expression, not merely the amount of work that made the system possible. [6]

| Legal system | Relevant requirement | Autonomous-output consequence | Human-assisted creation |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | Human authorship and minimal creativity | Registration faces a human-authorship barrier | Original human expression can qualify |
| European Union | Author’s own intellectual creation | Absence of human creative choices threatens protection | Free creative choices can support protection |
| United Kingdom | Special deemed-author rule | Necessary arranger may be treated as author | Ordinary human-authorship principles also apply |
| Australia | Identifiable human authorship and original effort | Automated compilations illustrate the obstacle | Human contribution remains important |

**Australia supplies a useful comparison.** Devarapalli’s 2018 analysis discusses *Telstra v. Phone Directories* and *Acohs v. Ucorp*, where automated production created authorship problems for directories and computer-generated material. These were not decisions about modern generative paintings, but they demonstrate why a novel or useful computer-produced result does not by itself establish an identifiable human author. [21]

### Paintings and code: which human choices count?

**For a painting, examine expressive control.** Choices about composition, subject placement, colors, source images, and substantial manual changes can be relevant. Their significance depends on how they shape the final expression. Merely selecting a desirable output from a large group is not automatically equivalent to having authored all the expressive details within it, although an original selection or arrangement can raise its own copyright question. [6]

**For code, examine the claimed contribution.** A programmer may design a module, write original surrounding code, restructure a suggestion, and contribute original implementation choices. Those human contributions can support rights in the resulting program where the applicable standards are met. Accepting an entirely automatic function does not, by itself, identify a human author of every line, and functionality alone is not the protected subject matter.

**Mixed works need not receive one answer.** A larger human-created work can contain machine-generated material alongside protected human expression. The copyright claim should correspond to the original contribution that supports it, rather than treating the entire project as necessarily protected or necessarily unprotected merely because AI participated. This follows the distinction between AI as an assisting tool and autonomous generation recognized by the European Parliament. [22]

**Third-party rights remain a separate issue.** An output’s lack of its own copyright does not extinguish copyright in material it reproduces. A machine-generated painting might retain protected elements of an input photograph; a generated function might reproduce protected code. Such output is not freely reusable merely because its new author claim is weak.

### British law: the computer-generated-work rule

**British law expressly addresses absent authors.** Section 178 of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 defines a computer-generated work as one generated by computer in circumstances where there is no human author. Section 9(3) then provides that, for a computer-generated literary, dramatic, musical, or artistic work, the author is taken to be the person who undertook the arrangements necessary for its creation. [23][3]

**The statute supplies a deemed author.** Its solution is not to give the computer legal personality. Instead, it attributes authorship to an arranging person even though the category is defined by the absence of a human author in the ordinary creative sense. The rule is relevant to literary and artistic works, so its significance extends beyond paintings to software expression. [3]

**The arranger is not automatically the programmer.** Depending on the circumstances, programming, configuring the system, supplying material, and directing generation may point toward different people. In *Nova Productions v. Mazooma Games*, the developer’s design of game elements and the rules generating frames supported authorship, rather than the player’s non-artistic interaction. That example does not establish that every programmer owns every output or that a sufficiently creative user can never qualify. [10]

**Modern systems complicate the attribution.** Burylo notes that dozens of people may design, program, train, and operate an AI system. Identifying a single person responsible for the necessary arrangements can therefore be difficult. This is a practical weakness of an apparently simple allocation rule: it moves the dispute from identifying direct human expression to identifying the legally significant arrangements. [19]

**Originality still produces interpretive tension.** Patrick Goold’s 2021 analysis questions how Section 9(3) fits the originality requirement and argues that it is either unnecessary or unjustifiably protects material belonging in the public domain. Williams offers a contrasting, user-oriented analysis emphasizing artistic judgment and the treatment of AI as a tool. The disagreement concerns both the meaning of the existing provision and the justification for special protection. [24][10]

**The British approach has international counterparts.** Burylo identifies South Africa, Hong Kong, India, Ireland, and New Zealand alongside the UK as jurisdictions with legislative treatment of computer-generated works. These are alternatives to the dominant human-authorship approach, not evidence of an identical worldwide ownership rule. The UK category also uses a 50-year protection period linked to creation rather than a human author’s lifespan. [19]

### The European Parliament’s recommendations

**The Parliament distinguished assistance from autonomy.** Its resolution of 20 October 2020 stated that the existing intellectual-property framework remained applicable when AI functioned as an author’s assisting tool. Autonomous generation raised different questions about ownership, remuneration, and potential market concentration. This distinction supports evaluating actual human contribution rather than treating every AI-related work alike. [22]

**It rejected AI legal personality.** The resolution considered it inappropriate to give AI technologies legal personality and linked copyright originality to natural persons and intellectual creation. It stated that autonomously produced works might not qualify for protection under that understanding. A corporation’s ability to own intellectual property does not contradict this position: corporate ownership is a legally recognized allocation, not recognition that a machine possesses human creativity. [22]

**Its ownership recommendation was expressly conditional.** Rights, “if any,” should be assigned only to natural or legal persons that created the work lawfully. Where protected material was used, authorization should have been obtained unless an exception or limitation applied. The words “if any” matter: the Parliament did not recommend that every generated work automatically acquire copyright simply because a company or user could be named as owner. [22]

**This was policy guidance, not harmonized ownership law.** The resolution supplied a position on how the issue should be approached, while preserving the distinction between lawful inputs, protectable human contributions, and autonomous output. It did not enact a binding EU equivalent of the British deemed-arranger rule.

### The three ownership options compared

**Each option rewards a different contribution.** Programmer ownership emphasizes investment in the generating system; user ownership emphasizes the person directing and deploying it; no copyright emphasizes public access where the output lacks a qualifying author. These are policy alternatives, although elements of them also correspond to existing legal approaches. [25][19]

| Ownership option | Principal advantage | Principal disadvantage | Strongest application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Programmer | Rewards development and investment | May detach rights from output-level creativity | Programmer shapes the output’s expression |
| User | Rewards creative direction and practical deployment | Minimal activation may not establish authorship | User exercises meaningful expressive control |
| No copyright | Expands access and avoids artificial attribution | Can weaken output-exclusivity incentives | Output lacks qualifying human authorship |

### Option one: assigning copyright to the programmer

**Programmer ownership can support investment.** Developing and maintaining an effective generative system can require significant technical effort and expenditure. Granting rights in outputs may help the developer recover those costs and provide a clear licensing counterparty. Devarapalli’s comparative analysis presents investment incentives as an important justification for allocating rights to those responsible for making the technology possible. [21]

**The strongest claim involves expressive design.** A programmer may create a system specifically to execute a particular artistic conception or determine significant expressive features. In that situation, the argument is stronger than the general proposition that writing a tool makes its developer the author of everything produced with it. The relevant question is whether the developer’s creative conception is sufficiently connected to the claimed work.

**A blanket rule risks over-rewarding toolmakers.** Programmers can already hold copyright in their original software and earn revenue from selling or licensing access to it. Automatic ownership of every output would add a potentially enormous portfolio of rights, including rights over results substantially shaped by users. Palace argues that this can reward the creation of the production mechanism rather than authorship of the resulting expression. [25]

**It can also obscure responsibility.** “The programmer” may be an unrealistic singular description where developers, trainers, contractors, and operators contribute. An ownership rule can simplify commercial administration, but it does not automatically establish who is responsible for infringing inputs or outputs. Nor did the existing U.S. work-made-for-hire doctrine itself make AI a statutory employee whose every production belonged to its developer; proposals to achieve that result contemplated legal change. [25]

**The programmer option is strongest selectively.** It is persuasive where the programmer also supplies the relevant expressive conception or qualifies under an applicable arranger rule. Mere authorship of a general-purpose tool is a weaker basis for claiming all downstream works.

### Option two: assigning copyright to the user

**User ownership follows the creative-tool analogy.** A photographer can author a photograph without designing the camera. Similarly, an artist may use complex software while remaining responsible for the expressive choices. CONTU’s 1978 report, discussed by Palace, treated computers as instruments activated by people and consequently favored the user as the relevant copyright holder. [25]

**Meaningful direction strengthens the user’s position.** A user who supplies original material, determines composition, adjusts results toward a creative objective, and makes substantial original edits has a stronger claim than someone who merely initiates automatic generation. In coding, the same reasoning favors a developer’s original architecture, implementation, and revisions over the bare act of accepting a suggestion.

**The advantage is practical alignment.** Users are often the parties choosing the project, bearing the cost of integrating the result, and bringing it to an audience or market. Allocating existing rights to them can reduce friction and support creative experimentation. Williams’s 2021 analysis argues that users’ judgment should receive recognition without requiring them to foresee every detail of the computational outcome. [10]

**The camera analogy has a boundary.** In April 2022, Holland & Knight described AI art programs as tools and favored user ownership, but also acknowledged developers’ possible contractual claims. Its broad analogy should be read alongside the Copyright Office’s human-authorship position: pressing a button does not invariably establish authorship of expression selected by an automated process. [12]

**Contracts allocate rights that legally exist.** Tool terms can assign existing rights, reserve royalties, or create obligations between developer and user. They do not by themselves create statutory copyright in otherwise unprotected material or erase a third party’s rights. User ownership is therefore most convincing when supported by both meaningful creative contribution and an appropriate contractual allocation.

### Option three: granting no copyright

**No copyright maximizes public access.** Where output lacks a qualifying human author, leaving it unprotected avoids inventing an author merely to create exclusivity. Other people can reuse, adapt, and combine that new material without obtaining permission from the system operator. Palace’s 2019 note recommends immediate public-domain treatment for autonomous output on these grounds. [25]

**The machine needs no financial reward.** Copyright incentives ordinarily address human decisions to create and invest. A machine does not need recognition or royalties to execute its processes. This weakens the argument for assigning rights to AI itself, although it does not eliminate the separate question of incentives for the people funding and operating it. [19]

**Investment is the strongest counterargument.** Burylo presents the concern that denying output exclusivity could reduce incentives to develop expensive systems, particularly for commercial projects. Palace counters that developers can still receive income from software, services, and related business models, while users retain the ability to exploit outputs without exclusive copyright. These are competing incentive arguments, rather than demonstrated numerical forecasts of investment effects. [19][25]

**Access also affects competition.** Automatically protecting vast quantities of generated material could concentrate rights in a small number of technology owners and increase clearance burdens for later creators. Conversely, a purchaser who needs exclusivity in a particular image or software component may find unprotected output commercially less attractive. The policy trade-off concerns the additional benefit of exclusivity, not whether unprotected material can have economic value.

**Public-domain treatment must remain precisely scoped.** It concerns the new output’s claim to protection. It does not release copyrighted photographs, articles, or code reproduced within that output, and it does not remove copyright from a person’s separately original additions or arrangement.

### The ownership judgment

**Human contribution provides the strongest ordinary basis.** Where a user or programmer makes identifiable original expressive choices, existing authorship principles can support protection for those contributions. Where a jurisdiction supplies a computer-generated-work rule, that statutory allocation must also be considered. Training-data owners retain rights in their inputs but do not automatically become co-authors of every output influenced by them.

**The policy preference should remain conditional.** Rights should follow demonstrated human expression or an applicable statutory attribution rule, rather than mere possession of a model. For genuinely autonomous output outside such a rule, public access is more defensible than assuming that someone must own a copyright; that judgment would change if the applicable law or persuasive evidence of necessary additional incentives established a different basis.

## 4. Case Study: GitHub Copilot

### What Copilot represented by early 2022

**Copilot made the training question concrete.** GitHub announced Copilot on 29 June 2021 as a tool suggesting code for developers. Its underlying approach used code and natural-language context to generate completions, bringing the debate about learning from protected works directly into everyday software development. The controversy continued through the early-2022 discussions considered here. [26][27]

**The reported training scale was substantial.** GitHub described Copilot as trained on **billions of lines of public code**, an approximate company estimate of corpus scale. Its suggestions were adapted to the user’s code while being informed by code written by others. This scale explains the importance of permissions and provenance, but it does not establish the copyright status of any particular repository or suggestion. [4]

**Its generation differed from ordinary search.** April 2022 commentary by Neil Ernst and Gabriele Bavota described Copilot’s underlying Codex system as a language model trained to associate text and code patterns. It could generate a completion fitting a developer’s comments and surrounding program, rather than simply return a search result with an identified source. That difference supported both the novelty argument and the concern that copied material could arrive without source information. [28]

### Controversy one: training on open-source and public code

**Public code is not necessarily permissionless code.** “Public” describes accessibility. “Open source” describes licensing that permits specified uses under stated conditions. A public repository can contain code carrying permissive or copyleft conditions, while the absence of a reuse license does not itself supply broad copyright permission. [7]

| Source category | Permission position | Training question | Output-reuse question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public code without a reuse license | Access alone grants no general copying right | Permission or an exception must justify restricted acts | Protected copying requires its own justification |
| Permissive code, such as MIT-licensed code | Broad reuse subject to conditions | Assess the relevant permission and conditions | Preserve required notices when applicable |
| Copyleft code, such as GPL-licensed code | Broad reuse with reciprocal conditions | Assess the training acts and applicable exception | Covered distribution can trigger source and licensing duties |

**The strongest U.S. defense was transformation.** Rothchild and Rothchild argued that repository code originally served a particular computing purpose—sorting data or performing a calculation—whereas GitHub used it to teach a system to generate code from descriptions. They considered this a strong changed-purpose argument under the first fair-use factor, capable of outweighing the service’s commercial character. [7]

**The market argument followed that distinction.** On this view, a code-generating tool does not necessarily replace the original program’s function or market. Its learning process can create a different service rather than redistribute a repository. This argument is strongest when outputs are independently generated implementations rather than substitutes incorporating the source’s protected expression. [7]

**The objections addressed more than commerciality.** Bradley Kuhn emphasized the distinction between Google Books-style search and a system producing new or derived code. He also questioned the consequences of using copyleft material in a commercial service whose suggestions could omit the conditions under which contributors had shared their work. The disagreement concerned the scope of existing permission and exceptions, not simply whether open-source authors approved of the technology. [29]

**GitHub’s terms supplied another argument.** In FOSSA’s initial analysis, lawyer Kate Downing read GitHub’s terms as authorizing use of hosted code to improve products and features. That was a contractual interpretation, distinct from fair use. Its practical reach depended on the permission actually granted, the rights held by the person granting it, and whether the use fell within its scope; it did not automatically settle downstream redistribution of copied suggestions. [30]

**EU treatment required a separate analysis.** A commercial code-training operation relying on EU exceptions would principally need to consider Article 4’s lawful-access, covered-rights, and reservation conditions. An eligible research partnership could involve Article 3, but commercial development alone did not qualify its operator for that route. Calling Copilot training fair use in the United States therefore did not answer the corresponding EU question.

### What GitHub’s recitation study actually measured

**GitHub documented occasional verbatim recitation.** Its early internal study examined 453,780 Python suggestions across 396 “user weeks,” using data with a 7 May 2021 cutoff. A user week meant a calendar week during which a participant actively used Copilot for Python. The study therefore supplied evidence about a defined internal population and activity, not a universal rate across languages or users. [4]

**The detector used a specific threshold.** GitHub first looked for overlap of at least 60 “words,” counting punctuation and other special characters while ignoring whitespace. The matching sequence could include code already present in the user’s context. This was a technical screening rule chosen for the investigation, not a legal threshold for substantial copying. [4]

| Analysis stage | Count | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Python suggestions examined | 453,780 | Initial study population |
| Suggestions passing overlap screening | 473 | Candidates for manual examination |
| Suggestions after duplicate removal | 185 | Remaining classified suggestions |
| Standard or otherwise excluded cases | 144 | Cases outside the study’s recitation category |
| Manually classified recitation cases | 41 | Specific overlap in code or comments |

**The counts support a bounded frequency estimate.** GitHub reported approximately one recitation event per 10 user weeks, with a 95% confidence interval of 7–13 user weeks using a Poisson test. The underlying calculation is consistent with that rounded frequency: 396 user weeks divided by 41 classified events is approximately 9.7 user weeks per event. This is an observational recitation measure, not an infringement probability. [4]

**The initial flags measured something broader.** The screening share was approximately 0.104%—calculated as 473 flagged suggestions divided by 453,780 suggestions, multiplied by 100. Most flagged candidates did not become the study’s final recitation cases. Treating the initial screening percentage, the final event count, and legal infringement as interchangeable would therefore misstate the evidence.

**Repeated exposure accompanied the observed cases.** None of the 41 classified cases appeared in fewer than 10 training files, and 35 appeared more than 100 times. One suggested text had appeared more than 700,000 times: the GNU General Public License itself. These observations support a repeated-exposure explanation for the detected recitations, while not proving that every repeated snippet would be reproduced. [4]

**GPL text is not GPL program code.** The more-than-700,000 figure referred to exposure to the license text, not to 700,000 proven infringing suggestions or an equivalent count of copied programs. Kuhn separately reported examples in which users obtained substantial distinctive GPL-licensed code and a suggested non-copyleft license. Those reports raised an output-compliance concern that required examining the particular code and license. [4][29]

**Context also affected observed recitation.** GitHub found quoting more likely in generic contexts, particularly near the beginning of files, than in more distinctive surrounding code. Its proposed response was to identify matching sources in the interface so users could attribute or reject quoted material. The study stated that its duplication search had not yet been integrated into the technical preview at publication. [4]

### The separate April 2022 T5 clone study

**The independent experiment studied T5, not Copilot.** Matteo Ciniselli, Luca Pascarella, and Gabriele Bavota used an inspectable T5 code-completion model so they could compare generated predictions directly with its training material. The model was pre-trained on approximately two million Java methods, with a fine-tuning collection initially containing 841,318 methods. Four versions varied the removal of near-duplicate training examples. [31]

**The reported analysis used a defined subset.** The test set contained 15,742 completion instances. For the principal results, the authors excluded 6,986 instances in which at least one model generated an unknown token, leaving 8,756 instances per model: 15,742 minus 6,986. Thus, the main percentages describe this experimental comparison rather than everyday Copilot use. [31]

**Clone definitions materially changed the rate.** Type-1 clones were exact copies under the detector’s comparison; Type-2 clones also allowed changes to identifiers and types while preserving the relevant structure. Type-2 includes Type-1, so their percentages are not additive. The headline findings were approximately 10% Type-1 clones and up to approximately 80% Type-2 clones for short predictions. [31]

| Minimum predicted length | Type-1 clone rate | Experimental setting |
|---|---:|---|
| At least 2 statements | 12.5% | Weak deduplication |
| At least 3 statements | 2.3% | Weak deduplication |
| At least 5 statements | 0.1% | Weak deduplication |

**Longer predictions matched less frequently.** In the detailed weak-deduplication configuration, the Type-1 rate fell from 12.5% for predictions of at least two statements to 0.1% for at least five statements. The authors summarized longer-output cloning as approaching zero. The positive 0.1% result shows why that summary should be understood as very low measured frequency, not a guarantee of no copying. [31]

**The two studies answer different questions.** GitHub’s study classified recitation during internal Python use; the T5 experiment measured Java completion matches against known training sets under controlled tasks. Together they show that generation and copying can coexist. They do not establish Copilot’s infringement rate, and a structural clone match alone does not determine whether the matched material contains protectable expression.

**Frequency and legal consequence can diverge.** Short predictions may match frequently because code contains conventional patterns with limited expressive freedom. A rare longer match may nevertheless reproduce more distinctive material. Accordingly, the experimental observation that longer matches were less common does not imply that a distinctive long match carries less legal significance when one occurs.

### Controversy two: when a suggestion infringes existing code

**Similarity must concern protected expression.** The central output question is whether a suggestion reproduces enough copyrightable expression from an existing work to constitute copying or an unauthorized adaptation. Performing the same computation is not sufficient by itself: copyright does not confer exclusive ownership of an algorithm or result merely because someone implemented it first. [27]

**Common fragments present a different case.** In August 2021 commentary, Andres Guadamuz argued that small, commonly used snippets were unlikely to amount to substantial reproduction or satisfy the originality threshold. That reasoning is persuasive for conventional fragments, but its premise matters. It does not resolve a suggestion containing a distinctive implementation, unusual comments, or a substantial sequence copied from one source. [32]

**Renaming is not a complete answer.** Changing variable names or formatting can leave recognizable expressive structure intact. Conversely, structural similarity identified by a detector may concern conventional or functionally constrained material. The legal assessment must distinguish those situations rather than treating either an exact-match detector or the absence of exact text as conclusive.

**These hypothetical examples show the distinction.** They illustrate legal applications, not measured Copilot events.

| Hypothetical suggestion | Relevant characteristic | Likely legal focus |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional short loop | Common, functionally constrained expression | Whether protectable expression was copied |
| Distinctive helper with original comments | Recognizable source-specific choices | Substantial copying and license compliance |
| Renamed version of a distinctive block | Similar expression despite identifier changes | Whether adaptation or copying remains substantial |
| Independently written implementation | Same function, different expression | Authorship of the new implementation |

**There is no safe line count.** U.S. fair use has no predetermined number of permissible code lines. EU law likewise asks whether a reproduced part expresses the author’s intellectual creation: *Infopaq* established that even an 11-word extract could qualify when that condition was met, not that every 11-word extract automatically infringed. The appropriate lesson is qualitative assessment, not adoption of a new numerical threshold. [1][13]

**Training permission does not settle suggestion reuse.** Even if GitHub’s training copies were fair use, a developer distributing a copied suggestion would still need to consider its own legal basis. Equally, a suggestion free of protected source expression does not retrospectively decide the legality of the copies made during training. Each act must be assessed under the permission or exception relevant to it.

### Attribution and open-source obligations

**Open-source conditions can survive automated delivery.** If a suggestion substantially reproduces protected licensed code, the fact that an AI supplied it does not necessarily remove the license conditions governing that code. Permissive licenses may require preservation of copyright and license notices; copyleft licenses can impose additional obligations when covered material is conveyed or distributed. [29]

**Copyleft is not triggered by mere influence.** It would be too broad to say that every output from a model trained on GPL code must itself be GPL-licensed. The relevant inquiry is whether the output incorporates protected material for which permission is required and what the license requires for the acts undertaken. Guadamuz’s analysis of GPLv3 emphasizes the threshold of copying substantial enough to require copyright permission. [32]

**Distribution makes the obligations consequential.** Where GPL-covered code is incorporated into a distributed derivative work, applicable obligations may include licensing the covered work under the GPL and providing corresponding source. The exact scope depends on the code, integration, license version, and distribution circumstances. Private use, training, and distribution should not be collapsed into a single event automatically producing identical duties.

**Attribution and permission are different.** Crediting an author does not authorize copying that otherwise requires permission. Conversely, omitting a required notice can create a compliance problem even where the license would otherwise permit reuse. Attribution also has an ethical dimension, but copyright infringement, license compliance, and plagiarism are not identical concepts.

**Source identification addresses a real information problem.** A developer cannot easily comply with a particular source license when a suggestion arrives without an identified source. GitHub’s proposal to show matching origins would help users examine permissions and preserve notices. Its usefulness would depend on detection quality and the ability to distinguish meaningful protected matches from routine code. [4]

**Operator ownership is not an infringement shield.** April 2022 commentary reported that Codex beta output was treated as the intellectual work product of the person activating it. That was a reported platform allocation, not a ruling that all such output carried copyright or that third-party claims disappeared. Kuhn similarly warned that an operator-ownership assurance was not an indemnity against possible GPL enforcement. [28][29]

### The Copilot judgment through April 2022

**The strongest defense concerned learning purpose.** Copilot had a credible U.S. argument that training used programs for a different function: learning to generate code rather than executing each source program’s task. The open-source community’s objections nevertheless identified consequential questions about permission, contracts, retained expression, and redistribution. By the cutoff, the legal position rested on statutory arguments, existing analogies, and commentary rather than a direct judicial determination of Copilot’s training or a particular suggestion. [7][29]

**The clearest output risk was recognizable reproduction.** Evidence showed that Copilot could recite material, while the studies also distinguished that behavior from its general production of context-adapted suggestions. The legally important response was therefore to assess distinctive matches and their licenses, rather than assume either that every suggestion was copied or that generated code was automatically clear of existing rights.

**The conclusion changes with the expression.** A routine functional fragment supports a different assessment from a distinctive reproduced implementation. Reliable source identification, a valid license, or a genuinely independent human implementation can materially change the result; a platform label asserting user ownership cannot substitute for those facts.

### Before publishing AI-generated art or code

**A short review follows the analysis.** The practical sequence is to establish the relevant jurisdiction, identify the material and permissions involved, and then examine the output and human contribution.

1. Identify where relevant copying and distribution occur; U.S. fair use is not a worldwide permission.
2. Keep records of source material, licenses, lawful access, and applicable EU rights reservations.
3. Examine outputs for recognizable protected images, wording, comments, or distinctive code.
4. Record the human expressive choices and edits supporting any claim of authorship.
5. Where reuse relies on a license, preserve required notices and satisfy its applicable distribution conditions.

**The evidence has distinct measurement bases.** This report concerns information through 30 April 2022. Statutes, judicial decisions, administrative registration practice, scholarly arguments, and policy proposals have different legal force. GitHub’s billion-line corpus description is an approximate company estimate; its internal Python recitation measure and the separate experimental Java clone rates concern different populations and definitions. The labeled calculations explain those figures, not probabilities of infringement.

**The closing judgment is straightforward.** Generative AI did not remove copyright’s ordinary questions: what was copied, under what permission, whose expression remains, and which human contribution supports protection. Keeping those questions separate gives artists and programmers a substantially clearer answer than treating either training or generation as a single legal category.

By 30 April 2022, GitHub Copilot was GitHub’s code-suggestion tool, and contemporary reporting described its legal status as unsettled because no clear ruling had decided whether training on publicly available code was lawful [117]. A 25 March 2022 law-school presentation likewise treated Copilot and “AI-generated code” as a copyright issue and identified GPL licences as implicated [266]. The controversy therefore has two stages: training on public or open-source code, and whether a later suggestion reproduces protected expression.

GitHub’s public position was that training machine-learning systems on publicly available data was fair use [117]. It also said that Copilot suggestions and code written with its help belonged to the user, who was responsible for testing, reviewing and vetting them [255]. These were provider representations, not judicial findings. The evidence about output provenance was mixed: one contemporaneous account quoted GitHub’s report that Copilot regurgitated learned code only **0.1%** of the time, while also describing online suggestions containing large amounts of source code and original comments [255].

Open-source code is not automatically public-domain code. It can remain copyrighted, and its licence can require credit or attribution, information about applicable terms, and compliance with copyleft conditions; GPL and AGPL licences were identified as relevant examples [266][95]. Training authorization, licence compliance, and distribution of an output are consequently separate questions. The analysis available at the cutoff treated training as something that **may** be fair use, while emphasizing that distributing model output is a distinct issue and that the law was unsettled [95]. In U.S. fair-use terms, the argument still turns on purpose and character—including transformation—nature of the work, amount and substantiality, and market effect [95].

For a generated code snippet, the comparison is with existing copyrighted code: does the snippet reproduce protectable expression, and is the reproduced amount substantial? Longer or more complex suggestions are more likely to contain protectable expression; a short repeated fragment may not, and functional similarity alone does not resolve infringement [255]. The reported **Q_rsqrt** example, in which Copilot reproduced the Quake Arena function with comments and profanity, illustrated the risk but was not a judicial finding [255]. Google Books supplied a transformative, non-substitutive analogy, whereas an opposing analysis stressed that repeated requests could expose substantial source portions and that generated code could compete with the original [255][95]. Thus, at the cutoff, Copilot infringement was a possible, fact-specific and unresolved conclusion—not an established one.
