# Sugar Substitutes Before March 2019: A Comparison and Three Proposed Health Mechanisms

**The main distinction is nutritional.** High-intensity sweeteners provide strong sweetness in tiny amounts; sugar alcohols usually provide bulk and some energy; and natural-origin sweeteners differ according to the compound, its purity, and the finished formulation. The three proposed negative-effect mechanisms are altered sweet-receptor signaling, changes in gut microbes, and weakened learning that sweetness predicts calories. Evidence available by **February 28, 2019** supported investigating all three, but did not establish that non-nutritive sweeteners universally cause metabolic disease in humans. [1]

**The report follows two questions.** First, which ingredients, brands, industrial applications, and sweetness values belong together? Second, how could sweetness without much energy affect the body, and how well do experiments support those explanations? The comparison comes first; the three mechanisms follow in their requested order.

**Read multipliers as ingredient potency.** Sucrose, or ordinary table sugar, is the reference at **1×**: a value of 0.5× means approximately half as sweet, whereas 200× means approximately 200 times as sweet under the comparison conditions. These are sensory comparisons for the named ingredients, not spoon-for-spoon replacement instructions for retail packets. Manufacturers frequently combine sweeteners with one another, sugar, or bulking ingredients. [2]

**The principal reference values agree.** FDA’s historical summary supports the ingredient identities, major applications, and headline values for the first six entries. A 2014 food-science review supplies the 180–200× aspartame range, the 300× saccharin comparison, and the 30–50× cyclamate range; its main comparison table uses a 10% sucrose solution. [3][4]

**Brands identify formulations, not necessarily molecules.** The January 2016 University of Arizona reference associates Sugar Twin with both aspartame and saccharin, while the 2014 review associates that name with cyclamate in its international discussion. Those associations make the practical point: the ingredient list for the particular country and product is more informative than the brand name alone. [5][4]

**Aspartame suits beverages better than baking.** Its approximately 200-fold potency explains its use in drinks, gelatin products, and tabletop sweeteners, but it loses sweetness when heated. FDA therefore described it as typically unsuitable for baked goods, despite its authorization for general-purpose food use. This is a formulation distinction, separate from its health assessment. [3]

**Aspartame has a specific medical exception.** It supplies phenylalanine, an amino acid that people with phenylketonuria, or PKU, must carefully restrict. U.S. labels must identify its presence for that population. This established, ingredient-specific issue should be kept separate from the three broader metabolic hypotheses discussed later. [6]

**Low-calorie does not always mean indigestible.** Aspartame provides about 4 kcal/g, approximately the same energy per gram as sugar, but its much greater sweetness allows a far smaller quantity to be used. Its low energy contribution is therefore principally a consequence of dose. A digestible sweetener can be effectively non-nutritive at food-use levels without being chemically incapable of providing energy. [5]

**Saccharin illustrates concentration-dependent sweetness.** FDA’s broad 200–700× range and the 2014 review’s 300× value are compatible with a sensory property that changes with the reference concentration and food. A single multiplier should not be interpreted as a constant that applies equally to a lightly sweetened drink, a concentrated syrup, and a confection. Its permitted applications include cooking and processed foods as well as beverages and tabletop products. [3][4]

**Sucralose offers broad formulation flexibility.** Its approximately 600-fold potency and documented use in beverages, baked goods, gum, gelatins, and frozen dairy desserts distinguish it from heat-sensitive aspartame. U.S. authorization initially covered 15 food categories in 1998 and expanded to general-purpose use in 1999. Those dates describe the U.S. regulatory history, not a simultaneous worldwide introduction. [3]

**Splenda is not pure sucralose.** A formulation described in the 2016 gastrointestinal review contained approximately 1% sucralose and 99% carbohydrate carrier by dry weight. The 600× figure applies to sucralose itself, not to that entire mixture. This distinction matters both when calculating food substitutions and when interpreting experiments that administered a branded preparation instead of the isolated compound. [7]

**Ace-K commonly works in blends.** FDA described acesulfame potassium as heat-stable and frequently combined with other sweeteners. Product examples in the gastrointestinal review paired it with aspartame in diet drinks and yogurt, and with sucralose in low-calorie sports drinks. These combinations are used to obtain a desired flavor profile rather than simply to maximize the largest possible sweetness number. [3][7]

**Neotame supplies sweetness at very low concentrations.** Its reported 7,000–13,000× range is substantially above those of the familiar packet sweeteners. FDA described it as suitable for baking and authorized it as a general-purpose sweetener and flavor enhancer in 2002, except in meat and poultry. A 2014 review gave approximately 8,000× as an average within its broader range. [3][4]

**Advantame leads on headline potency.** Among these entries, FDA’s approximately 20,000× figure is the highest headline value. JECFA’s more detailed 2013 assessment reported approximately 7,000–48,000× across applications corresponding to 3–14% sucrose equivalence. At a 6% sucrose-equivalence condition in water, it reported approximately 37,000×. These numbers describe different comparison conditions rather than competing measurements of one fixed constant. [8]

**Advantame’s stability remains application-specific.** FDA described it as usable in baking, but the technical assessment shows why “heat-stable” should not mean “unchanged under every process.” One short heat treatment of an orange-flavored beverage produced less than 2% loss, whereas approximately 50% of baseline advantame degraded during 26 weeks of room-temperature storage in a carbonated soft drink. Acidity, temperature, storage duration, and the surrounding food all affected performance. [8]

**Permission also depends on food category.** U.S. regulations allowed advantame as a sweetening agent and flavor enhancer in foods generally, except meat and poultry, at amounts consistent with good manufacturing practice. The European Union’s 2014 authorization assigned E969 and specified categories and maximum levels. For example, it permitted 400 mg/kg in no-added-sugar chewing gum but 6 mg/L or mg/kg, as appropriate, in specified energy-reduced or no-added-sugar flavored drinks. [9][10]

**Cyclamate requires the clearest geographic distinction.** It belonged in an international comparison because it was used outside the United States, including in the European Union. It was prohibited in U.S. foods, however, so a cyclamate-containing tabletop product from another market should not be treated as an equivalent U.S. formulation. The original UK sweetener schedule documented uses in specified drinks, desserts, confectionery, and chewing gum. [6][11][12]

**Regulatory safety and nutritional usefulness differ.** By 2014, FDA listed six high-intensity sweeteners approved as food additives: saccharin, aspartame, Ace-K, sucralose, neotame, and advantame. It concluded that approved uses were safe for the general population under their specified conditions. That conclusion addresses safety at relevant exposure levels; it does not establish that every product containing a sweetener improves diet quality or produces weight loss. [6]

**Takeaway for high-intensity sweeteners.** Choose the exact ingredient and application before interpreting potency or health evidence: neither a brand name nor a large sweetness multiplier establishes the amount consumed or its physiological effect.

**Polyols are principally bulk ingredients.** The September 2004 International Food Information Council, or IFIC, fact sheet supplies the principal sweetness ranges and applications above. It also identifies maltitol syrup and HSH as carbohydrate-derived mixtures, rather than single pure molecules. Their usefulness extends beyond sweetness to texture, moisture retention, cooling sensations, and replacing some of sugar’s physical bulk. [13]

**Identifiers are not brand names.** E420, E421, E953, E965, E966, E967, and E968 are European ingredient identifiers. The named gums and beverages are examples of finished products containing those ingredients, not alternative names for the pure polyols. In the 2016 product table, Doublemint combined sorbitol, xylitol, maltitol, and mannitol with other sweetening ingredients. [14][7]

**Sweetness and bulk are different functions.** A small amount of a high-intensity sweetener can replace sugar’s sweetness but cannot, by itself, replace the mass of sugar removed from a chocolate bar or baked product. Polyols can supply that mass while also contributing sweetness. Thus, a product may combine a relatively weak bulk sweetener, such as lactitol, with an intense sweetener to recover both texture and the desired sweetness. [13][15]

**Reference sweetness varies between preparations.** IFIC reported xylitol at approximately 1.0× sucrose, while Ruiz-Ojeda and colleagues reported approximately 0.95×. For maltitol, IFIC’s table gave 0.75×, whereas the later review described approximately 0.90×. The useful conclusion is not to average these into a falsely exact universal number: the values belong to their ingredient and formulation comparisons. [13][16]

**The energy columns use different conventions.** IFIC’s 2004 table provides its stated ingredient values, including 3 kcal/g for HSH. Zumbe, Lee, and Storey’s 2001 review supplies the historical U.S. figures for six polyols. EU food-label rules assign polyols 2.4 kcal/g except erythritol, assigned 0 kcal/g. These differences concern the reference or legal calculation used, not a change in the ingredient’s chemistry at a national border. [13][17][18]

**Erythritol is the low-energy exception.** The European Scientific Committee on Food’s 2003 opinion described it as approximately 60–80% as sweet as sucrose and considered a claimed energy range of 0–0.2 kcal/g. Its low energy contribution distinguishes it from sorbitol, xylitol, maltitol, lactitol, and isomalt, even though all belong to the same broad chemical family. The EU’s zero labeling factor should consequently not be generalized to other polyols. [19]

**The main immediate concern is digestive tolerance.** Slowly or incompletely absorbed polyols can leave material in the intestine that draws in water and becomes available for bacterial fermentation. Fermentation produces gases and other compounds; together, these processes can cause bloating, discomfort, or loose stools. This is a more direct explanation for many polyol-related symptoms than the proposed long-term metabolic mechanisms examined in the second part. [13][14]

**Serving pattern changes the experience.** IFIC emphasized total intake, the amount eaten in one sitting, food type, whether the stomach was empty, individual response, and adaptation over time. A person consuming several polyol-containing products therefore encounters a different exposure from someone eating a small portion of one product. “Sugar-free” describes sugar content; it does not mean that an unlimited portion will have no digestive effect. [13]

**The EU warning is product-based.** Foods containing more than 10% added polyols must carry the advisory statement that excessive consumption may produce laxative effects. That is a labeling threshold for the food’s composition, not a clinical claim that every individual experiences symptoms at the same dose. [14]

**Polyols can change microbes beneficially too.** Some unabsorbed polyols serve as substrates for bacteria, and the literature describes prebiotic-type effects for ingredients including lactitol and isomalt. “Prebiotic” here means favoring the growth or activity of particular intestinal microbes. These observations are important counterexamples to the idea that any sweetener-associated microbial change must be harmful. [14]

**Dental applications follow a separate mechanism.** Polyols generally do not support the acid production by mouth bacteria that drives tooth decay in the same way as readily fermentable sugars. Xylitol’s use in gum, mints, and oral-care products reflects that distinction. Evidence concerning bacteria in the mouth should nevertheless not be treated as a direct measurement of the intestinal microbiota, where conditions and exposures differ. [13]

**Takeaway for sugar alcohols.** Treat polyols as functional, usually energy-containing sugar replacements whose main practical trade-off is between bulk and reduced sugar on one side, and dose-dependent digestive tolerance on the other.

### Natural Sweeteners

| Sweetener Name | Brand Name | Primary Uses in the Food Industry | Sweetness Multiplier Relative to Sucrose | Nutritive Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Purified steviol glycosides, including stevioside and rebaudioside A | Truvia; PureVia; Enliten; Sun Crystals; Bestevia for specified Reb A | Tabletop products, beverages, juices, yogurt, desserts, baked goods, flavor modification | Approximately 200–400×; high-purity Reb A 200–300× | Nonnutritive at food-use levels; blends differ |
| Monk-fruit extracts and mogrosides, including mogroside V | Purefruit ingredient brand | Beverages, dairy, frozen desserts, juices, bakery mixes, granola, mints, protein products | Approximately 100–250×; Purefruit report 150–220× | Refined extracts: nonnutritive; sugary concentrates differ |
| Thaumatin I and II | Talin; San Sweet T-100 | Flavor enhancement, bitterness masking, beverages, gum, desserts, low-fat and soy products | Approximately 2,000–3,000× on a weight/volume basis | Digestible protein; negligible energy at usual use levels |

**These are three different ingredient families.** Steviol glycosides come from *Stevia rebaudiana*; monk-fruit sweetness comes principally from compounds called mogrosides in *Siraitia grosvenorii*; and thaumatin is a sweet protein associated with *Thaumatococcus daniellii*. Their common plant origin does not make their chemistry, digestion, or industrial uses interchangeable. [3][20]

**Purified stevia is not whole leaf.** FDA’s historical description applied the 200–400× range to high-purity steviol-glycoside preparations, including specified preparations with at least 95% purity. Whole-leaf stevia and crude extracts were not permitted as U.S. food sweeteners under that position. The distinction is between defined preparations, not a judgment that every substance associated with the plant has identical properties. [6]

**Rebaudioside A provides a concrete example.** The March 2016 GRAS notice for the Bestevia preparation described a product containing at least 97% rebaudioside A, or Reb A, with approximately 200–300× sucrose sweetness. Its intended uses included tabletop and general-purpose sweetening and flavor modification, excluding infant formula and meat and poultry products. Those specifications attach the potency and use description to an identifiable preparation. [21]

**Retail stevia products can include sugar.** Historical product examples included Coca-Cola Life with stevia and sugar, Crystal Light Pure Fitness with stevia and sugar, and VitaminWater Zero with erythritol and stevia. Consequently, the presence of a non-nutritive active ingredient does not make the whole food calorie-free. It is the complete formulation, rather than the word “stevia,” that determines energy intake. [7]

**Stevia’s processing performance is also conditional.** A 2017 steviol-glycoside submission summarized stability under normal food-processing and acidic-beverage conditions, while also documenting greater breakdown during prolonged heating at higher temperatures and lower pH. This supports their use in numerous foods without suggesting that purity, processing temperature, and storage conditions can be ignored. [22]

**Monk-fruit potency follows extract composition.** FDA’s historical summary reported approximately 100–250× sucrose sweetness depending on mogroside content. A 2012 industry report described Purefruit at approximately 150–220× and highlighted beverage and dairy applications, as well as frozen desserts, juices, bakery mixes, granola, mints, and protein products. These are preparation-specific values, not a multiplier for every product derived from the fruit. [3][23]

**Juice concentrate is a different product.** FDA’s October 2016 response described one monk-fruit juice concentrate containing approximately 56% sugars, 35% water, 3.5% mogroside V, and 1.5% other mogrosides. That sugar-containing mixture should not inherit the non-nutritive classification of a refined extract simply because both originate from monk fruit. The same letter cited a 100× value for a different fruit-extract preparation, designated SGFE. [24]

**Its sugars make the distinction measurable.** The concentrate supplies an estimated **2.24 kcal/g from sugars alone**, calculated as 0.56 g sugars per gram of concentrate × 4 kcal/g. That is not an estimate of its entire energy content, but it is sufficient to classify the concentrate as a caloric mixture rather than an energy-free purified sweetener. Its documented usual use levels of 0.25–0.50% in food also illustrate why ingredient energy density and finished-food energy contribution are different questions. [24][13]

**GRAS is a distinct regulatory pathway.** “Generally recognized as safe,” or GRAS, is not the same procedure as approval of a food additive. In its 2016 monk-fruit response, FDA said it had no questions about the notifier’s conclusion under the intended conditions, while explicitly stating that it had not made its own GRAS determination. This is more precise than describing every monk-fruit preparation as independently “FDA-approved.” [6][24]

**Thaumatin is an especially potent protein.** JECFA described preparations consisting essentially of thaumatin I and II obtained from the fruit’s fleshy seed covering. The 2009 ingredient factsheet reported approximately 2,000–3,000× sucrose sweetness on a weight/volume basis and emphasized flavor modification as well as sweetening. A 2017 GRAS submission hosted by FDA identified Talin and San Sweet T-100 as commercial names used in the United States, Europe, Japan, and elsewhere. [20][25][26]

The multiplier is explicitly a weight/volume comparison and is approximate; thaumatin's effect is context-dependent and can modify or mask other flavors. It is authorized as E957/INS 957 in relevant jurisdictions and can be used alone or with other sweeteners.

**Flavor modification explains its niche.** Thaumatin can help mask bitterness or undesirable flavor notes rather than supply all of a product’s sweetness. A 2004 Talin report described applications in low-fat, low-carbohydrate, and soy-based products, including reducing the amount of other sweeteners needed. The “primary use” question therefore has a broader answer than simply replacing table sugar. [27]

**Protein metabolism does not negate low energy.** Thaumatin is metabolized as dietary protein, but its very high potency means that usual sweetening quantities contribute negligible energy. Its practical classification is therefore a virtually calorie-free or very-low-calorie sweetener at use levels, not a protein that possesses no nutritional energy under any circumstances. [25]

**Authorization remains use-specific here too.** Thaumatin was identified as E957 in European schedules, with specified uses and levels. The original 1995 UK schedule, for example, listed 50 mg/kg in certain no-added-sugar confectionery and chewing gum, and 400 mg/kg in vitamins and dietary preparations. JECFA’s “ADI not specified” designation meant that a numerical daily limit was not considered necessary under the evaluated uses and good manufacturing practice—not that unlimited consumption was the intended use. [12][20]

**The catalog centers on common substitutes.** Alitame is a limited-market comparator: the 2014 review reported approximately 2,000× sweetness and approval in Mexico, Australia, New Zealand, and China. Brazzein and monatin also appeared in a 2012 review covering commercial and developing natural high-potency sweeteners. They sit outside the main common-use table rather than being treated as equivalent to the established retail and food-industry ingredients above. [4][28]

**Takeaway for natural sweeteners.** Judge the purified compound, extract composition, and complete food formulation; “natural” alone predicts neither energy content nor metabolic safety.

## Summary of Three Mechanisms

**The mechanisms ask different questions.** Receptor studies ask whether the body detects a sweetener and changes its immediate signals. Microbiota studies ask whether exposure changes intestinal organisms in ways that affect metabolism. Learning studies ask whether repeated experience changes how sweetness predicts energy. These routes can overlap, but evidence for one is not automatically evidence for the others. [1]

| Proposed Mechanism | Possible Path to a Negative Effect | Strongest Supporting Evidence | Pre-Cutoff Human Finding | Overall Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sweet-receptor signaling | Altered hormones or glucose absorption | Cell, animal, and human receptor-blocking experiments | Acute responses varied by context | Physiologically plausible; no uniform adverse response |
| Gut-microbiota changes | Microbial changes alter glucose handling or inflammatory signals | Antibiotic and microbiota-transfer experiments in mice | Saccharin response in 4 of 7 volunteers | Strong animal mechanism; heterogeneous human response |
| Sweetness–energy learning | Sweetness becomes a less reliable calorie cue | Controlled rat-conditioning experiments | Mixed feeding results; a 20-day conditioning study was null | Rodent-supported hypothesis, not inevitable human overeating |

**Metabolic activity is not necessarily harm.** A change in a gut hormone, a bacterial community, or brain activity shows a biological response; its health meaning depends on direction, duration, and consequences. Likewise, an association between diet-drink use and diabetes does not by itself identify a cause: people already gaining weight or developing metabolic problems may switch to diet products. This reverse-causation explanation was explicitly considered in the pre-cutoff literature. [1]

### 1. Interaction With Sweet-Taste Receptors, Especially in the Gut

**Gut detection is real; consequences vary.** Sweeteners can interact with chemical sensors related to those that detect sweetness on the tongue. These sensors occur in intestinal hormone-producing cells, creating a possible route from sweetener exposure to changes in appetite signals, insulin responses, or glucose absorption. Human experiments, however, did not consistently reproduce the effects observed in isolated cells or rodents. [29]

**A receptor is a chemical detector.** On the tongue, the principal sweet receptor is formed by two components, T1R2 and T1R3. When an appropriate sweet compound binds, the taste cell sends a signal that contributes to the perception of sweetness. Related machinery in the gut acts as a chemical-sensing system; it does not make a person consciously “taste” food inside the intestine. [29]

**Intestinal detectors can influence messenger hormones.** Certain gut cells release hormones that tell other organs what has arrived in the digestive tract. Two important examples are GLP-1 and GIP, which help coordinate insulin secretion after food intake. GLP-1 also participates in slowing stomach emptying and promoting fullness; PYY is another hormone involved in appetite signaling. [30]

**The proposed negative pathway is indirect.** A non-nutritive sweetener could activate a sensor without supplying the amount of sugar ordinarily associated with that signal. If this changes the timing or size of gut-hormone release, glucose uptake, or insulin secretion, it could alter the handling of a carbohydrate-containing meal. The adverse hypothesis concerns inappropriate or poorly coordinated responses—not the mere existence of a normal hormone response. [1]

**Glucose transport supplies a second route.** Intestinal cells have proteins that move glucose from the gut into the body. Researchers commonly call two of them SGLT1 and GLUT2; in ordinary language, they are parts of the intestine’s sugar-entry machinery. Rodent experiments suggested that sweet-sensing pathways could increase that machinery, potentially allowing a later glucose load to be absorbed more readily. [29]

**The key experiments do not align uniformly.** The following comparison separates observations about normal glucose sensing, responses to sweeteners, and evidence from whole people.

| Study or Model | Exposure or Test | Main Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human receptor-blocking studies, summarized in 2012 | Glucose plus lactisole | GLP-1 reduced 24–47%; PYY reduced 15–27% | Sweet-sensing machinery can contribute to glucose responses |
| Ma and colleagues, 2009; 7 healthy people | 80 or 800 mg sucralose infused into the stomach | No significant GLP-1, GIP, or insulin rise | Sucralose alone did not reproduce sucrose’s response |
| Human pilot, 2014; 10 healthy adults | Glucose plus aspartame, saccharin, or Ace-K | No consistent glycemic or appetite class effect | Commercially relevant doses behaved inconsistently |
| Pepino’s human work, summarized in 2015 | Sucralose before a glucose load in people with obesity | Altered glucose and insulin responses | Host characteristics and timing may matter |
| Saltiel and colleagues, 2017; rat intestinal preparation | Sweeteners applied inside the gut or through its blood supply | Responses depended on sweetener, route, and concentration | Simple receptor activation did not explain all secretion |

**Blocking studies support a physiological role.** In healthy humans, adding lactisole, a substance that inhibits the human sweet-receptor component T1R3, to glucose delivered into the stomach or intestine reduced GLP-1 by 24–47% and PYY by 15–27%. The important inference is that this sensing system can participate in the body’s response to glucose. It is not equivalent to showing that an ordinary serving of a sweetener produces a harmful response. [29]

**The distinction is logically important.** Preventing a normal glucose signal and adding a different sweet compound are not opposite versions of the same experiment. Glucose supplies energy, is transported across cells, and stimulates additional sensing pathways. A receptor-blocking result therefore establishes a possible component of the system, while leaving open how much a particular sweetener activates it during an actual meal. [31]

**The seven-person sucralose experiment was informative.** In Ma and colleagues’ 2009 randomized study, each participant received, on separate occasions, 50 g sucrose, 80 mg sucralose, 800 mg sucralose, or saline, delivered in 500 mL directly into the stomach. Sucrose increased blood glucose, GLP-1, GIP, and insulin; neither sucralose dose produced those significant increases. Sucralose also did not slow stomach emptying as sucrose did. [32]

**Delivery into the stomach isolates a question.** Bypassing the mouth helps test whether a sweetener acts directly through the gut without oral tasting. That makes this experiment particularly relevant to intestinal signaling. It also means the experiment answers a narrower question than ordinary consumption of a sweet drink with a meal, where taste, food composition, and learned expectations are present together.

**Adding glucose did not reveal a class effect.** The 2014 pilot studied 10 healthy adults receiving 45 g glucose alone or with 150 mg aspartame, 20 mg saccharin, or 85 mg Ace-K in 250 mL water. During the 60-minute observation period, none of the sweeteners changed reported hunger or fullness, and post-hoc comparisons found no significant difference from glucose alone at individual time points. [33]

**A descriptive difference was not decisive.** Ace-K’s average integrated glucose response was 17.4% above that of glucose alone in the pilot, but the relevant post-hoc comparisons did not establish a significant difference. The response was also variable. Selecting that percentage while omitting the statistical result would turn a tentative ingredient-specific observation into a stronger conclusion than the experiment supported. [33]

**Other human results depended on participants.** Pepino’s 2015 review described increased GLP-1 after diet soda or a small sucralose preload before glucose in some healthy children and young overweight or obese adults, but not in participants with type 2 diabetes. It also described altered glucose and insulin responses in a selected group of people with obesity who were not regular sweetener users. Studies in healthy lean adults often found no corresponding changes. [1]

**An increased hormone can be helpful.** GLP-1 normally helps regulate blood sugar and can increase fullness, so a higher GLP-1 result is not inherently an adverse finding. The same review discussed circumstances in which sucralose enhanced GLP-1 while glucose outcomes differed across studies. Determining health significance requires following the whole response, rather than labeling every increase in insulin or a gut hormone as damage. [1]

**Animal sweet receptors differ from human receptors.** Aspartame, for example, does not activate mouse sweet sensing in the same way as human sweet sensing. Some rodent experiments found increased intestinal glucose transport after sucralose, saccharin, or Ace-K but not aspartame. That pattern can reflect species-specific receptor recognition rather than a reliable ranking of their effects in people. [31]

**The 2017 rat experiment challenged simplicity.** Saltiel and colleagues preserved an isolated intestinal segment with controlled circulation. Sweeteners placed inside the intestine produced different responses from sweeteners supplied through its blood vessels: Ace-K stimulated GLP-1 from the intestinal side, while sucralose and stevioside did so through the blood supply at high experimental concentrations. Sucralose at lower blood-side concentrations did not produce the same response. [34]

**Receptor inhibition did not settle the response.** In that preparation, blocking the sweet receptor did not significantly reduce glucose-stimulated GLP-1 or GIP release. The authors therefore concluded that sweet-receptor activation was not the principal driver of those responses in their model. Their findings favored considering other glucose-sensing mechanisms, particularly transport through SGLT1, rather than treating sweetness detection as the sole explanation. [34]

**Transport and detection may interact.** The competing explanations are not necessarily that the gut has either sweet receptors or glucose transporters, with only one being real. Both exist, and their relative contributions can differ by cell type, nutrient exposure, and experimental conditions. The unresolved mechanistic issue was how much receptor stimulation independently changes glucose handling in a living person consuming ordinary foods. [31]

**Longer human exposure adds perspective.** A randomized, double-blind trial summarized in the 2011 review gave 128 people with type 2 diabetes either 667 mg sucralose daily or placebo for 13 weeks. It found no effect on fasting glucose, HbA1c—a measure reflecting longer-term blood-glucose exposure—or fasting C-peptide, an indicator of insulin secretion. This did not measure every possible receptor event, but it tested whether repeated exposure changed clinically relevant glucose-control markers. [30]

**The practical judgment is context-dependent.** A receptor-mediated adverse effect would become more convincing if ordinary food exposures consistently changed glucose absorption or hormone coordination and those changes predicted unfavorable human outcomes. Findings explained by glucose transport, limited to particular exposure routes, or accompanied by neutral glucose control support a narrower interpretation. The evidence therefore favors specifying the sweetener, dose, meal context, and population rather than treating all sweeteners as one endocrine exposure.

**Takeaway: receptor activation is a plausible route to physiological effects, but the pre-cutoff findings support context-dependent signaling rather than a universal harmful response.**

**Additional documented evidence**

By 28 February 2019, **Taste-receptor interaction in the gut** was best supported as functional sensing, not demonstrated harm. In an August 2011 human study, lactisole blockade during intragastric glucose infusion in 16 healthy subjects reduced GLP-1 (P=.026) and PYY (P=.034) secretion [290]. These gut hormones can enter blood or signal through mainly vagal nerves, potentially affecting digestion, metabolic regulation and eating—not proving disease [290].

These gut hormone-releasing (enteroendocrine) cells sense sweet compounds through α-gustducin-linked receptors including T1R3. α-gustducin was more prominent in proximal gut than ileum or colon; T1R3 and α-gustducin co-localized with GLP-1 in surface and crypt epithelial cells of human duodenum and colon. The localization sample was only two healthy, normal-weight subjects after 10 h fasting [290]. Cell, animal and mouse-knockout findings linked this signaling to GLP-1, insulin and glucose regulation; artificial sweeteners stimulated GLP-1 in gut-cell lines and antagonists blocked it [290].

Evidence was therefore mechanistic plus limited human tissue/functional evidence: data were sparse, and the experiment used glucose and receptor blockade, not every non-nutritive sweetener, dose, nutrient context or population [290]. A pre-2019 review lists gut-signaling work from 2007 and 2012 [289]. It reports no increase, or reduced energy intake, in short-term human low-energy-sweetener capsule studies; a contrary animal exception involved saccharin in moistened or fat-enriched diets, possibly via palatability and unlike usual human sugar replacement [289]. This is a plausible upstream pathway, not established human harm or proof of microbiota or sweetness-energy disruption. ### 2. Interference With or Disruption of Gut Microbiota

**The strongest causal evidence came from mice.** Suez and colleagues’ 2014 experiments linked sweetener exposure to altered intestinal microbes and impaired glucose tolerance, and showed that the metabolic effect could be transferred to germ-free mice. Their small human saccharin intervention found increased glycemic responses in **4 of 7 volunteers**, with no corresponding response in the other three. [35][36]

**Microbiota means the intestinal microbial community.** The term refers to organisms living in the gut, particularly bacteria in the studies considered here. These organisms process dietary substances and produce chemicals that can interact with the host’s digestion, immune signaling, and metabolism. “Microbiome” is often used more broadly for the organisms together with their genes and functional capacities.

**The proposed pathway has several links.** A sweetener might change which microbes thrive, reduce others, or alter what the community does. Those changes could modify microbial products, intestinal conditions, or inflammatory signaling. If the resulting signals change how the body responds to glucose or insulin, the microbial effect could contribute to metabolic problems. [31][36]

**Composition and function are different outcomes.** A change in the proportion of a bacterial group describes community composition. A change in the substances microbes produce, or in the metabolic pathways represented in their genes, describes function. The harmful-effect hypothesis requires more than finding a different bacterial profile: the change must connect to an unfavorable consequence for the person.

**The principal studies give a mixed picture.**

| Study or Evidence | Exposure or Population | Main Observation | Meaning for the Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suez and colleagues, 2014; mice | Commercial saccharin, sucralose, or aspartame; 11 weeks | Greater glucose excursions; microbiota changes | Supports a possible exposure-to-metabolism pathway |
| Suez antibiotic and transfer experiments | Antibiotic-treated or germ-free mice | Antibiotics removed effects; transferred microbiota reproduced them | Supports microbial causation within the model |
| Human saccharin intervention | 7 volunteers; 5 mg/kg/day for 1 week | 4 responders; 3 nonresponders | Suggests individual susceptibility |
| Human observational analysis | 381 healthy, non-diabetic participants | Sweetener use associated with metabolic and microbial measures | Association does not establish exposure as the cause |
| Human cross-sectional study | 31 adults; food records and stool sampling | No abundance-profile association; diversity differed | Shows that microbial findings are not uniform |
| Earlier sucralose-formulation study | Male rats; 12 weeks plus recovery | Reduced bacterial counts and altered intestinal proteins | Formulation-specific animal evidence |

**Transfer experiments strengthen the causal argument.** In the mouse work, treating animals with antibiotics abolished the observed glucose-intolerance effect. Transferring intestinal microbes from exposed animals to germ-free recipients could reproduce it, even though the recipients had not themselves consumed the sweetener. This moves the result beyond “two things changed together”: it implicates the microbial community as a mediator under those experimental conditions. [35]

**The demonstrated outcome was glucose intolerance.** This means an altered ability to handle a glucose challenge, commonly observed as higher blood-glucose excursions after the test. It is relevant to metabolic health, but it is not identical to demonstrating that an exposure causes type 2 diabetes over years. Keeping the tested outcome distinct from the disease of concern prevents an experimental finding from acquiring an unsupported clinical meaning.

**Saccharin received the deepest experimental follow-up.** The original mouse study tested saccharin, sucralose, and aspartame formulations, but the mechanistic work concentrated particularly on saccharin because it produced a strong glycemic response in that model. The most detailed transfer findings and the small human intervention therefore provide more direct evidence about saccharin than about every high-intensity sweetener in the comparison table. [1]

**The human dose was clearly defined.** The seven volunteers consumed saccharin at 5 mg/kg body weight per day for one week, the upper daily level used in that experiment. Four developed increased glycemic responses and three did not. The result is evidence of heterogeneity within that small group; four of seven should not be converted into a population-wide probability that a saccharin consumer will be harmed. [36]

**Human-to-mouse transfer added another link.** Microbiota from human responders transferred the glycemic phenotype to germ-free mice. This strengthened the argument that something about the responders’ microbial communities was functionally important. It still left separate questions about the initiating effect in humans, how representative those volunteers were, and whether the response persisted during everyday consumption. [36]

**The human comparison lacked randomization to exposure.** Pepino noted that the intervention did not include a separate saccharin-unexposed control group undergoing the same daily testing. Participants repeatedly consumed 75 g glucose for tolerance tests, so the design did not isolate saccharin from every other time-dependent change. The specific conclusion is that the human findings supported the proposed mechanism, while the experiment did not by itself assign all of the observed change uniquely to saccharin. [1]

**The larger human analysis was observational.** Among 381 healthy, non-diabetic participants, sweetener consumption was associated with measures including body mass index, blood pressure, HbA1c, fasting glucose, and aspects of microbial composition. These findings widened the phenomenon of interest beyond seven volunteers, but could reflect differences in diet, baseline metabolic health, or reasons for choosing sweeteners. A larger observational sample does not automatically resolve causal direction. [36]

**A smaller study gave a different pattern.** In a 31-adult cross-sectional study summarized by Ruiz-Ojeda and colleagues, participants completed four-day food records and supplied stool samples on the fifth day. Bacterial abundance profiles were not associated with sweetener consumption, especially aspartame, although overall diversity differed between consumers and nonconsumers. Thus, the result was not simply “nothing changed”; it showed that the answer depended on which microbial feature was measured. [16]

**Diversity is not a diagnosis.** A community with a different number or distribution of microbial types is not automatically healthier or less healthy. For this question, the decisive connection is whether a reproducible microbial change predicts an unfavorable physiological outcome. That is why a stool-profile difference and a transferred glucose-intolerance phenotype provide different kinds of evidence.

**Earlier rat work raised formulation questions.** A 2008 study exposed male rats to a sucralose-containing commercial preparation for 12 weeks, followed by a recovery period. The gastrointestinal review reported reduced counts of several bacterial groups, including an approximately 50% reduction in total anaerobic bacteria at the lowest tested dose, together with changes in fecal pH and intestinal transport or drug-processing proteins. [7]

**The tested material matters to attribution.** That preparation included carbohydrate carriers as well as sucralose, so it did not isolate pure sucralose from the complete formulation. The appropriate conclusion attaches the result to the administered mixture and conditions. This is the same ingredient-versus-brand distinction that matters in the comparison table, now applied to a proposed health mechanism. [7]

**Aspartame presents a mechanistic puzzle.** An eight-week rat study summarized by Pepino reported altered microbiota, higher fasting glucose, and impaired insulin tolerance after aspartame exposure. Yet aspartame is broken down before reaching the colon. A direct effect of intact aspartame on colonic bacteria therefore cannot simply be assumed; indirect changes involving its breakdown products, host physiology, or the intestinal environment would have to be considered. [1]

**Natural extracts also interact with microbes.** Steviol glycosides reach the large intestine and are broken down by intestinal bacteria to steviol, which is then absorbed, metabolized, and excreted. That is a documented part of their normal metabolic fate. It demonstrates microbial involvement without, on its own, demonstrating harmful disruption of the community. [22]

**Microbes can change exposure without being disrupted.** Cyclamate provides another distinction: some gut bacteria convert it to cyclohexylamine. That is microbial transformation of an ingredient, rather than proof that the ingredient has damaged the microbial community. The two processes—microbes changing a sweetener and a sweetener changing microbes—can affect health questions differently. [4]

**Microbial products offer plausible downstream routes.** Suez and colleagues described changes in microbial pathways related to sugar processing and the production of short-chain fatty acids. These small molecules can provide energy and act as signals to the host, making them candidates for linking microbes to glucose or fat metabolism. Their presence alone is not evidence of harm, however, because their effects depend on the particular compounds, amounts, and host context. [36]

**Inflammation was a proposed extension.** Earlier reviews suggested that changes in intestinal microbes could influence inflammatory processes associated with insulin resistance and fat storage. That provides a biologically plausible bridge from a microbial change to metabolic disease. It should not be read as a finding that every sweetener exposure produced inflammation, or that inflammation explained all observed glucose responses. [31]

**Polyol fermentation is an important counterexample.** Lactitol, isomalt, and related bulk sweeteners can alter microbes because an unabsorbed portion reaches the large intestine as a fermentable substrate. Reviews describe increases in bifidobacteria and other prebiotic-type effects for some of these ingredients. At the same time, fermentation and water movement can cause digestive symptoms, showing that microbial effects and practical tolerability need not move in the same direction. [14]

**The systematic evidence resisted a class conclusion.** The 2016 gastrointestinal review screened 617 papers and included 26 original studies across its questions. It described microbiome findings as conflicting and heterogeneous in the populations, sweeteners, and doses studied, with limited human evidence. This supports discussing specific ingredients and exposures rather than using “microbiota disruption” as a single established property of the whole category. [7]

**The practical judgment turns on mediation.** The strongest adverse interpretation would involve a realistic exposure repeatedly producing a defined microbial change that, in turn, predicts poorer human glucose control. A finding limited to particular responder communities, formulations, or doses would support a more targeted interpretation. The pre-cutoff evidence made microbiota a serious mechanistic candidate without establishing a uniform human response.

**Takeaway: microbiota-mediated glucose effects were convincingly demonstrated in particular animal experiments, while the human evidence pointed toward ingredient- and person-specific susceptibility rather than universal disruption.**

### 3. Interference With the Sweetness–Energy Learning Response

**The central hypothesis concerns prediction.** The brain and body learn that sweet taste often predicts incoming energy. Repeatedly experiencing sweetness with little or no energy might weaken that learned connection, potentially changing later appetite, digestive preparation, or glucose handling. Controlled rat experiments supported parts of this explanation, but human feeding and conditioning studies did not show that non-nutritive sweetness inevitably causes overeating. [31][37]

**Learning can prepare the body before absorption.** Food-related sights, smells, tastes, and textures can become signals of what is about to arrive. Some early digestive and hormonal responses are called “cephalic-phase” responses: they begin through sensory and brain-mediated preparation rather than only after nutrients enter the bloodstream. The sweetness–energy hypothesis proposes that reliable sensory predictions help coordinate these responses. [1]

**Noncaloric sweetness could make the cue unreliable.** If sweetness sometimes predicts substantial energy and sometimes predicts very little, the sweet sensation alone becomes less informative. In the proposed model, a later sugary food could then elicit weaker preparation or less accurate compensation for its calories. Possible consequences include eating more afterward, a smaller thermic response to the meal, or altered regulation of blood glucose. [31]

**Thermic response means processing expenditure.** The body expends energy digesting and handling food, and this contributes to a post-meal rise in energy expenditure. Some rat experiments reported a weaker response after experience with noncaloric sweetness. That observation is relevant to the proposed pathway, but it is distinct from measuring appetite, total food intake, or long-term body weight. [1]

**This differs from immediate gut detection.** The receptor hypothesis can operate during one exposure because a molecule binds a detector. The learning hypothesis depends on previous experience: what sweetness has predicted over repeated meals. A person or animal could therefore show a learned change even when an acute sweetener dose has little direct effect on intestinal hormones.

**The controlled evidence spans different outcomes.**

| Experiment or Evidence | Exposure | Main Finding | What It Tests |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rat flavor-conditioning experiment | 32 rats; 0.3% saccharin or water for 14 days, then flavor training | Greater intake of a glucose-paired flavor after saccharin exposure | Whether sweetness remains a reliable calorie cue |
| Rat feeding experiment | 60 rats; sweetened yogurt and three high-fat diets for 28 days | Greater intake and weight gain only with a particular sweet maintenance diet | Whether the learning effect depends on dietary context |
| Human conditioning experiment | 10 exposures to each drink over 20 days | No behavioral or brain-imaging reward change | Whether repeated exposure changes learned reward value |
| Aspartame studies summarized by AHA/ADA | 12 weight-control studies; approximately 24-hour compensation | Average compensation of 32% | Whether calories removed are fully eaten back |
| Human crossover comparison | 14 men; two 10-day periods | Subgroup mean compensation about 42%, highly variable | Individual short-term adjustment to substitution |

**The first rat experiment isolated sweet cues.** Thirty-two adult male rats received either 0.3% saccharin or water for 14 days, with 16 animals in each exposure group. They then underwent 20 days of flavor training involving glucose or polycose, a carbohydrate preparation used as a comparator. In a subsequent four-hour test, saccharin-exposed rats consumed more of the flavor previously paired with glucose. [38]

**The selective result strengthened the interpretation.** The corresponding difference did not appear for the polycose-paired flavor. That matters because it points toward altered use of sweetness as a calorie signal, rather than a general increase in responsiveness to every flavor or every carbohydrate. The experiment supports a specific learning process more directly than an uncontrolled observation that sweetener-exposed animals simply ate more.

**The second experiment tested a boundary.** Sixty rats received one of three high-fat maintenance diets: plain, supplemented with 20% glucose, or supplemented with 20% polycose. For 28 days they also received yogurt sweetened with either 20% glucose or 0.3% saccharin. Greater calorie intake and weight gain with saccharin-sweetened yogurt occurred specifically when the maintenance diet itself tasted sweet because it contained glucose. [38]

**That dependence is part of the finding.** The result did not show that saccharin caused the same weight response under every maintenance diet. Instead, it fit the idea that weakening sweetness as a signal becomes consequential when sweetness is also needed to help regulate intake of another energy-rich food. The relevant causal unit was the sweetener exposure together with the surrounding diet and learning history.

**Taste-bypass experiments separated possible routes.** Pepino’s review described rat work in which prior saccharin exposure altered glucose regulation when a glucose load was tasted and swallowed, but not when glucose was delivered directly into the stomach. That pattern supports involvement of an oral cue or learned anticipatory response. It is less consistent with an explanation based solely on what the glucose does after arriving in the gut. [1]

**Animal preparation is not identical to human preparation.** Sweet liquids reliably elicit certain early responses in rats, whereas such responses are less consistent in humans given sweet liquids alone. Human responses to chewing and tasting foods also differ from responses to isolated sweet solutions. Consequently, the existence of associative learning in both species does not guarantee that the same laboratory sweet-liquid protocol will produce the same metabolic outcome. [1]

**Sweet liking is a separate question.** Repeated exposure can affect preference, but preferring sweetness does not establish impaired calorie prediction. AHA and ADA emphasized that human sweet preferences also reflect food culture, expectations, and central reward processes. These influences can affect product choice without demonstrating that a physiological learning system has been damaged. [2]

**Brain images show responses, not overeating.** Human imaging studies found that sucrose and sucralose activated overlapping taste pathways, while sucrose sometimes produced stronger responses or different connections with reward-related brain regions. Other studies associated habitual diet-soda use with altered responses to sweet tastes. Those results identify differences in sensory or reward processing; they do not by themselves show increased food intake or weight gain. [39]

**Habitual-use comparisons have competing explanations.** Someone who regularly drinks diet soda may differ from a nonconsumer in dietary goals, previous weight change, preferred tastes, or other habits. Observed brain-response differences could precede sweetener use, follow it, or share another cause. This is the learning-mechanism version of the reverse-causation issue that also affects observational metabolic studies.

**The direct human conditioning result was null.** A repeated-exposure experiment gave participants sucrose-sweetened and non-nutritively sweetened drinks 10 times each over 20 days. It found no change in either behavioral reward value or the corresponding functional brain-imaging measure. This directly tested a learning-related outcome, although it covered a much shorter period than a person’s lifetime of food experience. [39]

**Energy compensation determines practical consequences.** When sugar calories are removed, a person may eat some of those calories back later; this is called compensation. Incomplete compensation means that later intake replaces only part of the removed energy, leaving a net reduction. Complete compensation would erase the reduction, while overcompensation would exceed it.

**The human average was not full replacement.** AHA and ADA summarized a meta-analysis of 12 aspartame weight-control studies in which average compensation over approximately 24 hours was 32%. That left 68% of the original energy deficit uncompensated. In the four beverage-only studies, compensation averaged 15%, again inconsistent with an automatic rule that sweetness without calories forces people to replace all the missing energy. [2]

**Compensation is not a mechanism diagnosis.** Eating somewhat more after a lower-energy product can reflect an ordinary adjustment to receiving less food energy. That response does not, by itself, show that sweetness–energy learning has been disrupted. The harmful-learning claim requires evidence that the sensory mismatch causes an unfavorable response beyond what would follow simply from the lower energy intake.

**A controlled feeding comparison illustrates this distinction.**

| Sweetened Test Condition | Energy in the Test Food | Subsequent Meal Intake | Combined Intake Pattern |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Sucrose | 493 kcal | Similar to the other conditions | Higher |
| Aspartame | 290 kcal | Similar to the other conditions | Lower |
| Stevia | 290 kcal | Similar to the other conditions | Lower |

**Lower later intake was not required.** In this comparison summarized by AHA and ADA, the aspartame and stevia conditions began with 203 fewer kcal than the sucrose condition, calculated as 493 − 290 kcal. Participants did not need to eat less at the subsequent meal to retain a lower combined intake; eating a similar amount was enough. The study therefore contradicts inevitable immediate compensation without proving a long-term effect on body weight. [2]

**Individual responses were nevertheless variable.** Mattes and Popkin described a crossover study in 14 men comparing sucrose-containing products with products containing aspartame and Ace-K over two 10-day periods. In the 10-man subgroup receiving the sucrose condition first, mean compensation was approximately 42%, with responses ranging from reverse compensation to approximately 90%. A group average therefore should not be treated as every individual’s response. [37]

**Appetite ratings do not equal energy intake.** A person may report greater hunger after a noncaloric drink without subsequently consuming enough additional food to offset the calories removed from a sugary drink. Conversely, reporting similar fullness does not establish identical daily intake. Studies measuring sensations, actual food consumption, and weight address related but distinct outcomes.

**Food context can change apparent effects.** Mattes and Popkin concluded that adding non-nutritive sweetness to products without energy might increase appetite in some circumstances, but this was not generally observed when sweeteners were consumed with other energy sources. This contrast is relevant to the hypothesis: a diet drink alone, a diet drink with lunch, and a sweetener-containing yogurt do not present identical sensory and nutritional information. [37]

**The replacement comparator remains decisive.** Replacing sugar with a non-nutritive sweetener removes sugar energy from the product; replacing water with a diet drink does not create that same energy reduction. A claim about the net dietary effect must identify which comparison was tested. Evidence that a sweetener-containing drink helps reduce intake relative to a sugary drink does not automatically establish an advantage over water, and the reverse comparison does not test whether sugar replacement can save calories.

**Longer trials did not support inevitable harm.** The 2018 Ibero-American Consensus described most higher-quality controlled trials as showing neutral effects on HbA1c, insulin, and fasting or post-meal glucose. It interpreted some longer sugar-replacement studies as showing modest weight benefits, while acknowledging that Azad and colleagues’ 2017 meta-analysis did not establish such benefits. The disagreement concerned the strength of benefit more than proof of the proposed learning-related injury. [40]

**Neutral outcomes still constrain a hypothesis.** A learning process could occur without dominating the total effect of a dietary substitution, because removing sugar changes energy intake at the same time. Nevertheless, repeated neutral or favorable controlled outcomes make a broad claim of inevitable overeating difficult to sustain. A proposed mechanism must be weighed against the net outcomes it is intended to explain.

**The strongest test concerns durable behavior.** The adverse-learning interpretation would strengthen if matched human exposures changed calorie prediction and those changes reliably led to greater intake, impaired glucose handling, or weight gain. It would narrow if responses remained confined to particular diets or learning histories, or if repeated-exposure and replacement studies continued to show preserved calorie control. This keeps the conclusion tied to observable consequences rather than the appealing metaphor that the body becomes “confused.”

**Takeaway: weakening the sweetness–energy association is a coherent, rodent-supported hypothesis, but pre-cutoff human evidence did not establish it as a general cause of overeating or metabolic disease.**

**The evidence uses several measurement bases.** Sweetness values describe approximate ingredient-level sensory comparisons; energy figures distinguish reference values from legal labeling factors, and the monk-fruit sugar-energy figure is a labeled calculation. Mechanistic evidence includes cells, animal experiments, acute human responses, and small interventions, which answer different questions from long-term clinical outcomes. All conclusions here concern evidence and product information available by February 28, 2019.

**The overall health judgment is specific.** The three mechanisms explain how a low-calorie sweetener could have biological effects without supplying much energy; they do not establish that all such effects are adverse. For a researcher or consumer, the most defensible interpretation identifies the exact ingredient, formulation, exposure, food replacement, and population—and separates an observed mechanism from a demonstrated human health outcome.
